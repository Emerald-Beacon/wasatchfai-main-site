Hammer Toe: Effective Treatment Options

When a toe bends and locks at the middle joint, it rarely straightens out on its own — here's what causes hammer toe and the treatments that actually help.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Hammer toe is a deformity in which a toe — most often the second, third, or fourth — bends downward in the middle, creating a hammer-like appearance. It develops gradually as the muscles and tendons around the toe joint fall out of balance and lock the toe in a bent position. Caught early, hammer toe usually responds well to simple, nonsurgical care; left alone, it tends to grow more rigid and painful over time.

If one of your toes has started curling downward and rubbing uncomfortably against the top of your shoe, you are dealing with one of the most common forefoot conditions we see in Farmington and South Ogden. Below, our podiatrists explain what causes hammer toe, how to spot it early, and the full range of treatment options available.

What is hammer toe?

Hammer toe occurs when the muscles and tendons that normally keep a toe straight become imbalanced, causing the middle joint of the toe to bend and eventually stiffen in that position. It typically affects the second through fourth toes and can occur on one toe or several. In its early stages the toe is still flexible and can be gently straightened by hand; over time, without treatment, the joint can become rigid and fixed.

Hammer toe often develops alongside other forefoot conditions. Tight, imbalanced muscles that pull on the lesser toes are frequently related to a bunion at the base of the big toe, since a bunion changes how weight and pressure are distributed across the front of the foot.

What causes hammer toe?

Hammer toe results from an imbalance in the muscles and tendons that hold the toe straight, which allows the middle joint to buckle. Common contributors include:

  • Tight, narrow, or pointed shoes that crowd the toes, including high heels
  • Structural foot issues such as flat feet or bunions that alter toe mechanics
  • A prior toe injury
  • Inherited foot structure and muscle imbalance
  • Medical conditions such as arthritis or diabetes, which can affect nerves and joints in the foot

Because footwear plays such a large role, people who regularly wear narrow or high-heeled shoes are at particularly high risk.

What are the symptoms of hammer toe?

Hammer toe usually starts subtly and worsens gradually. Watch for:

  • Mild aching or joint pain in the affected toe that increases over time
  • A visible bend or curl at the toe's middle joint
  • Corns or calluses on top of or at the tip of the toe from rubbing against shoes
  • Redness or swelling around the joint
  • Difficulty straightening the toe, or in severe cases, complete inability to straighten it

How is hammer toe diagnosed?

A podiatrist diagnoses hammer toe with a physical examination, checking the toe's alignment and how much flexibility remains in the joint. The skin is also examined for corns, calluses, or irritation. Diagnostic imaging, such as an X-ray, may be used to evaluate the joint and rule out other conditions like arthritis.

How is hammer toe treated?

Early intervention makes the biggest difference — the sooner treatment starts, the more likely the toe can be managed without surgery.

Nonsurgical treatment

  • Roomier footwear with a wide, soft toe box that does not crowd the toes
  • Custom orthotics or supportive insoles to correct underlying mechanics — see our custom orthotics options
  • Stretching and strengthening exercises to rebalance the muscles controlling the toe
  • Physical therapy to improve flexibility and joint mobility
  • Padding or non-medicated cushions to relieve pressure from corns and calluses

Surgery

When a hammer toe becomes rigid or does not respond to nonsurgical treatment, surgery may be recommended. The right procedure depends on the severity of the deformity and how much it affects daily function, and can range from soft-tissue releases to realigning the joint itself. Because hammer toe and hallux rigidus can share overlapping mechanical causes, our physicians evaluate the whole forefoot, not just the affected toe, when planning treatment.

Recovery

After surgery, recovery generally involves rest, protecting the foot, managing swelling, and a course of physical therapy. Most patients see meaningful pain reduction and improved toe alignment within a matter of weeks, though full recovery timelines vary by procedure and individual healing.

When should I see a podiatrist?

See a podiatrist as soon as you notice a toe beginning to bend or stiffen, rather than waiting for it to become painful or rigid. Early treatment is far more likely to succeed with simple, nonsurgical measures. Seek care sooner if you develop persistent pain, an open sore, or signs of infection, especially if you have diabetes or reduced circulation. Common Questions

Hammer toe FAQs

Can hammer toe be corrected without surgery?

Flexible hammer toes, where the joint can still be moved by hand, often respond well to nonsurgical care such as roomier shoes, custom orthotics, and stretching exercises. Rigid hammer toes that no longer bend are less likely to improve without a surgical procedure.

What shoes should I wear if I have hammer toe?

Choose shoes with a wide, deep, soft toe box that does not crowd or press on the toes, and avoid high heels or narrow, pointed styles. Leaving room between your longest toe and the end of the shoe helps reduce pressure on the affected joint.

Will hammer toe get worse if left untreated?

Yes, in most cases. Hammer toe tends to progress gradually, and a toe that starts out flexible can become rigid and fixed over time without treatment, making nonsurgical options less effective the longer it is ignored.

Is hammer toe surgery painful?

Hammer toe surgery is performed with anesthesia so you should not feel pain during the procedure. Afterward, some discomfort and swelling are normal during recovery and are managed with rest, foot protection, and guidance from your podiatrist.

Can hammer toe be prevented?

Wearing properly fitting shoes with adequate room in the toe box is the most effective way to reduce your risk. Addressing related issues such as bunions or flat feet early with supportive footwear or orthotics can also help prevent hammer toe from developing.

When should I see a podiatrist about a bent toe?

See a podiatrist as soon as you notice a toe bending or stiffening, even if it is not yet painful, since early treatment is more successful. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
