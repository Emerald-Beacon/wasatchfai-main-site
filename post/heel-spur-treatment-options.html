Heel Spur Treatment Options

A heel spur is a small bony growth on the heel bone — here's what causes it, and the full range of treatments that can bring lasting relief.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated October 6, 2026

If you've been told you have a heel spur, the most useful thing to understand is that treatment rarely targets the bony growth itself — it targets the irritated tissue around it. Care almost always starts with conservative steps that offload and calm the heel, moves on to in-office options like shock wave therapy if pain lingers, and only reaches surgery in the uncommon case that months of consistent treatment haven't helped. Removing the spur is seldom the goal.

That distinction catches a lot of people off guard. A spur sounds like something that needs to come out, but a bony heel spur on an X-ray and the pain you actually feel are two separate questions. This guide walks through how a spur is diagnosed, what each treatment option does, and what surgery and recovery really involve — so you can make sense of the plan your podiatrist in Farmington or South Ogden lays out.

What a heel spur is — and isn't

A heel spur is a calcium deposit that forms a small bony protrusion on the underside of the heel bone, usually where the plantar fascia attaches. It builds up slowly, over months or years, as the body responds to repeated tension at that spot. The fascia is a thick band of tissue running from the heel to the toes, and when it is chronically strained the pull at its anchor point can prompt the body to lay down extra bone.

Here is the part that changes how a spur should be treated: the spur is often not the pain generator. The sharp, stabbing heel pain most people feel comes from inflamed soft tissue, not the bone itself. Plenty of people have a visible spur and no symptoms at all, and plenty of people with severe heel pain have no spur on imaging. If you're trying to sort out whether your pain is coming from the fascia, a spur, or both, we unpack that question in detail in plantar fasciitis vs. heel spurs. This article picks up after that: you have a spur, so now what?

How a heel spur is diagnosed

A spur is confirmed on imaging, but the diagnosis that actually matters is figuring out what's driving your pain. That takes more than a picture of the bone.

  • History and hands-on exam. Your podiatrist will ask when the pain is worst — classic heel pain flares with the first steps in the morning or after sitting — and press along the heel and arch to locate the tender spot. Where it hurts tells us a great deal.
  • X-ray. This is what reveals the spur, and it also rules out other bony problems like a stress fracture. But a spur seen on film is only meaningful if it lines up with your symptoms.
  • Ultrasound or MRI, when needed. Soft-tissue imaging can show thickening or inflammation in the fascia, which often matters more than the spur for planning treatment.

Because a spur on X-ray doesn't automatically explain your pain, a careful evaluation keeps you from chasing the wrong target — and keeps you from assuming surgery to remove the bone is the answer when it usually isn't.

Treating the pain, not the spur

Most heel-spur pain settles with conservative care, so that's where treatment starts and where most people stay. The goal at this stage is to reduce the load on the heel and give the inflamed tissue a chance to recover.

Conservative care

  • Supportive footwear with a firm heel counter and real arch support, which keeps the heel from taking the full force of each step
  • Heel cups, cushioning, or custom orthotics to redistribute pressure away from the sore spot — often the single most effective change for a mechanical problem like this
  • Calf and plantar fascia stretching, especially before your first steps in the morning, to ease the tension pulling at the heel
  • Ice for short intervals to quiet inflammation after activity
  • Activity modification, trading high-impact time for lower-impact movement while the heel calms down rather than stopping altogether
  • Anti-inflammatory medication such as ibuprofen for pain and swelling, when appropriate for you
  • Physical therapy to address tight calves, weak foot muscles, or gait habits feeding the problem

When conservative care isn't enough

If weeks of consistent home care haven't helped, in-office options come next. Extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) is a noninvasive treatment that delivers focused sound-wave energy to the irritated tissue around the heel. The idea is to stimulate the body's own healing response in a stubborn area, and for many people it's a way to make real progress without an operation. Corticosteroid injections are another step for pain that won't settle; we walk through what those feel like and how long relief tends to last in our guide to heel injections. These are tools to break a pain cycle, not quick fixes, and they work best paired with the footwear and stretching changes above.

What heel spur surgery involves

Surgery is uncommon and sits at the end of the line, generally considered only when symptoms haven't improved after roughly six to twelve months of genuine, consistent conservative treatment. When it is warranted, the operation usually focuses on releasing tension in the plantar fascia rather than simply shaving off the spur; in some cases the surgeon removes the bony growth as well, but relieving the underlying pull is typically what resolves the pain.

Recovery varies from person to person and depends on the specific procedure and your overall health, so your surgeon is the one who will give you a timeline and tell you when it's safe to return to full activity. In general, you can expect a period of protected weight-bearing and a gradual, guided return to standing and walking. What matters is that no one should promise you a fixed number of weeks or a guaranteed outcome — healing is individual, and progress is confirmed in person at your follow-up visits.

When to see a podiatrist

See a podiatrist if heel pain lasts more than a week or two despite home care, keeps you from your normal activities, causes a limp, or returns every morning. Because spur-related pain is usually tied to chronic tension in the fascia, treating it early tends to keep the problem from digging in. And don't wait on red flags: sudden severe pain after an injury, an inability to put weight on the foot, or numbness all deserve prompt evaluation rather than a wait-and-see approach. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices, and we also treat ongoing heel pain from every cause, not spurs alone.

Common Questions

Heel spur FAQs

Will a heel spur go away on its own?

The bony growth itself generally does not dissolve once it has formed, but that doesn't mean you're stuck with the pain. Because most heel-spur pain comes from inflamed tissue around the spur rather than the bone, calming that inflammation usually relieves symptoms even though the spur stays visible on X-ray.

Do I need the spur removed to get rid of the pain?

Usually not. Treatment targets the irritated soft tissue, not the spur, so most people get relief from supportive footwear, orthotics, stretching, and in-office options like shock wave therapy. Surgery is uncommon, and even when it's done it typically focuses on releasing tension in the plantar fascia rather than simply removing the bone.

How is a heel spur diagnosed?

A spur shows up on an X-ray, but the more important part of the diagnosis is a hands-on exam that pinpoints where your pain is coming from. Your podiatrist may add ultrasound or MRI to look at the plantar fascia, since a spur on film only matters if it lines up with your symptoms.

Does shock wave therapy work for heel spurs?

Extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) is a noninvasive treatment that delivers focused sound-wave energy to the irritated tissue around a heel spur to stimulate healing. It's often tried when conservative measures haven't been enough, and for many people it's a way to make progress without surgery.

What is recovery like after heel spur surgery?

Recovery depends on the specific procedure and your overall health, so your surgeon is the one who sets your timeline and clears you for full activity. In general it involves a period of protected weight-bearing followed by a gradual, guided return to standing and walking. No one should promise a fixed number of weeks — healing is individual and confirmed at your follow-up visits.

When should I see a podiatrist about a heel spur?

See a podiatrist if your heel pain lasts more than a week or two, limits your activities, causes a limp, or keeps returning each morning. Seek prompt care for sudden severe pain, an inability to bear weight, or numbness. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
