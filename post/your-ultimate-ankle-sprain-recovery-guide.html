Your Ultimate Ankle Sprain Recovery Guide

From the first 48 hours to a safe return to activity — a step-by-step guide to healing a sprained ankle and keeping it from happening again.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

An ankle sprain happens when the ligaments that hold the ankle joint together are stretched or torn, usually from a sudden twist, roll, or awkward landing. It is one of the most common injuries we see, and while the bones of the ankle are strong and resilient, the surrounding ligaments are far more vulnerable. The good news is that most sprains heal well with the right care — as long as you treat them properly from the start and don't rush back to full activity too soon.

Whether you rolled your ankle on the trail, came down wrong after a jump shot, or simply stepped off a curb the wrong way, this guide walks through what to do in the first 48 hours, how to safely work your way back to normal activity, how to lower your risk of a repeat injury, and when it's time to have a podiatrist take a look.

What happens when you sprain your ankle

Sprains are graded by how much ligament damage has occurred, and the grade determines how long recovery typically takes:

  • Mild (Grade 1): Slight stretching and microscopic tearing of the ligament fibers, with mild swelling and tenderness. Often heals in a few days to about two weeks.
  • Moderate (Grade 2): Partial tearing of the ligament, with moderate swelling, bruising, and some looseness in the joint. Recovery usually takes several weeks.
  • Severe (Grade 3): A complete tear of the ligament, with significant swelling, bruising, and instability. This level can take a couple of months or longer to fully heal.

Because the severity isn't always obvious from symptoms alone, a proper evaluation helps confirm the grade of injury and rule out a fracture — the two can feel very similar at first.

Acute care: the first 48 hours

The standard first step for a fresh ankle sprain is the RICE method:

  • Rest the ankle and avoid putting weight on it until pain allows otherwise.
  • Ice for 15–20 minutes every couple of hours to control swelling and pain.
  • Compression with an elastic wrap to limit swelling — snug, but not so tight that it cuts off circulation or causes numbness or tingling.
  • Elevation of the foot above heart level whenever possible, especially in the first day or two.

It's tempting to keep the ankle completely still until it feels better, but total immobilization for too long can actually slow recovery. Once the acute swelling has started to settle, gentle, pain-free range-of-motion movement helps maintain flexibility and circulation without stressing the healing ligament.

Returning to your normal routine

Recovery from an ankle sprain is progressive — rushing any stage raises the risk of re-injury and can contribute to lasting instability. A typical progression includes:

  • Range-of-motion exercises to restore normal ankle movement as pain allows
  • Strengthening exercises, often with resistance bands, to rebuild the muscles that support the joint
  • Balance and proprioception training, such as single-leg standing drills, to retrain the ankle's sense of position and reduce the chance of it giving way again
  • Gradual return to activity, working back up to running, cutting, or sport-specific movement only once strength and balance have been rebuilt

How long each stage takes depends on the grade of the sprain and how your body responds — mild sprains may progress in days, while more severe ones need weeks of guided rehab before a full return to sport or heavy activity is appropriate.

Preventing future ankle sprains

Once you've sprained an ankle, you're more likely to sprain it again, which makes prevention worth the effort. Helpful measures include:

  • Ongoing ankle strengthening and balance exercises, even after symptoms resolve
  • Supportive, well-fitting footwear appropriate for your activity
  • Paying attention to uneven ground, poor lighting, and other environmental hazards
  • Using an ankle brace during higher-risk activities, particularly in the early months after a sprain

If you notice your ankle repeatedly "giving way" or feeling unstable, you may be dealing with lateral ankle instability, a related condition that develops after one or more prior sprains and often benefits from a more structured treatment plan.

When to see a podiatrist

Many sprains can be managed at home, but some warrant a professional evaluation. Reach out to a podiatrist if you experience:

  • Severe pain or an inability to bear any weight on the ankle
  • Inability to bear weight beyond 24–48 hours after the injury
  • Significant or worsening swelling and bruising
  • A sense that the ankle is unstable or repeatedly gives way
  • Symptoms that aren't steadily improving after a week or two of home care

Because an ankle fracture can present with very similar swelling and pain, it's worth getting evaluated any time you're unsure — imaging can quickly confirm whether you're dealing with a sprain, a break, or both. Common Questions

Ankle sprain recovery FAQs

How do I know if my ankle is sprained or broken?

Sprains and fractures can feel very similar — both cause pain, swelling, and bruising. Signs that point more toward a fracture include severe pain directly over the bone, an inability to bear any weight at all, or visible deformity. Because the two can be hard to tell apart without imaging, it's best to have a podiatrist evaluate any ankle injury that doesn't quickly improve.

When should I see a doctor for a sprained ankle?

See a podiatrist if you have severe pain, can't bear weight at all, still can't bear weight after 24–48 hours, have significant or worsening swelling, or feel like the ankle is unstable. You should also be evaluated if symptoms aren't steadily improving after a week or two of home care.

How long does it take to recover from a sprained ankle?

Recovery depends on the grade of the sprain. Mild sprains often improve within a few days to two weeks, moderate sprains typically take several weeks, and severe sprains with a complete ligament tear can take a couple of months or longer to fully heal and rehabilitate.

Can I still exercise with a sprained ankle?

Complete rest for too long isn't ideal — gentle, pain-free movement once the initial swelling has settled actually helps recovery. Low-impact activity and range-of-motion exercises are usually fine early on, but higher-impact exercise like running or hiking should wait until strength and balance have been rebuilt through a guided rehab progression.

Does wearing an ankle brace weaken the muscles?

Used appropriately, a brace supports the healing ligament and helps prevent re-injury without meaningfully weakening the surrounding muscles, especially when paired with an active strengthening and balance program. Bracing is most useful during early recovery and higher-risk activities, not as a long-term substitute for rebuilding strength.

Should I wear a compression wrap while sleeping?

Compression is most helpful during the day to control swelling; many people loosen or remove wraps at night so circulation isn't restricted during sleep. If you do keep a wrap on overnight, make sure it's snug rather than tight, and remove it if you notice numbness, tingling, or increased pain.
