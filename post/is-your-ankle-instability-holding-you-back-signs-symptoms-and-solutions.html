Is Your Ankle Instability Holding You Back?

Frequent rolling, giving way, or a nagging sense of weakness aren't things to just work around. Here's how to spot chronic ankle instability early — and what actually helps.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Chronic ankle instability is a persistent feeling that your ankle is unreliable — giving way, rolling, or wobbling during everyday activities — usually after one or more prior ankle sprains that didn't fully heal. Left unaddressed, it tends to get worse and increases your risk of repeat injury. The good news is that it responds well to a structured plan of strengthening, balance training, and, when needed, professional care.

Many people brush off ankle instability as "just something that happens" after an old sprain. But if your ankle keeps giving you trouble — during a hike, on stairs, or even walking on uneven ground — it's worth understanding why, because the warning signs are usually there well before a more serious injury occurs.

What are the early warning signs of ankle instability?

The first signs are often subtle enough to dismiss. Watch for:

  • A mild sense of imbalance or unsteadiness while walking, especially on uneven surfaces
  • Unusual weakness or fatigue in the ankle during exercise
  • A feeling that the ankle is "loose" or doesn't fully support you
  • Swelling or soreness that lingers after activity
  • A tendency to favor one side or avoid certain movements without quite realizing it

Frequent rolling or twisting of the ankle is one of the clearest signs of instability, and it's the symptom most people eventually notice — but by then, the ligaments have often already been weakened by repeated strain.

What causes chronic ankle instability?

Most cases trace back to one or more previous ankle sprains. When the ligaments that stabilize the ankle are stretched or torn and don't fully heal — or when rehab is skipped or cut short — the joint can be left mechanically loose and less able to sense its own position. Common risk factors include:

  • A history of prior ankle sprains or injuries
  • Weak muscles supporting the ankle and lower leg
  • Participation in sports involving pivoting, jumping, or uneven terrain
  • Inadequate or unsupportive footwear
  • Certain underlying conditions that affect joint stability or connective tissue

If instability has become a recurring pattern rather than an occasional inconvenience, it may indicate lateral ankle instability, a diagnosable condition our physicians treat regularly.

How is ankle instability treated?

Treatment usually starts conservatively and escalates only if needed. A typical rehabilitation plan includes:

  • Balance and proprioception training to retrain the ankle's sense of position
  • Resistance band exercises to rebuild strength in the muscles that stabilize the joint
  • Flexibility work for the calf and ankle to support a full range of motion
  • Guided physical therapy, where a therapist can design a personalized plan and correct movement patterns that contribute to instability
  • Bracing or taping during higher-risk activities while the ankle regains strength

Some patients also benefit from newer tools like smart ankle braces or neuromuscular electrical stimulation, which can support strengthening and awareness during recovery. When conservative treatment doesn't resolve persistent instability, our physicians can evaluate whether ligament reconstruction or another procedure — such as our Dual Syndesmosis TightRope approach — is appropriate.

When should you see a doctor for ankle instability?

Most instability can be managed with a consistent rehab program, but seek prompt medical attention if you experience sudden, intense pain, rapid swelling, or an inability to bear weight — these can signal a more serious injury like a fracture or ligament tear that needs immediate evaluation. Otherwise, if your ankle continues to give way despite several weeks of strengthening and balance work, a podiatrist can assess whether a more structured treatment plan is needed.

Recovery from chronic instability takes consistency, and it helps to set small, achievable goals along the way — many patients do best with support from a physical therapist and a clear plan they can stick to. When you're ready for an evaluation, you can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Ankle instability FAQs

What does ankle instability feel like?

Ankle instability often feels like a sense of looseness or unsteadiness — the ankle may wobble, give way, or roll unexpectedly, especially on uneven ground. Some people also notice fatigue or weakness in the ankle during exercise.

What causes chronic ankle instability?

It usually develops after one or more ankle sprains that didn't fully heal, leaving the supporting ligaments stretched or weakened. Weak stabilizing muscles, certain sports, and unsupportive footwear can all contribute as well.

Can ankle instability be fixed without surgery?

Yes — most cases respond well to balance training, resistance exercises, flexibility work, and guided physical therapy. Surgery is generally considered only when conservative treatment doesn't resolve persistent instability.

How long does it take to recover from ankle instability?

Recovery timelines vary depending on severity and consistency with rehab, but many patients see meaningful improvement over several weeks to a few months of dedicated strengthening and balance work.

When should I see a doctor for a rolled ankle?

Seek immediate care for sudden intense pain, rapid swelling, or inability to bear weight, as these may indicate a fracture or significant ligament tear. If your ankle keeps giving way despite home exercises, schedule an evaluation.

Are ankle braces helpful for instability?

Bracing or taping can provide added support during higher-risk activities while you rebuild strength and balance. They're best used alongside — not instead of — a rehabilitation program aimed at addressing the underlying weakness.
