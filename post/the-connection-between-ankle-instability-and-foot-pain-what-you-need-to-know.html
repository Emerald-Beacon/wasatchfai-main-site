The Connection Between Ankle Instability and Foot Pain — What You Need to Know

An ankle that keeps giving out doesn't just stay an ankle problem — over time it can change the way you walk and push pain into your arch, heel, and forefoot.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Chronic ankle instability — ankles that repeatedly roll, give out, or feel wobbly on uneven ground — doesn't stay contained to the ankle. As the joint compensates for weakened ligaments, your gait changes, and that altered movement pattern often shifts excess stress onto the structures of the foot. Left unaddressed, an unstable ankle can be the hidden root cause behind arch pain, heel pain, and other foot conditions that never seem to fully resolve.

Patients frequently come to us describing foot pain that started for "no reason," only to discover during the exam that a series of old ankle sprains left the joint loose and unsupported. Below, our podiatrists explain how ankle instability develops, how it drives foot pain, and what you can do about both.

Why does ankle instability develop?

Ankle instability most often develops after one or more ankle sprains. Each sprain stretches or tears the ligaments that hold the ankle joint together, and if those ligaments don't fully heal — or if the ankle is re-injured before it has recovered — they can remain lax. Over time, the ankle no longer holds the joint as securely as it should, a condition known as chronic ankle instability.

This is especially common among athletes and active adults who play sports involving cutting, jumping, or running on uneven terrain, but it can affect anyone with a history of ankle sprains, regardless of activity level.

How does an unstable ankle lead to foot pain?

When the ankle can't stabilize itself, the body compensates. You may unconsciously shift your weight, alter your stride, or tense different muscles to keep from rolling the ankle again. That compensation changes how force travels through the foot with every step, concentrating extra pressure on structures that weren't built to absorb it — particularly the plantar fascia, the Achilles tendon, and the nerves and joints of the forefoot.

Over months or years, this altered loading pattern can contribute to:

  • Arch pain and plantar fasciitis from uneven weight distribution across the foot
  • Heel discomfort from added strain on the plantar fascia and heel structures
  • Achilles tendonitis from compensatory changes in calf and ankle mechanics
  • Burning or tingling between the toes, which can point toward Morton's neuroma
  • Peroneal tendonitis along the outside of the ankle and foot
  • Metatarsalgia — pain and inflammation in the ball of the foot

Warning signs your ankle instability may be affecting your feet

Ankle instability doesn't always feel dramatic day to day, which is part of why it goes untreated for so long. Watch for:

  • A history of multiple ankle sprains, especially on the same side
  • A feeling that the ankle is "giving out" or rolling during normal activity
  • Increased pain or unsteadiness on uneven surfaces, like gravel or grass
  • New or worsening foot pain — arch, heel, or ball-of-foot — without a clear cause
  • A sensation of looseness or wobbliness in the ankle joint itself

If any of these sound familiar, it's worth having both your ankle and your foot evaluated together, since treating the foot pain alone often won't resolve the underlying cause.

How is ankle instability treated?

Most cases of chronic ankle instability respond well to nonsurgical care, especially when addressed before secondary foot problems set in.

Nonsurgical treatment

  • Physical therapy to rebuild strength in the muscles that support the ankle
  • Balance and proprioception training to help the ankle "relearn" stability
  • Custom orthotics to correct alignment and reduce compensatory stress on the foot — see our custom orthotics options
  • Ankle bracing for added support during activity
  • Supportive, well-fitted footwear for daily wear and sport

Surgery

When conservative treatment doesn't restore adequate stability — particularly in cases of significant ligament damage — ligament repair or reconstruction may be recommended. Surgery is generally reserved for cases that don't improve with an adequate course of nonsurgical care.

How to help prevent ankle instability from developing

A few habits go a long way toward protecting your ankles — and, by extension, your feet:

  • Warm up and stretch before sports or exercise
  • Wear supportive, activity-appropriate footwear
  • Use caution on uneven terrain, or avoid it when recovering from an injury
  • Cross-train with low-impact activities to build strength without overloading the joint
  • Work on strengthening the stabilizer muscles around the ankle, even after a sprain feels healed

When should you see a podiatrist?

See a podiatrist if you've had more than one ankle sprain, if your ankle regularly feels unstable or gives out, or if you have foot pain that doesn't have an obvious cause. Because ankle instability and foot pain are so closely linked, an evaluation of both together gives you the clearest path to lasting relief. Common Questions

Ankle instability & foot pain FAQs

Can a weak ankle really cause foot pain?

Yes. When the ankle can't stabilize itself properly, your body compensates by altering how you walk, which shifts extra stress onto the arch, heel, and forefoot. Over time, that added stress can contribute to conditions like plantar fasciitis, Achilles tendonitis, or Morton's neuroma.

What causes chronic ankle instability?

It usually develops after one or more ankle sprains that don't fully heal, or that are re-injured before recovery is complete. The ligaments that hold the ankle joint together remain lax, leaving the ankle prone to rolling or giving out.

What are the warning signs of ankle instability?

Common signs include a history of repeated ankle sprains, a feeling that the ankle is giving out or rolling during normal activity, increased unsteadiness on uneven ground, and a general sense of looseness in the joint.

Do I need surgery for ankle instability?

Most people improve with nonsurgical treatment, including physical therapy, balance training, bracing, and custom orthotics. Surgery to repair or reconstruct the ligaments is generally reserved for cases that don't respond to an adequate course of conservative care.

Can custom orthotics help with ankle instability?

Custom orthotics can help correct alignment and reduce the compensatory stress that an unstable ankle places on the foot, easing pain in the arch, heel, and forefoot while other stability-focused treatments take effect.

When should I see a podiatrist about ankle instability?

See a podiatrist if you've had more than one ankle sprain, your ankle feels unstable or gives out during normal activity, or you have foot pain without an obvious cause. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
