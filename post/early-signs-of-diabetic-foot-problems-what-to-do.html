Early Signs of Diabetic Foot Problems & What to Do

Diabetes can quietly mask an injury until it becomes serious. Here's what to watch for — and exactly what to do when you spot it.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Diabetes damages the nerves (peripheral neuropathy) and narrows the blood vessels that supply the feet, which means an injury can go unnoticed and heal slowly. Catching the early warning signs — skin color changes, numbness or tingling, swelling, and slow-healing sores — is what keeps a minor problem from becoming a serious one. If you notice any of these changes, don't wait it out; a same-week evaluation is the safest move.

Because reduced sensation can hide pain, many diabetic foot complications are discovered by sight rather than feel. That's why knowing what to look for — and acting on it promptly — matters as much as any other part of diabetes management. Our podiatrists in Farmington and South Ogden see this pattern often, and early intervention consistently leads to better outcomes than waiting.

Why diabetes puts your feet at risk

High blood sugar over time damages peripheral nerves, a condition called peripheral neuropathy, which reduces your ability to feel pain, heat, or pressure in your feet. It also narrows blood vessels, limiting the blood flow your feet need to fight infection and heal wounds. Together, these two changes mean an injury that would be obvious and quick to heal in someone without diabetes can go unnoticed and linger in someone who has it.

Early signs to watch for

Get in the habit of looking closely at your feet, since some of the earliest signs are things you'll see before you feel them. Warning signs include:

  • Skin color changes — pale, reddish, or bluish patches, or a foot that looks different from the other
  • Temperature differences between one foot and the other, which can point to circulation changes
  • Swelling or pain, especially pain that worsens with walking
  • Numbness, tingling, or a burning sensation, common early signs of neuropathy
  • Slow-healing cuts, blisters, or cracks in the skin
  • Signs of infection, such as red streaks, warmth, drainage, or a foul odor

Any one of these on its own is worth a closer look. Several together, or a wound that isn't visibly improving within a day or two, warrants a call to our office.

What to do if you notice a warning sign

Start by cleaning any open area gently with mild soap and water, covering it with a clean, dry dressing, and staying off that foot as much as possible. Check it again the next day. If the area is not clearly improving — or if you see redness spreading, swelling, drainage, a foul odor, or a wound that hasn't improved within one to two weeks — seek care promptly rather than waiting to see if it resolves on its own. People with diabetes should treat any foot wound as something to have evaluated, since what looks minor on the surface can be more serious underneath.

Daily habits that catch problems early

A short daily routine makes it far more likely you'll catch a problem while it's still small:

  • Inspect your feet every day, including between the toes, in good lighting
  • Wash with warm water and moisturize to prevent cracked, dry skin
  • Wear well-fitting shoes with seamless socks, and never go barefoot
  • Keep blood glucose in your target range
  • Avoid smoking, which further restricts circulation

These same habits are the backbone of our broader guide to diabetic foot care, which covers footwear, hygiene, and professional screening in more depth.

Why professional screening still matters

Even with a careful daily routine, some changes — like reduced circulation or early nerve damage — are easier to detect with a clinical exam than on your own. We recommend an annual comprehensive foot exam for anyone with diabetes, with more frequent visits if you have a history of ulcers, neuropathy, or poor circulation. If it's time for your exam, or if you've noticed any of the warning signs above, you can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Diabetic foot warning sign FAQs

What is the earliest sign of a diabetic foot problem?

Skin color or temperature changes and numbness or tingling are often the earliest signs, since they can appear before you notice any pain. Because diabetes can reduce sensation, it's important to look at your feet regularly rather than rely on how they feel.

Why might a diabetic foot injury not hurt?

Diabetes can damage peripheral nerves over time, a condition called peripheral neuropathy, which reduces your ability to feel pain, heat, or pressure. That's why an injury can go unnoticed and worsen before it becomes painful enough to catch your attention.

How long should I wait before seeking care for a foot wound?

If a wound hasn't clearly improved within one to two weeks of home care — or sooner if you see spreading redness, swelling, drainage, or a foul odor — you should have it evaluated promptly rather than waiting. Diabetic foot wounds can worsen quickly, so early care matters.

What does infection in a diabetic foot look like?

Signs of infection include red streaks extending from a wound, warmth or swelling in the area, fluid drainage, a foul odor, or a wound that isn't healing. Any of these signs call for prompt medical attention rather than continued home care.

Can early signs of diabetic foot problems be reversed?

Many early changes, such as minor skin irritation or a small wound, respond well to prompt care and improved blood sugar control. The key is catching them early — the longer a problem goes unaddressed, the harder it can be to resolve.

How often should diabetic patients have their feet examined?

An annual comprehensive foot exam is recommended for most people with diabetes, with more frequent visits if you have neuropathy, poor circulation, or a history of foot ulcers. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
