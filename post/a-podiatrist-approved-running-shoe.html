A Podiatrist-Approved Running Shoe

The right running shoe is built on real science, not marketing. Here's what to look for so your footwear supports your feet instead of working against them.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

A podiatrist-approved running shoe is one built with real biomechanical research behind it — proper cushioning, a supportive heel counter, and a fit matched to your foot type and running gait. Choosing the right pair is one of the simplest, most effective ways to reduce your risk of common running injuries like plantar fasciitis, shin splints, and stress fractures.

People ran for centuries before modern running shoes existed, so it's a fair question: do they actually matter? The answer is yes. Today's running shoes are designed using a scientific understanding of how the foot loads, absorbs impact, and moves through the running gait cycle — support that a standard sneaker or casual shoe simply isn't built to provide. If you're logging regular miles on Utah's roads and trails, the shoe on your foot is one of the biggest variables you control.

Why does the running shoe you choose matter?

Running places far more repetitive impact on your feet than walking does — each foot strike can transmit several times your body weight through the joint. Running shoes are engineered specifically for that motion, with cushioning and structure designed to absorb shock, support your arch through the stride, and stabilize your foot as it strikes and pushes off. A shoe not designed for running, or one that's simply the wrong fit for your foot, can leave your plantar fascia, Achilles tendon, and joints absorbing stress they weren't built to handle on their own.

What should you look for in a running shoe?

  • Adequate cushioning to absorb repeated impact, especially if you run on pavement
  • A supportive heel counter that keeps your heel stable and centered with each strike
  • Arch support matched to your foot type — flat, neutral, or high-arched feet each load differently
  • Room in the toe box so your toes aren't compressed, which can contribute to bunions and nail problems
  • A fit matched to your gait, including whether you overpronate, supinate, or strike neutrally

If you're unsure which category fits your foot, a gait evaluation with a podiatrist — or a fitting at a specialty running store — can identify whether you need extra stability, added cushioning, or a more neutral shoe.

Should I choose a specific running shoe brand?

Several major manufacturers have invested heavily in running-specific research and development, and each tends to fit differently. The right choice comes down to which shoe fits your particular foot shape and gait — not the logo on the side. When trying on running shoes, prioritize fit and function over brand name, and don't assume the shoe that works for a friend or training partner will work the same way for you.

When should you replace your running shoes?

Running shoes lose their cushioning and support well before they look visibly worn out. Most running shoes should be replaced roughly every 300–500 miles, or sooner if you notice new aches in your feet, shins, knees, or hips during or after runs — a sign the shoe may no longer be absorbing impact the way it should.

Can the wrong running shoe cause foot problems?

Yes. Ill-fitting or unsupportive running shoes are a common contributing factor in overuse injuries such as plantar fasciitis and Achilles tendonitis, as well as blisters, black toenails, and worsening symptoms in people with flat feet or bunions. If you're dealing with foot pain that flares up during or after running, the shoe itself is one of the first things worth evaluating — alongside your training volume and running surface.

If you've changed shoes, upped your mileage, or started a new training plan and you're now dealing with foot or ankle pain, it's worth having it checked out. For runners who need extra arch support beyond what a shoe alone provides, custom orthotics can be built into your running shoes for a more tailored fit. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office if running-related pain isn't improving on its own.

Common Questions

Running shoe FAQs

What makes a running shoe different from a regular sneaker?

Running shoes are designed with cushioning, structure, and support specifically engineered for the repetitive impact of running, based on research into how the foot moves through the running gait. A regular sneaker isn't built to absorb that same repeated impact, which can leave your feet and joints doing more of the work.

How do I know what type of running shoe is right for my foot?

The right shoe depends on your foot shape and running gait, including whether you overpronate, supinate, or strike neutrally. A gait evaluation with a podiatrist or a fitting at a specialty running store can help identify whether you need extra stability, added cushioning, or a neutral shoe.

Does the brand of running shoe matter?

Several major brands invest heavily in running-specific research, but each fits differently. What matters most is finding a shoe that fits your particular foot shape and gait, not the brand name on the side.

How often should I replace my running shoes?

Most running shoes should be replaced roughly every 300–500 miles, since cushioning and support break down before the shoe looks visibly worn. New aches in your feet, shins, knees, or hips during or after runs can also signal it's time for a new pair.

Can the wrong running shoe cause foot pain or injury?

Yes. Ill-fitting or unsupportive running shoes are a common contributing factor in overuse injuries like plantar fasciitis and Achilles tendonitis, as well as blisters and worsening symptoms in people with flat feet or bunions.

Should I see a podiatrist if running causes foot pain?

Yes, especially if the pain persists after resting, changing shoes, or adjusting your training. A podiatrist can evaluate whether your footwear, gait, or an underlying condition is contributing to the pain. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
