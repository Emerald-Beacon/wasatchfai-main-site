Chronic Ankle Instability: Treatment and Exercises

If your ankle keeps "giving way" long after a sprain should have healed, you may have chronic ankle instability. Here's why it happens and how to rebuild lasting stability.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Chronic ankle instability is a condition in which the ankle repeatedly "gives way," rolls, or feels wobbly during everyday activity — long after an initial sprain should have healed. It typically develops when the ligaments on the outside of the ankle don't heal properly after one or more sprains, leaving the joint mechanically loose and the surrounding muscles less able to react quickly enough to prevent another roll. The encouraging news is that most people regain stability with a focused program of bracing, balance training, and strengthening.

Almost everyone rolls an ankle at some point, and most sprains heal without lasting problems. But if your ankle still feels unreliable weeks or months later — buckling on uneven ground, giving out during sports, or simply feeling "loose" — that's a sign the ligaments and stabilizing muscles never fully recovered. Left unaddressed, chronic ankle instability tends to lead to repeat sprains, which can compound the underlying damage. Here's what our podiatrists want you to know about causes, exercises, and treatment.

What is chronic ankle instability?

Chronic ankle instability is ongoing weakness and looseness in the ankle joint, usually on the outer (lateral) side, that causes the ankle to give way repeatedly during normal activity. It most often follows one or more ankle sprains — particularly injuries to the anterior talofibular ligament, one of the main ligaments that stabilizes the outside of the ankle. When that ligament heals with excess laxity or scar tissue instead of its original strength, the ankle loses some of its mechanical stability.

Instability isn't only a ligament problem, though. Repeated sprains also disrupt proprioception — your body's sense of joint position and its ability to react quickly to protect the joint. That combination of loose ligaments and slower protective reflexes is why the ankle keeps rolling even on relatively flat ground.

What are the symptoms of chronic ankle instability?

The hallmark symptom is a persistent feeling that the ankle is unreliable, even well after an initial sprain. Common signs include:

  • The ankle repeatedly "gives way" or rolls, even on flat surfaces
  • A chronic feeling of looseness, wobbliness, or weakness in the joint
  • Recurring sprains with less and less force required to cause them
  • Persistent swelling or tenderness around the outer ankle
  • Pain or discomfort with activity, especially on uneven ground
  • Reduced confidence or hesitation during sports and exercise

What causes chronic ankle instability?

Chronic ankle instability is most often the result of one or more ankle sprains that didn't fully heal — either because the ligament healed loosely, rehabilitation was skipped, or the person returned to activity before strength and balance were restored. Related contributing factors include:

  • Damage to the anterior talofibular ligament or other lateral ankle ligaments — see our lateral ankle instability page for more detail
  • Reduced proprioception, or the body's sense of joint position
  • Weak muscles surrounding the ankle, particularly the peroneals
  • Naturally loose ligaments or a prior history of multiple sprains
  • Returning to sports or activity too soon after a sprain
  • Unsupportive footwear or training on uneven surfaces

How is chronic ankle instability treated?

Most patients regain stability with a structured, nonsurgical rehabilitation program. Treatment usually starts conservatively and only progresses to more advanced options if the ankle continues to give way despite consistent effort.

Conservative management

  • RICE therapy (rest, ice, compression, elevation) during flare-ups
  • Physical therapy focused on rebuilding strength and range of motion
  • Balance and proprioceptive training to retrain the ankle's reflexes
  • Ankle bracing, lace-up or rigid, for extra support during activity
  • Biomechanics correction through custom orthotics if foot alignment is contributing

Strengthening & balance exercises

A consistent home exercise routine is often the difference between an ankle that keeps giving way and one that holds up. Exercises we commonly recommend include single-leg balance holds, resistance-band ankle strengthening (inversion, eversion, dorsiflexion, and plantarflexion), calf raises, and balance-board or wobble-board drills that retrain the ankle to react quickly to uneven surfaces. Your physical therapist or podiatrist can build a progression that matches your activity level.

Advanced & surgical options

For ankles that remain unstable despite a thorough course of conservative treatment, additional options include platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections to support ligament healing, and, for more significant cases, the Broström procedure — a surgical repair that tightens the damaged lateral ligaments. Severe or long-standing cases occasionally require a tendon graft to reconstruct ligament support. Surgery is reserved for patients who don't respond to rehabilitation.

What's the recovery timeline?

Recovery depends on how long the instability has been present and how consistently you follow your rehab program. Milder cases can improve within a matter of weeks, while longer-standing chronic instability typically requires several months of consistent strengthening and balance work before the ankle feels reliably stable again. The majority of patients do regain stability with proper rehabilitation, strengthening, and bracing — but skipping the exercise program is one of the most common reasons instability persists or recurs.

See a podiatrist if your ankle continues to give way, swell, or hurt weeks after a sprain, or if you've had multiple sprains on the same side. Left untreated, chronic instability raises your risk of further injury to the ankle cartilage and surrounding joints. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Chronic ankle instability FAQs

What does chronic ankle instability feel like?

It usually feels like your ankle is "loose" or unreliable — giving way, rolling, or buckling during normal activity, even on relatively flat ground, well after an initial sprain should have healed.

What causes an ankle to keep giving way?

It's usually caused by lateral ankle ligaments — most often the anterior talofibular ligament — that healed loosely after a sprain, combined with reduced proprioception and weakened stabilizing muscles that can't react quickly enough to prevent another roll.

Can chronic ankle instability be fixed without surgery?

Yes, in most cases. The majority of patients regain stability through physical therapy, balance and proprioceptive training, strengthening exercises, and bracing. Surgery is reserved for cases that don't improve after a thorough course of conservative treatment.

What exercises help with chronic ankle instability?

Single-leg balance holds, resistance-band strengthening in all directions, calf raises, and balance-board or wobble-board drills are commonly used to rebuild strength and retrain the ankle's protective reflexes.

How long does it take to recover from chronic ankle instability?

Milder cases can improve within a few weeks of consistent exercise, while longer-standing instability often takes several months of rehabilitation before the ankle feels reliably stable. Consistency with the exercise program is the biggest factor in recovery time.

When should I see a podiatrist about ankle instability?

See a podiatrist if your ankle continues to give way, swell, or hurt weeks after a sprain, or if you've experienced multiple sprains on the same side. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
