What to Know About Bunion Surgery in 2025

Modern bunion correction has come a long way from the cast-and-crutches surgery your parents may remember. Here's how today's options compare — and how to decide if surgery is right for you.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

A bunion forms when the big toe gradually angles inward toward the second toe while the first metatarsal bone shifts outward, creating a bony bump along the inside edge of the foot. Beyond the cosmetic change, bunions can cause chronic pain, make shoes uncomfortable, and limit how far and how comfortably you can walk. When conservative care no longer keeps pace with the pain, surgery is the most reliable way to correct the underlying bone position.

If you're weighing bunion surgery, you've likely noticed the landscape has shifted in recent years. Minimally invasive and computer- and robotic-assisted techniques have joined traditional osteotomy as real options, each with its own trade-offs in incision size, recovery time, and the degree of correction it can achieve. Here's what our podiatrists want you to know before you decide.

What causes a bunion to form?

Family history is the single biggest factor — bunions run in families because foot shape and mechanics are inherited. Beyond genetics, several things can accelerate or worsen a bunion over time:

  • Tight, narrow, or pointed footwear, especially high heels
  • Prolonged standing or walking on hard surfaces
  • Arthritis affecting the big toe joint
  • Natural changes in foot structure that come with age
  • Flat feet or other mechanical imbalances that shift how weight loads the forefoot

Bunions do not reverse on their own, and they typically progress gradually. Addressing footwear and other contributing factors early can slow that progression, even if it can't fully stop it.

Do I need surgery, or can I manage a bunion without it?

Not every bunion needs surgery. We start with conservative measures — wider or more supportive shoes, padding, orthotics, activity modification, and anti-inflammatory care — and many patients manage comfortably for years this way. Surgery becomes the right conversation when the bunion causes persistent pain that limits your daily activities, when it interferes with finding shoes that fit, or when conservative measures have stopped helping.

How has bunion surgery changed?

Traditional bunion surgery (osteotomy) involves cutting and realigning the bone, then securing it with screws or plates while it heals. It's a well-established, effective approach, but recovery typically involves weeks of limited weight-bearing, often in a cast or boot, followed by a longer course of physical therapy.

Newer minimally invasive techniques use a series of very small incisions to reposition the bone with less disruption to the surrounding soft tissue. Because there's less tissue trauma, many patients experience less post-operative pain, less scarring, and an earlier return to protected weight-bearing than with traditional open surgery. Some practices also use 3D imaging and computer- or robotic-assisted guidance to plan and execute the correction with greater precision.

Which approach is best depends on the severity of the deformity. Mild to moderate bunions are often good candidates for minimally invasive correction, while more severe deformities may still require a more extensive open procedure to achieve a lasting correction. Your age, overall health, activity level, and personal goals all factor into the decision.

What does recovery look like?

Recovery unfolds in stages regardless of technique, though the timeline varies with how extensive the surgery is:

  • The first days to weeks focus on elevation, icing, and pain control while the initial swelling subsides. Most patients use a surgical shoe, boot, or crutches to limit weight on the foot.
  • The following weeks bring a gradual return to weight-bearing as your surgeon clears you, along with gentle range-of-motion exercises.
  • Later weeks to months involve strengthening and physical therapy to restore normal gait and function.

Many patients return to daily activities in athletic or supportive shoes within a matter of weeks, though returning to snug dress shoes or high heels takes longer — often a few months — regardless of which surgical technique was used. Every recovery timeline is individual, and your surgeon will tailor guidance to your procedure and healing progress.

What should I ask before choosing a surgeon?

Look for a board-certified, fellowship-trained podiatric or orthopedic surgeon with specific experience in the technique you're considering. It's reasonable to ask how many similar procedures they perform, what results their patients typically see, and what your specific recovery plan will look like. A good surgeon will walk you through the pros and cons of each option honestly rather than steering you toward one technique for every patient.

Common Questions

Bunion surgery FAQs

Is bunion surgery worth it for a mild bunion?

Conservative treatments like supportive shoes, padding, and orthotics should be tried first for a mild bunion. Surgery is generally reserved for bunions causing persistent pain that limits your activities or quality of life, rather than for cosmetic reasons alone.

What's the difference between traditional and minimally invasive bunion surgery?

Traditional osteotomy uses a larger incision to cut and realign the bone and typically involves a longer, more restricted recovery. Minimally invasive techniques use several small incisions, which generally means less tissue trauma, less scarring, and a faster return to protected weight-bearing — though not every bunion is a candidate for the minimally invasive approach.

Will I be able to walk right after bunion surgery?

It depends on the technique and the extent of the correction. Some minimally invasive procedures allow limited, protected weight-bearing in a surgical boot within days, while more extensive traditional surgery often requires a longer period of non-weight-bearing or limited weight-bearing while the bone heals.

How long until I can wear normal shoes after bunion surgery?

Most patients can wear athletic or supportive shoes within several weeks, with a return to regular shoes soon after as swelling continues to resolve. Snug dress shoes, narrow shoes, and high heels typically need to wait several months to allow the bone to fully heal and swelling to fully settle.

Can a bunion come back after surgery?

Recurrence is possible with any bunion surgery, though modern techniques and careful surgical planning aim to minimize that risk. Following your surgeon's post-operative guidance on footwear and activity is one of the best ways to protect your correction long-term.

Does insurance cover bunion surgery?

Most insurance plans cover bunion surgery when it is deemed medically necessary — generally when conservative treatment has failed to relieve pain that affects daily function. Coverage details, deductibles, and out-of-pocket costs vary by plan, so it's best to confirm specifics with your insurer and our office before scheduling.
