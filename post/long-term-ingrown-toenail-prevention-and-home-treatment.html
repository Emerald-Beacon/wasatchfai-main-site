Long-Term Ingrown Toenail Prevention and Home Treatment

A tender, reddened nail edge doesn't have to become a recurring problem. Here's how to treat a mild ingrown toenail at home, spot the signs it needs professional care, and stop it from coming back.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

An ingrown toenail happens when the edge of the nail grows into the surrounding skin, causing pain, redness, and swelling — most often on the big toe. Mild cases often respond well to careful at-home care, but nail habits, footwear, and even genetics can make some people prone to repeat episodes. Knowing how to treat a mild ingrown toenail safely, and when to stop trying and see a podiatrist, can save you from a much more painful and stubborn infection.

Ingrown toenails are one of the most common foot complaints we see in Farmington and South Ogden, and for good reason — the causes are everyday ones: tight shoes, an aggressive at-home pedicure, or simply the way your nail happens to grow. Below, our podiatrists cover what causes them, how to care for a mild one at home, and the long-term habits that keep them from coming back.

What causes an ingrown toenail?

Some people are simply more prone to ingrown toenails due to genetics — the natural curve or width of the nail plate can push it into the skin fold no matter how carefully it's trimmed. Beyond genetics, the most frequent contributing factors include:

  • Cutting nails too short or rounding the corners instead of trimming straight across
  • Tight, narrow shoes or high heels that crowd the toes
  • Repeated pressure from athletic activity, especially in snug athletic shoes
  • Stubbing or otherwise injuring the toe
  • Poor foot hygiene, which allows minor nail-edge irritation to progress

What are the early warning signs?

Catching an ingrown toenail early makes home treatment far more likely to work. Watch for:

  • Tenderness along one or both edges of the nail, especially when wearing shoes
  • Redness, swelling, or warmth around the nail fold
  • A feeling of pressure or sharpness when the area is bumped
  • Pus, drainage, or a noticeably warm, throbbing sensation — signs the area may be infected

If you notice pus or a spreading warm, red area, that points to a possible infection rather than a simple mild case — see the guidance on professional care below.

How do you treat a mild ingrown toenail at home?

For a genuinely mild ingrown toenail — tender and a little red, but without pus, spreading redness, or fever — the following steps can help it grow out on its own:

  • Warm soaks: Soak the foot in warm water (plain or with Epsom salt) for 15–20 minutes, two to three times a day. This softens the skin and nail and reduces swelling.
  • Gentle nail lifting: After soaking, some people use a clean, sanitized cotton swab to very gently lift the edge of the nail away from the skin, encouraging it to grow up and over the skin fold rather than into it. Stop if this causes significant pain.
  • Proper trimming: Trim toenails straight across — never rounded or too short — roughly every 4–6 weeks, and avoid picking or tearing at the nail edge.
  • Supportive footwear: Switch to shoes with a roomy, breathable toe box while the area heals, and avoid tight athletic shoes or high heels in the meantime.
  • Natural anti-inflammatory aids: Some people find a diluted tea tree oil application or a turmeric paste soothing for mild inflammation, though these are supportive measures, not substitutes for the steps above.

Give home treatment several days to work. If tenderness, redness, or swelling isn't clearly improving — or gets worse — it's time to have it evaluated rather than continuing to self-treat.

How can you prevent ingrown toenails long-term?

Because nail shape and skin-fold anatomy play such a large role, some people will always be more prone to ingrown toenails than others. Even so, a few consistent habits meaningfully lower how often they happen:

  • Always trim nails straight across with clean, sharp clippers — never curved or too short
  • Choose shoes with adequate toe room and breathable materials, and avoid consistently tight or pointed footwear
  • Keep feet clean and dry, changing socks daily and drying thoroughly between the toes
  • Support nail strength with a diet that includes adequate biotin, protein, zinc, and iron
  • If you're prone to recurring ingrown toenails on the same corner, ask your podiatrist about in-office options that address the nail permanently — see our ingrown toenail treatment page for details

When should you see a podiatrist?

Home care is appropriate for mild, uncomplicated cases, but you should schedule an evaluation if you notice pus or drainage, spreading redness or warmth, worsening pain, or if the area hasn't improved after several days of consistent home treatment. Anyone with diabetes, poor circulation, or neuropathy should see a podiatrist for any ingrown toenail right away rather than attempting home treatment, since minor nail infections can progress quickly in those conditions. In the office, we can safely and often painlessly remove the offending nail edge — a quick in-office procedure that resolves both the current episode and, when needed, prevents that portion of the nail from regrowing. Common Questions

Ingrown toenail FAQs

Can I treat an ingrown toenail myself at home?

Yes, if it's mild — tender and slightly red without pus or spreading redness. Warm soaks, gentle nail lifting, proper trimming, and roomier footwear can help it resolve. If it isn't improving after several days, or gets worse, it's time to see a podiatrist.

How do I know if my ingrown toenail is infected?

Signs of infection include pus or drainage, spreading redness or warmth around the nail, increasing pain, or a throbbing sensation. Any of these signs mean you should see a podiatrist rather than continuing home treatment.

What's the right way to trim toenails to prevent ingrown nails?

Trim toenails straight across with clean, sharp clippers — never rounded at the corners or cut too short. Doing this roughly every 4–6 weeks, along with choosing roomier footwear, is one of the most effective habits for preventing ingrown toenails.

Why do I keep getting ingrown toenails on the same toe?

Recurring ingrown toenails on the same spot usually point to the natural shape or curve of that nail. If it keeps happening despite good trimming and footwear habits, ask about in-office options that treat the nail edge permanently.

Should people with diabetes treat ingrown toenails at home?

No. People with diabetes, poor circulation, or neuropathy should have any ingrown toenail evaluated by a podiatrist right away, since minor nail infections can progress quickly and are harder to feel early when nerve sensation is reduced.

What does a podiatrist do for a stubborn ingrown toenail?

In the office, we can safely remove the offending nail edge, usually with local anesthesia, providing prompt relief. For nails that keep coming back in the same spot, we can also treat the nail bed so that portion doesn't regrow.
