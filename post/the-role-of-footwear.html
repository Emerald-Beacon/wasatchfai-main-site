The Role of Footwear in Foot Health

Your shoes can either support your feet or quietly work against them. Here's how to tell which one yours are doing — and what to look for instead.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Footwear is one of the most overlooked causes of chronic foot pain. Shoes that lack adequate cushioning, arch support, or a firm heel counter force your feet to absorb more stress with every step, and over time that added strain can trigger or worsen conditions like plantar fasciitis, bunions, and neuromas. The good news is that most footwear-related pain improves once you identify the problem and switch to shoes that actually support how your feet move.

Patients often come to us assuming their foot pain has an internal cause — an injury, a joint problem, something structural — when in reality their shoes are doing much of the damage. Below, our podiatrists explain how footwear shapes foot health, the warning signs your shoes aren't working for you, and how to choose footwear that will.

How does footwear affect foot health?

Every step you take sends force through your foot, up your ankle, and into your knees, hips, and lower back. Well-built shoes absorb and distribute that force evenly; poor ones don't, so the impact concentrates in specific spots — usually the heel, the ball of the foot, or the arch. Individual differences like flat feet, high arches, and gait imbalances change how any given shoe performs on your particular foot, which is why the "right" shoe is different for everyone.

Can the wrong shoes actually cause foot pain?

Yes. Poor footwear choices are a common trigger for several conditions we treat regularly, including plantar fasciitis, neuromas, and tendon strain. Plantar fasciitis in particular thrives in shoes that provide little shock absorption or arch support, since the plantar fascia ends up doing work the shoe should be sharing. Tight or rigid shoes — especially narrow toe boxes and high heels — can also worsen existing deformities such as hammertoes and bunions by crowding the toes into an unnatural position.

What are the warning signs my shoes aren't working for me?

Your shoes are often trying to tell you something before the pain sets in. Watch for:

  • Uneven wear on the outsole (more wear on one side than the other)
  • Blisters or hot spots that show up in the same place repeatedly
  • Numbness or tingling in the toes during or after wearing certain shoes
  • Shoes that need constant retightening to stay secure
  • Pain that predictably worsens after a day in a specific pair of shoes

Any of these is a good reason to reevaluate what's on your feet — and if the pain persists despite a footwear change, it's worth having it looked at.

What should I look for when choosing shoes?

Different activities call for different shoes, and no single pair does everything well. As a general guide:

  • Everyday wear: supportive shoes with real arch support and a firm heel counter
  • Exercise and sports: athletic shoes built for the specific impact level of that activity
  • Work environments: slip-resistant soles appropriate to the surfaces you're on
  • Winter conditions: insulated, traction-focused boots that also support the arch

Whatever the category, look for cushioning that hasn't broken down, a toe box roomy enough for your toes to move naturally, breathable and well-constructed materials to prevent moisture and friction, and a fit that stays secure without pinching. It also helps to try shoes on later in the day, when your feet are at their most swollen — a shoe that fits at 8 a.m. can feel tight by evening.

Do I need custom orthotics?

Not always — many people do well with supportive off-the-shelf shoes. But custom orthotics can make a real difference if you have persistent pain despite wearing supportive footwear, or if you have flat feet, high arches, or a gait imbalance that off-the-shelf shoes can't fully accommodate. They're built specifically for your foot shape and can be moved between shoes as needed.

What else supports healthy feet besides good shoes?

Footwear matters most, but daily habits reinforce it: stretching your calves and plantar fascia, strengthening the small muscles of the foot, working on ankle flexibility, and rotating between two or more pairs of shoes rather than wearing the same pair every day. Together, these reduce repetitive strain on any one part of the foot.

When should I see a foot specialist?

See a podiatrist if foot pain lasts more than a week, affects your ability to walk normally, or keeps coming back even after you've changed your footwear. Chronic pain deserves a full evaluation to identify the underlying cause and build a treatment plan specific to your foot. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Footwear & foot health FAQs

What kind of shoe should I wear if I have chronic foot pain?

Look for shoes with good arch support, cushioning, and a firm heel counter. If pain continues despite supportive footwear, custom orthotics from a foot specialist can be added to target your specific condition.

Can old or worn-out shoes cause foot pain?

Yes. Once the cushioning in a shoe compresses and flattens, your feet have to work harder to absorb impact with every step. This can contribute to heel pain, tendon strain, and joint pain over time.

Do I need orthotics?

Orthotics tend to help people who still have persistent pain despite wearing supportive shoes, as well as those with flat feet, high arches, or gait abnormalities. A podiatrist can tell you whether they're a good fit for your feet.

Are sandals bad for my feet?

Sandals aren't inherently harmful, but many lack the arch support your feet need for extended wear. If you'll be on your feet for a while, choose sandals with a firm midsole and a supportive footbed.

How do I know if my shoes are the cause of my foot pain?

Signs your shoes may be the culprit include uneven sole wear, recurring blisters, numbness in the toes, or pain that shows up predictably after wearing a certain pair. Switching shoes and seeing whether symptoms improve is a good first step.

When should I see a foot specialist for foot pain?

See a specialist if your pain lasts more than a week, limits your ability to walk, or keeps recurring even after changing your footwear. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
