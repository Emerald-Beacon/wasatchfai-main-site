Why You Shouldn't Fly Right After a Foot Surgery

A long flight is one of the riskiest things you can do in the weeks right after foot or ankle surgery. Here's why, and how to plan your travel safely.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated November 29, 2021

Flying too soon after foot or ankle surgery raises your risk of a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) — a blood clot that can form in the leg and, in serious cases, travel to the lungs. The combination of prolonged sitting on a plane and your body's natural post-surgical clotting response is what makes air travel particularly risky during early recovery. Most surgeons recommend waiting at least four weeks before flying, and longer for more involved procedures.

It is a common question in our post-op visits: "I have a trip booked — can I still fly?" The honest answer depends on the surgery, how your recovery is progressing, and how long the flight is. Below, we explain the medical reasoning behind the recommendation and what to do if travel is unavoidable.

Why is flying after foot surgery risky?

Any flight longer than about four hours increases blood clot risk for every passenger, healthy or not, because sitting still for extended periods slows circulation in the legs. After foot or ankle surgery, that baseline risk is compounded by hemostasis — the body's natural clotting process that activates to help you heal from the incision. With reduced mobility from your surgical boot, cast, or crutches added on top, blood can pool in the lower leg and form a clot more easily than it normally would.

A DVT is concerning enough on its own, causing pain, swelling, and warmth in the leg. The greater danger is that part of the clot can break free and travel to the lungs, causing a pulmonary embolism — a medical emergency. This is the core reason surgeons are cautious about air travel in the early weeks after a procedure.

Who is at higher risk of a blood clot after surgery?

While anyone recovering from foot or ankle surgery has some elevated risk, certain factors raise it further:

  • Prolonged sitting or immobility, whether on a plane, in a car, or at a desk
  • Reduced mobility from a cast, boot, or non-weight-bearing recovery
  • Being overweight
  • Pregnancy
  • Age over 60
  • Use of oral contraceptives or hormone therapy
  • A personal or family history of blood clotting disorders

If more than one of these applies to you, it is especially important to talk with your surgeon before booking any travel during recovery.

How long should you wait to fly after foot or ankle surgery?

As a general guideline, most surgeons recommend avoiding air travel for at least four weeks after foot or ankle surgery. For more extensive procedures, or for patients with additional risk factors, your surgeon may advise waiting three months or longer before a long flight. The right timeline depends on the specific procedure, your individual risk factors, and how your recovery is progressing — which is why this is always a conversation to have directly with your surgical team rather than a one-size-fits-all rule.

What if you have to fly during your recovery?

Sometimes travel cannot be postponed. If your surgeon clears you to fly during recovery, these steps can help reduce your risk:

  • Move regularly. Stand, stretch, or take a short walk down the aisle every hour or so when possible
  • Do seated leg exercises. Ankle pumps and calf raises in your seat help keep blood circulating
  • Consider compression stockings. Graduated compression hosiery can help reduce pooling in the lower leg
  • Stay hydrated. Drink water throughout the flight
  • Limit caffeine and alcohol. Both can contribute to dehydration, which increases clot risk
  • Ask your surgeon about additional precautions. Depending on your risk factors, your surgeon may recommend blood-thinning medication for the trip

What symptoms should prompt immediate medical attention?

Know the warning signs of a blood clot so you can act quickly if they appear during or after travel. Seek immediate medical care for leg swelling, warmth, redness, or pain — especially in the calf — and seek emergency care right away for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or coughing up blood, which can indicate a pulmonary embolism. If you have any of these symptoms after a flight during your recovery, do not wait to be evaluated. When you are planning a trip during recovery, it is always best to talk with your surgical team first.

Common Questions

Flying after foot surgery FAQs

How long after foot surgery can I fly?

Most surgeons recommend waiting at least four weeks after foot or ankle surgery before flying. More extensive procedures, or additional risk factors, may call for waiting three months or longer. Always confirm the right timeline with your surgeon before booking travel.

Why is flying risky after surgery specifically?

Flying combines two clot risk factors at once: prolonged sitting, which slows blood flow in the legs, and your body's natural post-surgical clotting response. Reduced mobility from a boot, cast, or crutches adds further risk, making blood clots more likely than during normal daily activity.

What is a DVT and why is it dangerous?

A deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a blood clot that forms in a deep vein, usually in the leg. It is dangerous because part of the clot can break loose and travel to the lungs, causing a pulmonary embolism — a serious, potentially life-threatening emergency.

What can I do to lower my clot risk if I must fly during recovery?

Move and stretch periodically during the flight, do seated ankle pumps and calf raises, wear compression stockings, stay well hydrated, and limit caffeine and alcohol. Your surgeon may also recommend blood-thinning medication for the trip depending on your risk factors.

What symptoms of a blood clot should I watch for after flying?

Watch for swelling, warmth, redness, or pain in the leg, especially the calf. Seek emergency care immediately for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or coughing up blood, as these can signal a pulmonary embolism.

Am I at higher risk of a clot after surgery if I'm older or overweight?

Yes. Age over 60, being overweight, pregnancy, hormone-based medications, and a personal or family history of clotting disorders all increase clot risk after surgery. If any apply to you, discuss travel plans with your surgeon before booking a flight.
