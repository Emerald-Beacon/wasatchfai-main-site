How to Get Rid of Severe Ankle Pain at Home: Expert Tips

Sprains, arthritis, fractures, and tendonitis can all cause severe ankle pain — and each responds a little differently. Here's what actually helps at home, and when it's time to stop waiting it out.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Severe ankle pain most often comes from a sprain, arthritis flare, fracture, or tendonitis, and how you should treat it at home depends on which one you're dealing with. For most acute injuries, start with rest, ice, and elevation; for longer-lasting stiffness, gentle movement and anti-inflammatory strategies tend to help more. Either way, if the pain hasn't meaningfully improved within a week, it's time to have it evaluated rather than continuing to wait it out.

Ankle pain can range from a dull ache after a long day to a sharp, swelling injury that stops you in your tracks. Because so many different problems can cause it, treating "ankle pain" as one single thing can actually slow your recovery. Below, our podiatrists break down the most common causes, what genuinely helps at home, and the signs that mean you should come see us.

What's causing your severe ankle pain?

Four conditions account for the large majority of severe ankle pain we see:

  • Sprains — the ligaments that stabilize the ankle are strained or torn, usually from rolling or twisting the joint, causing swelling and bruising
  • Arthritis — long-term joint inflammation that produces persistent stiffness and aching, often worse in the morning or after rest
  • Fractures — a break in one of the ankle bones, typically from a fall, twist, or direct impact
  • Tendonitis — inflammation of the tendons around the ankle, particularly common in runners and other athletes who load the joint repetitively

Identifying which of these is most likely matters, because a sprain and an arthritis flare respond to very different at-home strategies.

Should you treat acute pain differently than chronic pain?

Yes. A fresh, acute injury — like a sprain from a fall or a misstep — generally responds best to immediate rest, ice, and elevation to limit swelling in the first 24 to 48 hours. Long-standing, chronic pain, such as from arthritis or lingering tendonitis, tends to respond better to gentle movement, stretching, and anti-inflammatory strategies rather than complete rest, since stiffness from staying still can make chronic joint pain worse.

What can you do at home for ankle pain?

Rest, ice, and elevation

For a new or acute injury, apply ice for 15 to 20 minutes at a time and keep the ankle elevated above heart level when possible. This combination helps limit swelling in the early stages, when inflammation is doing the most damage.

Gentle movement and stretching

Once the initial swelling has settled, or for chronic stiffness, gentle ankle circles, calf stretches, and toe raises can help maintain range of motion and prevent the joint from stiffening further. Avoid pushing through sharp pain — mild discomfort during stretching is different from pain that worsens with movement.

Heat therapy

Heat is generally more helpful after the initial swelling has decreased, since it can improve blood flow and muscle flexibility around the joint. Applying heat too early on a freshly swollen ankle can sometimes make swelling worse.

Diet and lifestyle support

Anti-inflammatory foods — like turmeric, ginger, garlic, salmon, walnuts, blueberries, and strawberries — can be a reasonable part of an overall recovery routine, alongside stress-reduction techniques that support the body's healing process. These are complementary measures, not a substitute for appropriate rest, movement, and medical evaluation when needed.

When should you see a podiatrist instead of treating it at home?

Seek prompt medical attention if you notice any of the following:

  • You cannot walk or bear weight on the ankle at all
  • Signs of infection — fever, warmth, or spreading redness around the joint
  • Pain that persists beyond about a week despite consistent home care
  • A visibly deformed ankle, or pain following a hard fall or direct impact, which may indicate a fracture
  • Repeated ankle rolling or a feeling of instability, which can point to ankle instability

At-home care is a reasonable first step for mild, recent injuries, but it isn't a substitute for diagnosis. A podiatrist can determine whether you're dealing with a sprain, arthritis, tendonitis, or a fracture — and each of those calls for a different treatment plan. Common Questions

Ankle pain FAQs

What is the fastest way to relieve ankle pain at home?

For a fresh, acute injury, rest, ice for 15 to 20 minutes at a time, and elevation above heart level are the most effective first steps to limit swelling. For chronic or long-standing pain, gentle movement and stretching often help more than complete rest.

Should I use ice or heat for ankle pain?

Use ice first, especially in the initial 24 to 48 hours after an injury, to control swelling. Heat is generally more useful later, once swelling has decreased, to improve blood flow and flexibility. Applying heat too early can sometimes make fresh swelling worse.

What are the most common causes of severe ankle pain?

The most common causes are sprains (strained or torn ligaments), arthritis (long-term joint inflammation), fractures (bone breaks, often from a fall), and tendonitis (inflammation of the ankle tendons, common in athletes). Each responds to a somewhat different treatment approach.

Can diet help with ankle pain?

Anti-inflammatory foods like turmeric, ginger, garlic, salmon, walnuts, blueberries, and strawberries can be a reasonable supporting part of a recovery routine. They're a complement to appropriate rest, movement, and medical care, not a replacement for them.

How long should I try home treatment before seeing a doctor?

If pain persists beyond about a week despite consistent home care, it's time to be evaluated. Seek care immediately, rather than waiting, if you cannot bear weight on the ankle, notice signs of infection, or the ankle appears deformed after an injury.

When is ankle pain a sign of something serious?

Severe ankle pain may point to a fracture or serious ligament injury if you can't walk on it, the joint looks deformed, or pain follows a hard fall or impact. Fever, warmth, or spreading redness can indicate infection and also need prompt evaluation. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
