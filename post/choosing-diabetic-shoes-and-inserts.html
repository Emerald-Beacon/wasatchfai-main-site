Choosing Diabetic Shoes and Inserts

The right shoes and inserts are one of the quietest, most effective ways to protect a diabetic foot — but "diabetic shoe" means something specific, and the wrong pair can do harm.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated November 10, 2026

A diabetic shoe isn't a brand or a look — it's footwear built to protect a foot that can't always feel trouble coming. The best choice matches the real shape of your foot, leaves depth for a protective insert, and has a smooth, seam-free interior with nothing inside to rub or press. Paired with the right insert, good shoes keep steady pressure off the spots where diabetic wounds tend to start.

If you have diabetes, your feet face two problems at once. Nerve changes can dull the warning signals that tell you a shoe is too tight or a pebble is digging in, and slower healing means a small sore has more time to become a serious one. Footwear is where those two problems meet. Choosing shoes and inserts well is less about comfort in the store and more about what the pair does to your skin over a long day you may not be able to feel.

What makes a shoe a "diabetic shoe"?

The phrase gets stamped on a lot of products, so it helps to judge the shoe, not the label. What actually protects a diabetic foot is a short list of features you can check yourself:

  • Extra depth. A deeper toe area makes room for a protective insert and for toes that curl, overlap, or sit under a bunion without jamming them against the top of the shoe.
  • A wide, rounded toe box. Toes should spread naturally. A tapered, pointed front squeezes the sides and the tips — common pressure points for calluses and sores.
  • A seamless or soft interior lining. Run your hand inside. Hard stitching, raised logos, or rough seams become hot spots over miles of walking.
  • A firm heel counter and a stable sole. The back of the shoe should resist being crushed flat, and the sole should not twist easily. That stability steadies your stride and spreads load more evenly.
  • Adjustable closures. Laces or hook-and-loop straps let you loosen the shoe as feet swell through the day — something a slip-on can't do.

Notice what's not on the list: a particular color, a medical-looking logo, or a high price. A well-built walking or athletic shoe that checks these boxes can protect a lower-risk foot perfectly well. Purpose-built therapeutic footwear earns its place when a foot needs more room or more offloading than an off-the-shelf shoe can give.

Diabetic inserts vs. regular orthotics

Here's a distinction that trips people up: the insert that belongs in a diabetic shoe is usually not the same device sold to fix flat feet or heel pain. The two have opposite jobs.

Corrective orthotics — the kind many people think of first — are firm. They reposition the foot and resist its motion to change how weight loads through the arch and heel. That firmness is the point when you're treating a biomechanical problem. We walk through that trade-off in our comparison of custom orthotics versus store-bought inserts, and in the overview of our custom orthotics service.

Diabetic inserts are accommodative, not corrective. They're made from soft, cushioning, often multi-layer materials that mold to the foot as it already is and spread pressure away from bony points and old scar tissue. The goal isn't to straighten anything — it's to protect skin. A firm corrective orthotic under an insensitive foot can actually create a new pressure point, which is exactly why the device and the shoe should be chosen together, by someone who has examined your feet.

Do you actually need therapeutic footwear?

Not everyone with diabetes needs a prescription shoe. Someone with well-controlled blood sugar, normal sensation, and no foot deformity can often wear ordinary, well-fitted footwear and do just fine. The calculation changes when risk factors stack up. Protective, therapeutic footwear matters most if you have any of the following:

  • Loss of protective sensation — numbness or reduced feeling from neuropathy. If you can't reliably feel a shoe pinching, you've lost your early-warning system. This is the single biggest reason footwear choices stop being about comfort and start being about safety; we explain why in why diabetic neuropathy hides foot injuries.
  • A previous ulcer or amputation. Skin that has broken down once is more likely to break down again in the same place.
  • Foot deformity — bunions, hammertoes, a high arch, or the midfoot collapse of Charcot arthropathy. An unusual shape needs a shoe that accommodates it rather than forcing it into a standard last.
  • Poor circulation that slows healing.
  • Recurring calluses in one spot, which signal repeated pressure your current shoes aren't relieving.

If you're not sure which category you fall into, that's a reason to be examined, not a reason to guess. A quick sensory check and a look at your pressure points tells us whether ordinary good shoes are enough or whether your feet have moved into higher-risk territory.

Getting the fit right

Fit is where good intentions most often go wrong, because the usual advice — "buy what feels comfortable" — assumes you can feel the shoe accurately. Many diabetic feet can't. Use measurements and habits instead of relying on sensation:

  • Shop later in the day, when your feet are at their largest from swelling. A shoe that fits in the morning can be tight by afternoon.
  • Fit the bigger foot. Most people have one foot slightly larger; size to it.
  • Leave about a thumb's width between your longest toe and the end of the shoe, and make sure the widest part of your foot sits in the widest part of the shoe.
  • Bring your inserts and your everyday socks and try the shoe with both in place, since they change the fit.
  • Skip the "break-in" period. A diabetic shoe should feel right the first time. A shoe you have to break in is a shoe rubbing your skin while you wait.

After the first few wears, take the shoes off and look at your feet in good light. Red marks that linger, warm spots, or any blistering mean the fit is wrong — stop wearing that pair and have it checked.

How therapeutic shoes are prescribed and covered

Purpose-built therapeutic shoes and custom inserts are prescribed, not just purchased. For people with diabetes and qualifying foot disease, there's a defined benefit under Medicare's therapeutic shoe program: in a given year it generally covers either one pair of extra-depth shoes with inserts or, for a foot that can't wear them, one pair of custom-molded shoes, along with additional pairs of inserts. The process has a few required steps — the physician managing your diabetes certifies that you need the footwear, a podiatrist or other qualified doctor writes the order, and the shoes come from an enrolled supplier.

Coverage details and out-of-pocket costs depend entirely on your specific plan, so we won't quote figures here. For what applies to you, see our insurance and payment page or call the office. Our diabetic foot care team can also tell you, after an exam, whether you meet the criteria and handle the certification and prescription as part of your visit.

Building a footwear rotation that protects you

One perfect pair isn't a plan. The feet that stay healthiest usually have a small system around them. Keep a dedicated pair of supportive shoes or slippers for indoors so you're never walking barefoot at home, even on carpet — an unfelt step on a dropped tack is a classic way a diabetic injury starts. Rotate between two pairs if you can, so each one fully dries out between wears and the cushioning recovers. Match the shoe to the activity rather than wearing one pair for everything, and retire the beloved worn-out pair instead of hanging on to it.

Socks are part of the shoe, not an afterthought. Choose seamless, moisture-wicking socks without tight elastic bands, change them daily or sooner if they're damp, and consider light-colored socks that make any drainage easy to spot. Together, the shoe, the insert, and the sock are the barrier between your skin and the ground.

When to replace them — and warning signs to watch for

Protective footwear wears out from the inside. Accommodative inserts compress over months of use and quietly lose the cushioning that offloads pressure, long before the outside of the shoe looks worn. Watch for flattened insert padding, a sole that's worn unevenly, a heel counter that no longer holds its shape, or new rub marks on your feet — any of these means it's time to replace the pair, not push it further.

Some foot changes can't wait for a scheduled replacement. Call the clinic promptly if you notice a new blister, a callus building in a pressure spot, a red or warm area that doesn't fade, or any break in the skin. And treat these as urgent — call us the same day or seek urgent care — if you have a wound that isn't starting to heal, spreading redness, drainage or a foul odor, a foot that is hot and swollen, or a fever alongside any foot problem. In a diabetic foot, "wait and see" is the one approach that reliably makes things worse. If you catch a problem early, it's often as simple as adjusting the footwear; our guide to diabetic foot ulcers explains why speed matters so much once skin breaks down. When in doubt, request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices and let us take a look.

Common Questions

Diabetic footwear FAQs

What's the difference between diabetic shoes and regular comfortable shoes?

A diabetic shoe is defined by its features, not its label: extra depth to hold a protective insert and accommodate toe deformities, a wide rounded toe box, a smooth seamless interior, a firm heel counter, and adjustable closures. A well-built regular walking shoe with those same features can protect a lower-risk foot, while a purpose-built therapeutic shoe is for feet that need more room or offloading than off-the-shelf options provide.

Can I use over-the-counter inserts instead of custom diabetic inserts?

Sometimes, if your feet are low-risk and have normal sensation and no deformity. But diabetic inserts are meant to be accommodative — soft and cushioning to spread pressure — rather than the firm corrective type sold for arch or heel problems. A firm insert under an insensitive foot can create a new pressure point, so it's worth having a podiatrist confirm which type is right for you before relying on a store-bought one.

Does insurance or Medicare help pay for diabetic shoes?

There is a defined Medicare therapeutic shoe benefit for people with diabetes and qualifying foot disease, which generally covers a pair of therapeutic shoes plus inserts each year when a physician certifies the need and a qualified doctor prescribes them. Coverage and any out-of-pocket amount depend on your specific plan, so check our insurance and payment page or call the office for what applies to you.

How do I know what size diabetic shoe to buy?

Measure rather than relying on how the shoe feels, since neuropathy can mask a poor fit. Shop later in the day when your feet are largest, fit the larger foot, leave about a thumb's width at the toe, and make sure the widest part of your foot sits in the widest part of the shoe. Try the shoe with your inserts and usual socks in place, and don't count on breaking it in.

How often should I replace diabetic shoes and inserts?

Inserts lose their cushioning from the inside over months of wear, often before the shoe looks worn out, so replace them once the padding flattens. Replace shoes when the sole wears unevenly, the heel counter stops holding its shape, or you notice new rub marks on your feet. Rotating two pairs so each dries fully between wears helps both last.

Can I wear diabetic shoes if I don't have neuropathy yet?

Yes — protective, well-fitted footwear is reasonable for anyone with diabetes, and it's a good habit to build early. Formal therapeutic footwear becomes more important once risk factors appear, such as reduced sensation, a prior ulcer, a foot deformity, or poor circulation. A foot exam is the best way to tell which level of protection your feet need right now.
