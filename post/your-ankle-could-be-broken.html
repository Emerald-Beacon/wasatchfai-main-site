Your Ankle Could Be Broken: Signs of a Hairline Fracture

Not every broken bone looks dramatic. Hairline, or stress, fractures can hide behind "just a sprain" — here's how to tell the difference.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

A hairline fracture is a very small crack in a bone of the foot or ankle — smaller and often less obvious than a typical break. Because the pain can be mild and swelling minimal, many people mistake a hairline fracture for a sprain and keep walking on it, which can make the injury worse. If you have persistent ankle or foot pain after a repetitive activity, it is worth having it checked rather than assuming it will resolve on its own.

Hairline fractures — also called stress fractures — are one of the more commonly overlooked injuries we see in Farmington and South Ogden. Unlike a sudden, high-impact break, they develop gradually, which is exactly why they are so easy to shrug off. Here is what to watch for, why they happen, and how we treat them.

What is a hairline fracture?

A hairline fracture is a thin, small crack in the bone rather than a complete break. It most often develops in the bones of the foot and ankle because of repeated stress rather than a single traumatic event. Because the bone is not fully broken, X-rays taken too soon after the injury can sometimes miss it, which is one reason ongoing pain deserves a closer look even if initial imaging looks clear.

What are the symptoms of a hairline fracture?

Symptoms can be subtle, especially early on. Some people notice only a mild, nagging ache, while others have more classic signs. Common symptoms include:

  • Pain that develops gradually and worsens with activity
  • Swelling around the top or side of the foot or ankle
  • Bruising in some, but not all, cases
  • Tenderness to the touch over a specific spot on the bone
  • Pain that eases with rest but returns as soon as you're active again

Because some hairline fractures cause only slight discomfort with no visible swelling or bruising, they can be difficult to self-diagnose. If pain lingers for more than a few days despite rest, it is best to have it evaluated.

What causes hairline fractures, and who is at risk?

The majority of hairline fractures are caused by repetitive motion rather than a single injury. Repeated impact gradually wears down the bone faster than it can rebuild itself, eventually creating a small crack. Common contributors include:

  • Running or other high-impact sports, especially with a sudden increase in mileage or intensity
  • Not allowing enough recovery time between workouts
  • Switching to a new sport or activity without proper conditioning
  • Worn-out or unsupportive footwear
  • Lower bone density, which can make bones more susceptible to stress injuries

Runners and other athletes who train frequently are at the highest risk, but anyone who repeats the same motion — walking, marching, or standing on hard surfaces for long stretches — can develop a hairline fracture over time.

How are hairline fractures treated?

Treatment depends on the location and severity of the fracture, as well as how much stress the affected bone continues to bear.

Conservative care

  • Rest and activity modification to take stress off the healing bone
  • Ice and elevation to help control swelling
  • A protective boot or brace to limit motion while the bone heals
  • Crutches for a period of time if weight-bearing needs to be limited
  • Gradually returning to activity with a plan that prevents re-injury

When surgery may be needed

Most hairline fractures heal with conservative care, but some — particularly in bones that carry a lot of weight or have a poor blood supply — may require surgical stabilization to heal properly and prevent the fracture from progressing to a complete break. Our physicians will determine the right approach based on your imaging and activity goals.

Why you shouldn't ignore ankle or foot pain

Continuing to put weight on a hairline fracture can turn a small crack into a complete fracture, which typically means a longer recovery and, in some cases, surgery that could otherwise have been avoided. Varying your activities, allowing adequate recovery time, and listening to persistent pain are the best ways to protect your bones.

If you have foot or ankle pain that doesn't improve with a few days of rest, or that flares up every time you return to activity, it's worth getting evaluated. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Hairline fracture FAQs

How do I know if my ankle pain is a hairline fracture or just a sprain?

Sprains and hairline fractures can feel similar, which is part of what makes them tricky to tell apart without imaging. A hairline fracture often causes pain that gradually worsens with activity and is tender over a specific point on the bone, while sprains tend to involve more diffuse pain around a joint. If pain persists beyond a few days of rest, it's best to have it evaluated.

Can a hairline fracture heal on its own?

Many hairline fractures do heal with rest, activity modification, and protective support like a boot or brace. However, continuing to bear weight on the injury can prevent proper healing and turn a small crack into a complete break, so it's important to reduce stress on the bone during recovery.

How long does a hairline fracture take to heal?

Healing time varies with the location and severity of the fracture as well as how well activity is limited during recovery. Many stress fractures improve over several weeks of protected rest, though some locations take longer and require a more gradual return to activity.

Will an X-ray always show a hairline fracture?

Not always. Because hairline fractures are small, they can be difficult to see on X-rays taken soon after the injury. If your pain persists despite a normal initial X-ray, your podiatrist may recommend follow-up imaging or additional evaluation.

Do hairline fractures ever need surgery?

Most heal with conservative care such as rest, bracing, or crutches. Surgery is sometimes needed for fractures in weight-bearing bones or areas with limited blood supply, where surgical stabilization helps the bone heal properly and lowers the risk of the crack progressing to a full break.

When should I see a podiatrist for a suspected hairline fracture?

See a podiatrist if you have foot or ankle pain that doesn't improve after a few days of rest, worsens with activity, or is accompanied by swelling or tenderness over a specific spot. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
