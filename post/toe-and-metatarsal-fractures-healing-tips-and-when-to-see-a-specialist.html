Toe and Metatarsal Fractures: Healing Tips and When to See a Specialist

A stubbed toe or a sudden jolt of pain in your foot can mean more than a bruise. Here's how to tell, what helps a fracture heal, and when it's time to see a podiatrist.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Toe and metatarsal fractures are among the most common foot injuries we see — the foot has 26 bones, and any one of them can crack from a direct blow, a twist, or the repetitive stress of running and walking. Most heal well with the right care, but treating a fracture like "just a bruise" can slow healing and, in some cases, lead to lasting problems.

Whether you dropped something on your foot, rolled it awkwardly, or have noticed a nagging ache that won't go away after weeks of training, it helps to understand what kind of fracture you might be dealing with, what you can safely do at home, and when it's time to get it looked at.

What happens when you fracture a toe or metatarsal?

A fracture is simply a break in the bone, but not all fractures behave the same way. Podiatrists generally group them into a few categories:

  • Acute fractures happen suddenly, from a direct impact like stubbing a toe or dropping something heavy on your foot
  • Stress fractures develop gradually from repetitive pressure — common in runners, dancers, and anyone who suddenly increases their activity level
  • Displaced fractures occur when the broken bone shifts out of alignment
  • Open fractures break through the skin and require urgent care to prevent infection

The toe and metatarsal bones are especially vulnerable because they absorb impact with nearly every step, and the fifth metatarsal — along the outside edge of the foot — has its own well-known fracture patterns; read more about those on our fifth metatarsal fractures page.

How do you know if it's actually broken?

It isn't always obvious. A sprain and a fracture can feel similar at first, which is why imaging matters. Signs that point toward a fracture include:

  • Pain that gets worse when you put weight on the foot
  • Swelling and bruising around the injured area
  • Visible deformity or a toe that looks out of place
  • Numbness or tingling in the toes
  • Pain that lingers or worsens over days rather than improving

Stress fractures are trickier — the pain often builds gradually during activity and eases with rest, which makes it easy to dismiss until the bone is more seriously injured.

What can you do at home right after an injury?

If you suspect a fracture, start with the RICE method while you arrange to be seen:

  • Rest the foot and stay off it as much as possible
  • Ice the area for 15 to 20 minutes at a time to control swelling
  • Compression with a gentle wrap can help limit swelling
  • Elevation above heart level reduces throbbing and swelling

Home care can calm symptoms, but it cannot confirm what's actually broken or how displaced the bone is — that's where professional evaluation comes in.

Why professional care matters

An X-ray (and occasionally an MRI for a suspected stress fracture that isn't yet visible on X-ray) lets us confirm the fracture, see how the bone is aligned, and choose the right treatment. Depending on the type and location of the break, treatment may include:

  • A stiff-soled shoe, walking boot, or cast to protect the bone while it heals
  • Buddy-taping a broken toe to the one next to it for support
  • Custom orthotics to offload pressure during and after healing
  • Surgery for fractures that are significantly displaced, unstable, or involve a joint

Most uncomplicated toe and metatarsal fractures heal in about 6 to 8 weeks, though more severe or displaced fractures can take longer. Following your treatment plan closely — including how much weight you're allowed to put on the foot — has a real impact on how well and how quickly it heals.

Supporting recovery through everyday choices

Bone healing depends on more than immobilization. Getting enough calcium, vitamin D, magnesium, and vitamin K in your diet supports the bone-repair process, and avoiding tobacco use helps too, since smoking slows bone healing. Gentle, cleared movement and a gradual return to activity — guided by your podiatrist — help you regain strength without re-injuring the area.

The risks of ignoring a fracture

A fracture that goes untreated or heals in a poor position can lead to chronic pain, early arthritis in the joint, and changes to how you walk. That altered gait can, in turn, put extra strain on the knees, hips, and back on the same side of the body. What starts as a small, missed fracture can become a much bigger problem months or years later.

If you have foot pain after an injury that isn't improving with rest, swelling or bruising that seems out of proportion to the injury, or any visible deformity, it's worth having it evaluated. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Toe & metatarsal fracture FAQs

How can I tell if my toe is broken or just sprained?

Sprains and fractures can feel similar, which is why an X-ray is often needed to tell them apart. Pain that worsens with weight-bearing, significant swelling or bruising, visible deformity, or numbness are all signs that point toward a fracture rather than a simple sprain.

How long does a metatarsal fracture take to heal?

Most uncomplicated toe and metatarsal fractures heal in about 6 to 8 weeks. More severe, displaced, or surgically treated fractures can take longer. Following your podiatrist's weight-bearing and activity instructions closely supports faster, more complete healing.

Can I still walk on a broken toe?

It depends on the fracture. Some minor toe fractures can be treated with a stiff-soled shoe and limited weight-bearing, while others need a walking boot or cast to protect the bone. Walking on a fracture without the right support can delay healing or cause the bone to shift.

Do all fractures require surgery?

No. Most toe and metatarsal fractures heal with nonsurgical care such as a boot, cast, or buddy-taping. Surgery is generally reserved for fractures that are significantly displaced, unstable, or involve a joint surface.

What happens if a fracture isn't treated?

An untreated or poorly healed fracture can lead to chronic pain, early arthritis in the affected joint, and a change in the way you walk. That altered gait can place extra stress on the knees, hips, and back over time, so it's best to have persistent foot pain evaluated.

When should I see a podiatrist for a foot injury?

See a podiatrist if you have foot pain after an injury that isn't improving with rest, swelling or bruising that seems severe, visible deformity, or numbness and tingling. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
