Diabetic Foot Ulcers: Stages, Treatment, and Why Speed Matters

An open sore on a diabetic foot is not a wound to watch and wait on. How deep it goes decides the treatment, and how fast you act decides a lot of what happens next.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated October 29, 2026

A diabetic foot ulcer is an open sore — most often on the bottom of the foot or over a bony pressure point — that forms when nerve damage and reduced circulation let a small injury go unnoticed and fail to close on its own. Ulcers are staged by how deep the wound goes and whether infection or poor blood flow is involved, and that depth is exactly what determines the treatment plan. Speed matters because an ulcer that is cleaned, offloaded, and protected early has a real chance to heal, while one left open becomes a route for infection that can reach the bone.

If you or someone you care for has diabetes and has noticed a sore, a callus that opened up, or a spot of drainage on a sock, this is the article for the stage that comes after prevention. We cover what an ulcer is, how it is graded, what treatment looks like, and why the calendar is not on your side once the skin is broken.

What a diabetic foot ulcer actually is

An ulcer is a break in the skin that will not heal through the normal process. In a diabetic foot, three problems usually line up to create one. The first is neuropathy — nerve damage that dulls or erases the sensation of pain, so a blister, a stone in the shoe, or a seam rubbing all day never registers. The second is reduced circulation, which starves the tissue of the oxygen and immune cells it needs to repair itself and fend off bacteria. The third is pressure: high blood sugar over time stiffens tissue and can reshape the foot, concentrating force on spots that were never built to carry it.

Put those together and a minor injury that most people would feel, adjust, and shrug off instead goes unnoticed, sits under constant pressure, and lacks the blood supply to close. That is why ulcers tend to appear under the ball of the foot, on the heel, at the tips of toes, or anywhere a deformity rubs against a shoe. Understanding the mechanics behind them is the foundation of good diabetic foot care, and it is also why these wounds behave so differently from an ordinary scrape.

How doctors stage a foot ulcer

Not all ulcers are equal, and the first thing a podiatrist does is figure out which one is in front of them. Staging systems grade an ulcer along a few lines: how deep it extends, whether infection is present, and whether blood flow to the area is adequate. The widely used Wagner system runs from a foot that is intact but at high risk, through a shallow sore that involves only the skin, to a wound that reaches tendon or bone, and on to tissue that has started to die from lack of circulation. Other systems, such as the University of Texas classification, layer infection and poor blood flow onto that depth so the full picture is captured.

The grade is not paperwork. A shallow ulcer with good circulation and no infection may heal with offloading and attentive wound care. A deeper wound that tracks toward bone, or one in a foot with weak blood flow, is a different situation that calls for imaging, possibly antibiotics, and sometimes a vascular evaluation. Two sores that look similar on the surface can need very different plans, which is the whole reason an in-person exam beats guessing from a photo.

Why speed matters once the skin is broken

An open wound on a foot with diabetes is a clock. Bacteria that would be a nuisance on healthy skin can spread quickly through tissue that has little blood supply to defend it, and an infection that reaches bone — osteomyelitis — is far harder to treat than one caught in the soft tissue. The longer an ulcer stays open and under pressure, the deeper it tends to go.

The stakes are real. In a peer-reviewed review of diabetic foot disease, researchers note that up to 25% of people with diabetes develop a foot ulcer in their lifetime, and these ulcers precede roughly 85% of non-traumatic lower-limb amputations. That second figure is the one worth sitting with: the ulcer is usually the step that comes before the worst outcomes, which means it is also the step where prompt care changes the trajectory. Getting a wound evaluated within days rather than weeks is one of the most protective things a person with diabetes can do, and it is why our team treats a new ulcer as a reason to be seen soon, not at the next routine visit.

How diabetic foot ulcers are treated

Treatment is built around the same factors that cause the ulcer, and it usually combines several pieces at once:

  • Debridement. A clinician removes dead tissue and the thick callus that often rings an ulcer. Clearing that away reduces the bacterial load and lets healthy tissue at the edges start to close the gap.
  • Offloading. Taking pressure off the wound is one of the most important steps and one of the hardest to do on your own. Depending on the ulcer, that can mean a total contact cast, a removable walking boot, specialized footwear, or padding and inserts that redistribute weight away from the sore.
  • Wound care and dressings. The goal is a clean wound kept at the right moisture balance — not too dry, not soaked — with dressings changed on a schedule. The right dressing depends on how much the wound is draining and what stage it is in.
  • Infection control. An infected ulcer may need antibiotics, and a deep or bone-involved infection sometimes needs surgery to clear it. Imaging helps determine how far an infection has traveled.
  • Blood flow. If circulation is poor, no wound can heal well. A vascular assessment can show whether blood flow needs to be improved before the ulcer will respond, and in advanced cases that work becomes part of a broader limb salvage effort to preserve the foot.
  • Blood sugar management. Keeping glucose in a healthy range supports every other part of healing and is handled alongside your primary care provider or endocrinologist.

Which of these a given person needs depends entirely on the stage of the ulcer and the health of the foot around it. The plan is tailored at the exam and adjusted as the wound responds.

What healing looks like, and what slows it down

Diabetic foot ulcers heal on a timeline measured in weeks to months rather than days, and the pace varies a great deal from one patient to the next. No honest provider can promise an exact recovery date; your own healing depends on the depth of the wound, your circulation, your blood sugar control, and how consistently pressure stays off the area, and the timeline is something your surgeon confirms as the wound progresses.

Several things reliably slow healing: blood sugar that stays high, continued walking on the wound without offloading, smoking, poor circulation, and infection that is not fully cleared. The flip side is encouraging — addressing those same factors is often what turns a stalled ulcer around. Keeping every follow-up appointment matters here, because a wound that looks stable on Monday can change by Friday, and small adjustments made early keep a healing ulcer on track.

When an ulcer is an emergency

Some changes mean you should be seen right away rather than waiting for a scheduled visit. Call the clinic or seek urgent care if you notice:

  • Redness or warmth spreading out from the wound
  • Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell
  • Foul-smelling drainage, pus, or a sudden increase in fluid
  • Black or darkening tissue around the sore
  • A sudden change in pain, numbness, or the color or temperature of the foot
  • A wound that is getting larger or simply not healing

These can signal a spreading infection or a circulation problem, and both are far easier to manage the sooner they are addressed. With a diabetic foot, "wait and see" is never the right response to a wound that is changing for the worse.

Preventing the next ulcer

Healing one ulcer is only half the job, because a foot that has ulcerated once is more likely to do it again. Lowering that risk comes down to the fundamentals: daily foot checks, shoes and inserts that protect pressure points, prompt attention to calluses and blisters, and regular podiatry visits so problems are caught while they are still small. We walk through the day-to-day routine in our guide to diabetic foot care prevention tips and strategies, and we cover the warning signs that should prompt a call in early signs of diabetic foot problems and what to do. For people whose feet are changing shape — a process that dramatically raises ulcer risk — our overview of Charcot arthropathy explains why structural changes deserve close attention.

Common Questions

Diabetic foot ulcer FAQs

What do the stages of a diabetic foot ulcer mean?

Staging describes how serious an ulcer is based on how deep it goes, whether infection is present, and whether blood flow to the area is adequate. A shallow sore that involves only the skin is an earlier stage than a wound that reaches tendon or bone, and the grade guides how aggressively it needs to be treated.

Can a diabetic foot ulcer heal on its own?

It is very unlikely. The nerve damage, reduced circulation, and constant pressure that create an ulcer are the same things that keep it from closing without help. Ulcers that are debrided, offloaded, and protected have a real chance to heal, which is why professional wound care matters rather than hoping it resolves.

What does "offloading" a foot ulcer mean?

Offloading means taking pressure off the wound so it can heal. Depending on the ulcer, that can involve a total contact cast, a removable walking boot, specialized footwear, or padding and inserts that shift weight away from the sore. It is one of the most important parts of treatment and one of the hardest to manage without guidance.

How long does a diabetic foot ulcer take to heal?

Healing is usually measured in weeks to months and varies widely from person to person. The depth of the wound, your circulation, your blood sugar control, and how consistently pressure stays off the area all affect the pace. Your surgeon confirms the timeline as the wound responds, so there is no single number that fits every patient.

Does a foot ulcer always lead to amputation?

No. Many ulcers heal when they are treated promptly and properly. The reason speed matters is that ulcers left open can become infected and reach bone, and research links foot ulcers to the majority of diabetes-related lower-limb amputations. Early, consistent care is what keeps a wound from heading in that direction.

How do I keep a healed ulcer from coming back?

A foot that has ulcerated once is at higher risk of doing so again, so prevention continues after the wound closes. Daily foot checks, protective footwear and inserts, prompt attention to calluses and blisters, and regular podiatry visits all help. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office to build a plan.
