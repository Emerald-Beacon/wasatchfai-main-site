Preventing Ankle Instability: Lifestyle Changes & Treatments to Consider

Repeat sprains and a wobbly, "giving way" feeling in the ankle are common — and often preventable. Here's what actually helps build lasting stability.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Ankle instability is a chronic weakness or looseness in the ankle joint that leaves it prone to giving way or re-spraining, often after an earlier ankle sprain never fully healed. The good news is that most cases can be improved — and future sprains prevented — with targeted strengthening, supportive footwear, and, when needed, bracing or physical therapy.

If your ankle rolls easily, feels unsteady on uneven ground, or you have sprained the same ankle more than once, you are dealing with a very common problem. Once an ankle sprain occurs, the ligaments and the body's sense of joint position (proprioception) can stay weakened even after the initial pain fades, setting the stage for repeat injuries. Below, our podiatrists cover what causes ankle instability, the lifestyle changes that make the biggest difference, and when it's time to consider additional treatment.

What makes an ankle stable in the first place?

Ankle stability depends on a group of ligaments — including the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), calcaneofibular ligament (CFL), and posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL) — that hold the ankle joint in proper alignment, along with proprioception, your body's built-in sense of where your foot and ankle are in space. When either the ligaments or the proprioceptive signaling is compromised, the ankle becomes more prone to rolling and re-injury.

What causes ankle instability?

The most common cause is a prior ankle sprain that did not fully heal or was not adequately rehabilitated. Ligaments that heal in a stretched or lax position provide less support going forward, and repeated sprains are a well-recognized risk factor for developing chronic instability — many people who sprain an ankle once go on to sprain the same ankle again within the following months if the underlying weakness isn't addressed. Other contributing factors include:

  • Weak or deconditioned muscles around the ankle
  • Reduced proprioception following injury
  • Unsupportive footwear, including worn-out shoes or those without a firm heel counter
  • Participation in sports with frequent cutting, jumping, or uneven-surface movement
  • Naturally loose ligaments or a prior history of multiple sprains

Lifestyle changes that help strengthen your ankles

For most people with mild to moderate instability, consistent daily habits make a real difference over time.

  • Supportive footwear with a firm heel counter and good arch support to help control excess motion
  • Balance training, such as single-leg stands, to rebuild proprioception
  • Strength training for the muscles around the ankle and lower leg, ideally several times a week
  • Low-impact cross-training — swimming, cycling, or yoga — to stay active while reducing repetitive impact on a healing ankle
  • Anti-inflammatory nutrition, including foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids and vitamins C and D, to support overall joint health
  • Custom orthotics to help redistribute pressure and improve alignment — see our custom orthotics options

When should you seek professional help?

Occasional mild wobbliness after activity is common, but you should not ignore an ankle that gives way repeatedly, remains swollen or painful between episodes, or limits your ability to exercise or work. Persistent instability increases your risk of cartilage damage and further ligament injury over time, so earlier evaluation typically means simpler treatment.

Treatment options for persistent ankle instability

When lifestyle changes alone aren't enough, additional treatment can restore stability and function.

Nonsurgical treatment

  • Ankle bracing to provide external support during activity
  • Structured physical therapy focused on strength, balance, and proprioception
  • Activity modification while the ankle rebuilds strength

Advanced and surgical options

For chronic instability that does not respond to conservative treatment, ankle arthroscopy allows a surgeon to evaluate and address damage inside the joint with a minimally invasive approach. In select cases, ligament reconstruction may be recommended to restore lasting stability. Emerging regenerative options, such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and stem cell injections, are also being used in some cases to support ligament healing.

Building a long-term recovery mindset

Ankle instability tends to respond best to a consistent, active approach rather than a one-time fix. Sticking with balance and strength exercises even after symptoms improve helps prevent the cycle of repeat sprains. If you've had more than one ankle sprain or notice your ankle giving way during everyday activities, our team can evaluate your ankle and build a plan suited to your activity level and goals. Common Questions

Ankle instability FAQs

What exercises help with ankle instability?

Balance work like single-leg stands, along with strength training for the muscles surrounding the ankle and lower leg, are among the most effective habits for rebuilding stability. A physical therapist or podiatrist can tailor a routine to your specific weaknesses and activity level.

Does nutrition really affect joint health?

Nutrition supports — but doesn't replace — strengthening and bracing. Anti-inflammatory foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids and vitamins C and D can help support overall joint health as part of a broader plan that includes exercise and, when needed, professional treatment.

Can physical therapy fix chronic ankle instability?

Physical therapy is often very effective, particularly when it targets strength, balance, and proprioception together. Many patients see meaningful improvement in stability and a reduced rate of re-injury with a consistent, guided program.

Is ankle bracing a long-term solution?

Bracing can be a helpful long-term tool for certain activities, especially sports with cutting or jumping, but it works best alongside — not instead of — strengthening and balance training. Your podiatrist can help you decide how much and how long to brace based on your specific instability.

When does ankle instability require surgery?

Surgery, such as ankle arthroscopy or ligament reconstruction, is generally considered only after conservative treatment — bracing, physical therapy, and activity modification — has not resolved persistent instability, or when there is significant damage inside the joint.

When should I see a podiatrist for ankle instability?

See a podiatrist if your ankle repeatedly gives way, stays swollen or painful between episodes, or limits your activity. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
