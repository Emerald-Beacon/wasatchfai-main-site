Ingrown Nail Care Guide: Daily Habits, Treatments & Prevention

Ingrown toenails are one of the most common — and most preventable — reasons people end up in a podiatrist's chair. Here's how to care for one at home, and how to keep it from coming back.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

An ingrown toenail happens when the edge or corner of a nail — almost always on the big toe — grows into the surrounding skin instead of over it, causing pain, redness, and swelling. Mild cases often respond well to careful at-home care, but nails that become infected or keep recurring need a podiatrist's attention. Below, our team walks through daily habits that prevent ingrown nails, how to manage a mild one at home, and the warning signs that mean it's time to come in.

What causes an ingrown toenail?

Ingrown nails have a few common causes, and they often overlap:

  • Improper trimming — cutting nails too short or rounding the corners instead of trimming straight across
  • Tight or narrow footwear that crowds the toes and presses the nail into the skin
  • Trauma to the toe, such as stubbing it or dropping something on it
  • Nail shape or thickness — naturally curved nails are more prone to growing inward
  • Sweaty feet or poor foot hygiene, which softens the skin around the nail

Older adults may face additional challenges, including thicker nails and reduced flexibility that make trimming and daily foot checks harder to manage independently. Younger, active people more often run into trouble from improper trimming or tight athletic shoes.

Daily habits that prevent ingrown toenails

Most ingrown nails are preventable with a consistent care routine. Our podiatrists recommend building these habits into your day:

  • Check your feet daily. Use a mirror if needed to look for redness, swelling, or changes in your nails, especially if you have diabetes or reduced sensation in your feet.
  • Wash and dry thoroughly. Clean your feet with mild soap in lukewarm water, and dry completely — especially between the toes — to keep skin from softening and breaking down.
  • Trim nails straight across. Avoid rounding the corners or cutting too short, which encourages the nail to grow into the skin.
  • Choose shoes with adequate toe room. Tight, narrow, or pointed shoes put constant pressure on the nail.
  • Wear cushioned, moisture-wicking socks and avoid walking barefoot in places where your toes can get stubbed or injured.

Nutrition also plays a supporting role — a diet rich in lean proteins, whole grains, fruits, and vegetables, along with adequate calcium, vitamin D, and biotin, supports the immune system and tissue repair that keep skin and nails healthy.

How to care for a mild ingrown toenail at home

If the toe is only mildly red or tender, with no drainage or spreading infection, home care can often help:

  • Soak the foot in warm water for 15–20 minutes, two to three times a day, to soften the skin and ease discomfort
  • After soaking, gently lift the edge of the nail away from the skin — never dig into the corner or attempt "bathroom surgery" with sharp tools
  • Keep the area clean and covered with a bandage if needed
  • Wear roomy, open-toed shoes or sandals while the toe heals
  • Take an over-the-counter pain reliever if needed for discomfort

If the toe hasn't improved within a few days of consistent home care, it's time to have it looked at rather than continuing to manage it on your own.

When should you see a podiatrist?

Some ingrown nails need professional treatment rather than home care. See a podiatrist promptly if you notice:

  • Redness that is spreading beyond the nail fold
  • Pus or drainage from the area
  • A foul odor coming from the toe
  • Persistent, sharp, or worsening pain
  • Fever, or red streaking up the foot — signs of a more serious infection

People with diabetes or poor circulation should never try to manage an ingrown nail at home and should be seen promptly, since even minor foot injuries can become serious more quickly. For nails that keep recurring, a simple in-office procedure can permanently narrow the nail border and prevent future ingrown nails. We recommend at least an annual podiatry visit for routine nail and foot care, with more frequent visits if you have recurring issues.

Common Questions

Ingrown toenail FAQs

How do I trim my nails to avoid ingrown toenails?

Trim nails straight across rather than rounding the corners, and avoid cutting them too short. The nail edge should be even with or slightly beyond the tip of the toe, so it can't dig into the surrounding skin as it grows.

Can I treat an ingrown toenail at home?

Mild cases with no signs of infection often respond to warm soaks two to three times a day, gently lifting the nail edge away from the skin, and wearing roomy shoes. Never dig into the corner of the nail with sharp tools. If it isn't improving within a few days, see a podiatrist.

What are the warning signs of an infected ingrown toenail?

Watch for redness that is spreading, pus or drainage, a foul odor, persistent or worsening pain, or fever. These signs mean it's time to see a podiatrist rather than continue home care.

Why do ingrown toenails keep coming back?

Recurring ingrown nails are often related to naturally curved or thick nail shape, tight footwear, or trimming habits. For nails that keep recurring, a simple in-office procedure can permanently narrow the nail border to prevent future episodes.

Are people with diabetes at higher risk from ingrown toenails?

Yes. People with diabetes or poor circulation should not try to manage an ingrown toenail at home, since minor foot issues can progress to serious infections more quickly. Prompt evaluation by a podiatrist is important.

How often should I see a podiatrist for nail care?

We recommend at least one podiatry visit per year for routine nail and foot care, with more frequent visits if you have recurring ingrown nails or other foot concerns. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
