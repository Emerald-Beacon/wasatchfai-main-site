Charcot Foot: What People With Diabetes Should Know

A foot that suddenly turns warm, swollen, and red — often without much pain — can be an early sign of Charcot foot. Recognizing it quickly is what protects the shape of the foot.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated November 5, 2026

Charcot foot is a condition in which the bones and joints of the foot weaken, fracture, and shift out of position — frequently without the person feeling it — because diabetic nerve damage has silenced the pain that would normally stop someone from walking on an injured foot. It usually starts as a foot that becomes warm, swollen, and red over a few days, and because it often doesn't hurt the way a broken bone should, it is easy to mistake for an infection or a sprain. Caught early and kept off weight, the foot has a real chance to settle before it changes shape. Walked on, the arch can collapse.

If you have diabetes with any numbness in your feet, and one foot has recently become puffy and hot, this is the article for you. We walk through what Charcot foot actually is, why diabetes is the usual setup, the warning sign clinicians see misread again and again, how the condition moves through stages, and what treatment involves. The goal is to help you recognize it early, because with Charcot foot the calendar matters.

What is Charcot foot?

Charcot foot — physicians also call it Charcot neuroarthropathy — is a breakdown of the bones and joints in the foot and ankle. The name sounds technical, but the idea underneath it is straightforward: the internal scaffolding of the foot loses its strength and begins to give way. Tiny fractures form, ligaments stretch and loosen, and the joints that normally lock the midfoot into a firm arch start to slip. Across a few weeks, a foot that looked perfectly ordinary can flatten, broaden, or tilt to one side.

What sets it apart from a simple broken bone is that the person usually keeps walking on it. Each step drives the weakened bones further out of alignment, and because the warning signal of pain is muffled or gone, there is nothing telling the body to stop. In its more advanced form the arch can sink downward into what clinicians call a rocker-bottom foot, where the middle of the sole bulges out where the arch used to curve up. That bulge then becomes a pressure point, which is one of the main reasons Charcot foot and diabetic foot problems so often travel together.

Why diabetes sets the stage

Charcot foot is overwhelmingly a complication of diabetes, and the reason comes down to the nerves. Years of elevated blood sugar can damage the nerves in the feet, a process called peripheral neuropathy. Two consequences of that damage line up to create the problem. The first is loss of protective sensation: the foot stops reliably feeling the pain, pressure, and heat that would normally make a person favor an injured limb. The second is a change in how the body regulates blood flow and bone turnover in the foot, which can leave the bones more fragile than they look.

Add continued weight-bearing to fragile bones that send no pain signal, and a minor event — a long day on the feet, a small stumble, even recovery from an earlier foot surgery — can quietly set off a cascade of fractures. We go deeper into how numbness conceals this kind of damage in our explainer on why diabetic neuropathy hides foot injuries. Charcot foot is essentially the bone-and-joint version of that same blind spot: the injury is real, but the alarm never sounds.

The warning sign that gets mistaken for something else

The opening act of Charcot foot almost always looks the same. One foot becomes swollen, red, and warm to the touch, often noticeably warmer than the other foot when you rest a hand on each. There may be no injury you can point to, or only something minor you'd ordinarily forget. And here is the part that trips people up: it may ache only a little, or not at all.

That specific combination — hot, swollen, and red, yet not especially painful — is exactly why Charcot foot is so often read as something else. It can resemble a skin infection (cellulitis), a gout flare, a blood clot, or a run-of-the-mill sprain. A few features help point toward Charcot rather than infection: there is usually no open wound, no fever, and no feeling of being generally unwell, and the swelling often eases somewhat when the foot is propped up. None of those clues is proof, though, which is why the foot needs to be examined rather than guessed at.

The practical takeaway is simple and worth remembering: if you have diabetic neuropathy and a foot turns hot and swollen over a short time, treat it as a reason to be seen within a day or two — not something to watch for a week. If that same foot also develops spreading redness, fever, or a wound that is draining, that is a medical emergency; call the clinic or seek urgent care right away. With a numb foot, "wait and see" is the response that most often leads to a lasting deformity.

How Charcot foot progresses

Doctors tend to describe Charcot foot as moving through phases, and understanding them helps explain why treatment is set up the way it is. The active, or acute, phase is the hot and swollen stage, when the bone is actively fragmenting and the foot is at its most vulnerable to deforming. This is the window where protecting the foot does the most good.

As the inflammation settles, the foot enters a healing phase in which the body begins to knit the loosened bones back together. Over additional months the foot reaches a remodeling phase, where the bone consolidates into whatever shape it has arrived at, the warmth fades, and the swelling resolves. The entire point of treatment is to carry the foot through that active phase in the best possible alignment, so that when the bone finally hardens, it hardens into a foot that can still bear weight without breaking down. How long each phase lasts varies a great deal from one person to the next, and that timeline is something your surgeon tracks over repeated visits rather than something anyone can promise at the outset.

How we diagnose it

Diagnosis starts with the story and the exam. We compare the two feet side by side, including their temperature, look at the pattern of swelling and any change in the foot's shape, and ask about your history of diabetes and nerve symptoms. Because the earliest changes can hide, imaging is a key part of the picture. X-rays are usually the first study, but in the early days they can look deceptively normal even while the bone is already in trouble. An MRI can reveal swelling within the bone before an X-ray catches up, which is why it is sometimes ordered when the exam is suspicious but the X-ray is clear.

A large part of the workup is ruling out infection, since the two can look alike and occasionally occur together, especially if there is an open sore. Blood tests and imaging help sort that out, but no single test confirms Charcot on its own. The diagnosis comes from putting the whole picture together — the exam, the imaging, and the context of diabetic neuropathy. This is also why an in-person evaluation matters so much here: a photo or a phone description simply cannot substitute for feeling the warmth, checking sensation, and reading the films.

How Charcot foot is treated

The heart of treatment during the active phase is taking weight and movement off the foot so the bone can stabilize. That usually means immobilization, most often with a total contact cast — a carefully molded cast that spreads pressure evenly and keeps the foot still. As the foot calms down, patients are typically transitioned into a protective device such as a CROW boot (short for Charcot Restraint Orthotic Walker), a custom-made brace designed to support the foot and let a person move more safely. Throughout, staying off the foot as directed — including using crutches, a knee scooter, or a wheelchair when asked — is the single most important thing a patient contributes, even though it is also the hardest.

When a foot has already deformed badly, become unstable, or keeps breaking down into ulcers, surgery may be considered to realign and fuse the bones into a stable, shoeable foot. In the most advanced situations, that reconstructive work becomes part of a broader limb salvage effort aimed at preserving the foot and keeping a person walking. Our overview of Charcot arthropathy as a condition we treat goes into the surgical options in more depth. Which path makes sense depends entirely on the stage of the foot and the health of the tissue around it, and that plan is built at the exam and adjusted as the foot responds.

Living with Charcot foot and preventing another crisis

Once the active phase has passed, the work shifts to protecting the foot for the long run. Because a remodeled Charcot foot is often a different shape than it started, custom footwear and custom orthotics are frequently used to cushion the new pressure points and keep the skin intact. Daily foot checks become non-negotiable: with sensation reduced, you are looking for the problems you can no longer feel — a reddened spot, a callus starting to form, a patch of skin that is warmer than the rest. A warm, swollen area is worth a prompt call, because a Charcot foot can flare again, and the opposite foot carries real risk too.

Keeping those pressure points from opening into wounds is central, since an ulcer on a misshapen foot is harder to heal and can expose the deeper bone. Our guide to diabetic foot ulcers and why speed matters covers what to do if the skin does break down. Steady blood sugar control, regular podiatry visits, and shoes that fit the foot you have now — rather than the foot you used to have — are what keep a Charcot foot stable over the years. If you have diabetes and a foot has suddenly changed, you can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices; this is one condition where being seen early genuinely changes the outcome.

Common Questions

Charcot foot FAQs

Is Charcot foot painful?

It can be, but one of the things that makes Charcot foot dangerous is that it is often far less painful than the amount of bone damage would suggest. Because diabetic nerve damage dulls sensation, many people feel only mild discomfort — or none — even while the foot is actively fracturing. That is exactly why a hot, swollen foot in someone with neuropathy should be taken seriously whether or not it hurts.

How is Charcot foot different from a foot infection?

Both can make a foot red, warm, and swollen, so they are genuinely easy to confuse. An infection is more likely to come with a fever, a wound or drainage, and a feeling of being unwell, while early Charcot foot often has none of those and tends to improve somewhat when the foot is elevated. The two can also occur at the same time. Because no single sign is definitive, the difference needs to be sorted out with an exam and imaging rather than guesswork.

Can Charcot foot be reversed?

The active inflammation can settle, and an early, well-protected foot may stabilize with little lasting change in shape. Once bones have shifted and the foot has deformed, however, that structural change generally does not reverse on its own — treatment then focuses on keeping the foot stable and protected. This is the core reason early recognition matters so much: the sooner the foot is offloaded, the more of its original shape can often be preserved.

How long will I have to stay off my foot?

Longer than most people expect, often measured in months rather than weeks, and the exact length varies from one patient to the next. The foot has to pass out of its active phase and let the bone consolidate before normal weight-bearing is safe. Rather than a fixed date, your surgeon follows the foot over repeated visits — checking warmth, swelling, and imaging — and advances your activity as the foot is ready.

Can you get Charcot foot in both feet?

Yes. The nerve damage that sets up Charcot foot usually affects both feet, so a person who has had it in one foot carries real risk in the other. That is why ongoing foot protection and regular monitoring apply to both feet, not just the one that was affected.

Who is most at risk for Charcot foot?

The highest risk is in people who have had diabetes for a long time and have developed peripheral neuropathy, the loss of protective sensation in the feet. Risk is higher still for those who have already had a foot ulcer or a previous Charcot episode, or who have recently had foot trauma or surgery. If you have diabetes and reduced feeling in your feet, it is worth asking your podiatrist how to watch for the early signs.
