Why Diabetic Neuropathy Hides Foot Injuries

Diabetes can quiet the nerves that make an injury hurt. Here's why a serious foot problem can develop without any warning — and how to find it when you can't feel it.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated November 3, 2026

Diabetic neuropathy hides foot injuries because it damages the sensory nerves that would normally make a cut, blister, burn, or broken bone hurt. When those nerves stop carrying pain signals, the injury is still there — it simply sends no alarm. A wound can form, deepen, and become infected while the foot feels completely normal. That is why diabetic foot problems are so often found by looking, not by feeling, and why a routine of checking your feet matters more than waiting for something to bother you.

Pain gets a bad reputation, but it is one of the body's most useful tools. It is the reason you pull your hand off a hot stove before you think about it and the reason a pebble in your shoe becomes unbearable within a block. High blood sugar over time can blunt or erase that signal in the feet, and when the alarm goes quiet, ordinary mishaps that would stop anyone else in their tracks go completely unnoticed.

What "loss of protective sensation" actually means

Clinicians have a specific name for the problem at the center of this article: loss of protective sensation, often shortened to LOPS. It means the foot can no longer reliably feel the kinds of pressure, friction, heat, and sharp contact that would normally trigger you to react and protect yourself. According to the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases, research suggests up to one-half of people with diabetes have peripheral neuropathy, the nerve damage that most often affects the feet and legs.

We test for this in the office with a simple tool called a monofilament — a thin nylon strand pressed against several spots on the sole. If you cannot feel it touch your skin, protective sensation is reduced, and your feet are at higher risk even if you have never had a wound. Nerve damage like this usually builds slowly. Most of it develops over many years, and mild loss of feeling can go unnoticed for a long time, which is exactly what makes it sneaky.

Three kinds of nerve damage that work together to hide injury

Neuropathy is not one single thing. Diabetes can affect three different types of nerves in the foot, and each one hides injury in its own way.

  • Sensory nerves carry feeling. When they are damaged, you lose the ability to detect pain, temperature, and pressure — so you never notice the injury itself.
  • Motor nerves control the small muscles that hold the toes and arch in balance. When they weaken, the foot can gradually change shape: toes claw, the arch shifts, and bony spots start pressing against the shoe in places that were never pressure points before. Those new high-pressure spots build calluses and, underneath them, wounds.
  • Autonomic nerves run things you never think about, including sweat and skin moisture. The NIDDK notes that more than 30 percent of people with diabetes have autonomic neuropathy. In the feet it leaves skin dry and prone to cracking, and those cracks are open doors for infection.

Put together, the three create a trap: the foot develops a new weak spot, breaks down there, and never tells you about any of it. If you also have nerve symptoms that do cause discomfort — burning, tingling, electric jolts — that is a different side of the same condition, and we cover it in our guide to neuropathic pain in the foot. Many people have both: pain in some areas and numbness in others.

The injuries neuropathy hides most often

Certain everyday events are the usual culprits behind a wound that seemed to appear out of nowhere. Knowing them helps you look in the right places.

  • Burns. Heating pads, electric blankets, hot bath water, a car's floor heater, campfire rings, and sun-baked pavement or sand can all scald a foot that can't feel the heat. Testing bath water with a numb foot is unreliable — use your elbow or a thermometer instead.
  • Foreign objects. A tack, staple, small stone, or bunched sock seam can ride inside a shoe all day and press a hole into the skin without ever registering. People are sometimes surprised to find an object embedded in the sole that they never felt.
  • Shoe friction. A new pair of shoes, a tight toe box, or a wrinkled insole creates blisters and raw spots. Without pain to signal "these don't fit," the rubbing just continues.
  • Pressure wounds under a callus. A callus is a sign of repeated friction. When the pressure keeps up, the tissue beneath it can break down into an ulcer that stays hidden under the thickened skin until it is picked or trimmed open. These are the wounds that become diabetic foot ulcers if they are not caught and offloaded early.
  • Fractures you can't feel. A bone can crack and the foot can keep bearing weight because it does not hurt. In a neuropathic foot, repeated stress on bones and joints can trigger Charcot arthropathy, a serious condition in which the foot's architecture collapses — often with swelling and warmth but little or no pain.

Why "it doesn't hurt" is the most dangerous reassurance

Most of us read the absence of pain as a sign that nothing is wrong. With a neuropathic foot, that instinct works against you. No pain does not mean no problem — it can mean the warning system is broken. A small wound that would send anyone else limping to the medicine cabinet gets stepped on again and again, grinding deeper, while the person keeps walking, gardening, or working a shift.

The consequences compound quietly. An unnoticed wound has time to grow and to let bacteria in. Diabetes can also slow healing and reduce blood flow to the feet, so a problem that is caught late is harder to turn around than the same problem caught on day one. This is the chain of events that, at its worst, leads toward the kind of advanced care we describe in our overview of limb salvage — and it very often starts with an injury nobody felt.

Northern Utah adds its own wrinkles. Our dry climate and cold winters mean cracked heels and heavy reliance on space heaters and heating pads, both of which raise the odds of a burn or a fissure in a foot that can't feel it. An afternoon on a Wasatch trail in stiff boots can raise a blister you won't notice until you take your socks off at home.

How to find injuries you can't feel

If feeling is no longer a reliable alarm, you replace it with sight, touch with your hands, and routine. None of this is complicated, and done daily it catches most problems while they are still minor.

  • Look at your feet every day in good light — tops, soles, heels, and especially between the toes. Use a hand mirror on the floor, or ask a family member, to see the parts you can't.
  • Use your hands. Run your fingers over the soles to feel for swelling, warm spots, wetness, or something stuck in the skin. A spot that is warmer than the rest of the foot can be an early sign of trouble brewing underneath.
  • Check inside your shoes before you put them on. Turn them over and shake them out, and smooth any wrinkles in socks or insoles. This single habit heads off a lot of puncture wounds and blisters.
  • Watch your socks. A stain, a damp patch, or an odor after taking them off can be the first clue to a wound you never saw happen.
  • Protect the skin. Keep feet moisturized to prevent cracks (but not between the toes), never go barefoot, and test bath water with a thermometer or your elbow, not your foot.
  • Keep your professional exams. A podiatrist can test your protective sensation, trim calluses safely before they ulcerate, and fit footwear that spreads pressure away from weak spots. If your feet are already changing shape, custom orthotics and proper diabetic shoes can take load off the areas most likely to break down.

If you want a broader checklist of what else to watch for and what each warning sign means, our article on the early signs of diabetic foot problems walks through them in detail.

When to call us right away

With diabetes, a foot problem is never something to watch for a few weeks. Call the clinic or seek urgent care the same day if you notice any of these:

  • Any open wound, sore, or blister — even a small one, and even if it does not hurt
  • Redness, warmth, swelling, drainage, or a bad smell, which can mean infection
  • A foot that is suddenly red, hot, and swollen, with or without pain — this can signal an early Charcot foot and is an emergency
  • A cut or wound that is not clearly healing within a day or two
  • New or worsening numbness, tingling, or weakness

Do not wait to see whether these settle down on their own. The whole reason neuropathy is dangerous is that it removes the pain that would normally tell you to act, so you have to act on what you see rather than what you feel. Common Questions

Diabetic neuropathy and hidden injury FAQs

Why don't I feel it when I injure a diabetic foot?

Diabetes can damage the sensory nerves that carry pain, temperature, and pressure signals from your feet. When those nerves no longer work normally, an injury happens but sends no alarm, so a cut, blister, or burn can form and worsen while the foot feels completely normal.

Can nerve damage really hide a broken bone?

Yes. If protective sensation is lost, a bone can crack and you may keep walking on it because it does not hurt. In a neuropathic foot, repeated stress on unfelt injuries can lead to Charcot arthropathy, where the foot's bones and joints break down and the shape of the foot changes — often with swelling and warmth but surprisingly little pain.

If nothing hurts, how will I know something is wrong?

You replace feeling with looking. Check your feet every day in good light, including the soles and between the toes, using a mirror or a family member's help for areas you can't see. Watch for redness, swelling, a warm spot, drainage, or stains and odors on your socks — these are the clues that take the place of pain.

How often should I check my feet if I have neuropathy?

Every single day, and more often if you have been active, worn new shoes, or been hiking. A quick daily inspection catches most problems while they are still minor. You should also keep regular professional foot exams so a podiatrist can test your sensation and address calluses or pressure points before they turn into wounds.

Is a hot, swollen foot with no pain an emergency?

It can be. A foot that is suddenly red, warm, and swollen — with or without pain — may be an early Charcot foot or an infection, both of which need urgent evaluation. Do not wait to see if it settles. Call the clinic or seek urgent care the same day.

Can I get feeling back once protective sensation is gone?

Nerve damage from diabetes is often lasting, and how much sensation can improve varies from person to person. Keeping blood sugar well controlled helps protect the nerves you still have, and your care team can tell you what to expect in your situation. Even when feeling does not return, a strong daily checking routine keeps your feet safe.
