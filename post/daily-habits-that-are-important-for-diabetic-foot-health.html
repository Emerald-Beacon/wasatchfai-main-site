Daily Habits That Are Important for Diabetic Foot Health

A handful of small habits — done every single day — catch problems early and keep at-risk feet out of the emergency room.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Diabetes damages the nerves and blood vessels that keep your feet healthy, which means small injuries can go unnoticed and heal slowly. The single best defense is a short list of daily habits: inspecting your feet, wearing the right shoes, practicing good hygiene, and keeping blood sugar in a healthy range. None of these habits take more than a few minutes, but done consistently, they prevent the vast majority of diabetic foot complications before they start.

If you live with diabetes, your feet deserve a few extra minutes of attention every day. Peripheral neuropathy can mask pain from a blister, cut, or pressure sore, and reduced circulation slows healing once a wound does form. That combination is why a minor scrape can quietly become a serious problem. Below, our podiatrists in Farmington and South Ogden outline the daily routine we recommend to every diabetic patient.

Check your feet every day

A daily foot check is the single most effective habit for catching problems while they're still minor. Look — and feel — over every part of your foot, including the sole, heel, and the skin between your toes, in good lighting. If you can't see the bottom of your foot easily, use a mirror or ask a family member to help.

Look for:

  • Cuts, blisters, or cracks in the skin
  • Redness, swelling, or warmth in one area
  • Calluses or corns that could break down into sores
  • Changes in skin color, including pale, blue, or dark patches
  • Any fluid drainage or unusual odor

Tying this check to a habit you already have — brushing your teeth or getting dressed — makes it easier to stick with. Taking a quick photo on your phone each week can also help you track subtle changes over time.

Wear the right shoes, every time

Well-fitting footwear is one of the most effective ways to prevent diabetic foot injuries. Look for shoes with a wide, roomy toe box, soft and breathable materials, extra cushioning, and a shock-absorbing sole. Seamless, moisture-wicking socks reduce friction that can lead to blisters. Break in new shoes gradually — wearing them for short periods at first — rather than all at once, and never go barefoot, even at home, since bare feet are far more likely to suffer an unnoticed injury.

Build hygiene into your daily routine

Wash your feet daily in warm — not hot — water with a mild soap, and dry them thoroughly, especially between the toes, since trapped moisture encourages fungal infections. Apply a fragrance-free moisturizer to keep skin from cracking, but avoid putting lotion directly between your toes, where excess moisture can also promote fungal growth. Trim toenails straight across to help prevent ingrown nails, and if reaching your feet or trimming nails safely is difficult, ask our office about routine nail care visits.

Keep blood sugar in a healthy range

Blood sugar control is directly connected to foot health. Consistently high glucose levels damage the nerves and narrow the blood vessels that supply your feet, which both increases your risk of injury and slows your body's ability to heal one. Working with your primary care provider or endocrinologist to keep your blood sugar in your target range is one of the most protective things you can do for your feet long-term.

Stay active in ways that protect your feet

Regular movement improves circulation without placing excess strain on your joints. Walking, swimming, cycling, and gentle yoga are all good options for most people with diabetes. Always wear supportive shoes during activity, and check your feet afterward for any new redness, blisters, or pressure spots.

Partner with your healthcare team

Daily habits work best alongside routine professional care. We recommend an annual comprehensive foot exam for anyone with diabetes, with more frequent visits if you have neuropathy, poor circulation, a history of ulcers, or other complications. These exams catch changes in sensation, circulation, and skin health that can be hard to notice on your own.

If you notice a wound that isn't healing, spreading redness, drainage, or a change in skin color, don't wait for your next scheduled visit — request an appointment right away. Our team also treats related conditions like Charcot arthropathy that can develop from long-term nerve damage in the foot.

Common Questions

Diabetic foot health FAQs

How often should I check my feet if I have diabetes?

Check your feet once every day, ideally at the same time so it becomes a habit — many people tie it to brushing their teeth or getting dressed. Look over the entire foot, including the sole and the skin between your toes, in good lighting.

What should I look for during a daily foot check?

Watch for cuts, blisters, cracks, redness, swelling, warmth in one area, calluses, changes in skin color, or any drainage or odor. Any of these can signal a problem that needs attention, especially if it doesn't improve within a day or two.

Why is it dangerous to go barefoot if I have diabetes?

Diabetes-related nerve damage can reduce your ability to feel small injuries, so stepping on something sharp or hot without shoes can cause a wound you don't notice right away. Wearing supportive shoes, even indoors, protects your feet from unnoticed injuries.

What kind of shoes are best for diabetic feet?

Look for shoes with a wide, roomy toe box, soft breathable materials, extra cushioning, and a shock-absorbing sole, paired with seamless socks. Break new shoes in gradually and avoid tight or poorly fitting footwear that can rub and cause blisters.

How does blood sugar affect foot health?

Consistently high blood sugar damages the nerves and blood vessels that supply your feet, which reduces sensation and slows healing. Keeping your blood sugar in your target range protects your feet as much as it protects the rest of your body.

How often should I see a podiatrist if I have diabetes?

An annual comprehensive foot exam is recommended for most people with diabetes, with more frequent visits if you have neuropathy, poor circulation, or a history of foot ulcers. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
