Foot Pain 101: Identifying the Root Causes and Finding Relief

Where does it hurt? Use the location of your pain as a map — each spot on the foot points to a short list of likely causes and tells you what to read or do next.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated November 19, 2026

Foot pain is a symptom, not a diagnosis — and the single most useful clue to what's behind it is where it hurts. The heel, the arch, the ball of the foot, the toes, the top, and the outer edge each point to a short list of likely causes. Think of this page as a map: find the spot that matches your pain, see the usual suspects for that area, and know which ones are worth a closer look and where to read more.

A map narrows the field; it doesn't make the diagnosis. Two conditions can produce pain in the same place, and the exact pattern — when it hurts, what makes it worse, whether there's swelling or numbness — is what an exam sorts out. Use the sections below to get oriented, then follow the links to the specific condition or to a visit.

Pain in the heel

The heel is the most common complaint we see, and the cause usually splits by exactly where on the heel it hurts. Pain on the underside, sharpest with your first steps in the morning, points to plantar fasciitis — irritation of the thick band of tissue along the sole. Pain at the back of the heel, where the Achilles tendon attaches, is a different problem and is treated differently. Deep, bruise-like heel pain that worsens the longer you're on your feet can signal a stress fracture. Our overview of chronic heel pain and spurs walks through how these are told apart.

Pain in the arch

Arch pain often shares roots with plantar fasciitis, since the fascia runs the length of the sole. But aching that tracks along the inner arch and ankle, sometimes with the arch slowly flattening, points instead to the posterior tibial tendon — the main support for the arch. Flat feet and worn-out, unsupportive shoes keep the arch under strain. If your arch pain eases for a while and then returns, the reason it keeps coming back is usually structural; we dig into that pattern in why your arch pain keeps coming back.

Pain in the ball of the foot

Pain under the ball of the foot — metatarsalgia — comes down to pressure. When weight concentrates across the front of the foot, the result is a persistent ache or the classic feeling of "walking on a pebble." Narrow or high-heeled shoes, high-impact activity, thinning of the foot's natural fat pad with age, and structural shifts from a bunion or hammertoe all funnel load into this zone. A burning or electric feeling between the third and fourth toes, rather than a dull ache, is more typical of a pinched nerve (a neuroma) than of metatarsalgia.

Pain in the toes

The toes carry a wide range of causes. A bunion — the enlarged, angled joint at the base of the big toe — is one of the most common, and narrow footwear makes it worse. Stiffness and pain in that same big-toe joint, especially when you push off, can instead be hallux rigidus, a form of arthritis. Sudden, intense pain, redness, and swelling in the big toe that comes on overnight is a hallmark of gout. Ingrown toenails and hammertoes round out the list.

Pain on the top of the foot

Pain across the top of the foot is easy to overlook because there's no single famous culprit. Tight shoelaces and the pressure of certain shoes can inflame the tendons that run over the top. Arthritis in the midfoot joints shows up here too. The one not to dismiss is a stress fracture: a deep, focal ache over a bone in the top of the foot that worsens with activity and lingers afterward deserves evaluation, particularly in runners and anyone who recently ramped up mileage or standing.

Pain along the outer edge

Pain on the pinky-toe side of the foot has its own short list. A tailor's bunion — a bony bump at the base of the little toe — rubs against shoes much the way a regular bunion does. The peroneal tendons that run down the outer ankle and foot can become irritated, and a roll of the ankle can leave soreness there. A sudden pain on the outer edge after a twist or a jump can mean a fracture of the fifth metatarsal, which is worth ruling out rather than walking off.

Burning, tingling, or whole-foot pain

When the problem is nerve-related, the sensation changes character — burning, tingling, numbness, or a pins-and-needles feeling rather than a mechanical ache. Tarsal tunnel syndrome, where a nerve is compressed near the inner ankle, is one cause; diabetic neuropathy is another and tends to affect both feet. Pain, stiffness, and swelling across several joints at once, worse in the morning, points toward arthritis. Numbness or loss of sensation in the feet should always be checked, especially if you have diabetes.

What the location points you toward

Once you have a sense of the likely cause, early care looks similar across most of these: relative rest, supportive footwear, ice for a flare, and an over-the-counter anti-inflammatory for short-term comfort. Lasting relief, though, comes from treating the actual cause — stretching and offloading for plantar fasciitis, custom orthotics for a collapsing arch or pressure in the ball of the foot, or medical management for gout or arthritis. For a fuller rundown of what you can try at home before an appointment, see our guide to easing chronic foot pain with simple techniques.

When location isn't enough — and when to come in

Some signs mean the map has done its job and it's time for an exam. See a podiatrist if pain lasts more than a week or two despite rest, keeps returning, limits your daily activities, or comes with swelling, redness, a visible deformity, or numbness. Loss of sensation, an inability to bear weight, spreading redness or fever, or a wound that won't heal in a diabetic foot are not "wait and see" problems — call the clinic or seek urgent care the same day. We lay out those warning signs in more detail in when foot pain isn't normal. Common Questions

Foot pain FAQs

Does the location of my foot pain tell you the exact diagnosis?

Location narrows the field — it doesn't close the case. Where it hurts points to a short list of likely causes, but conditions overlap, and the pattern of pain, swelling, numbness, and what makes it worse is what an exam and sometimes imaging sort out. The map is a starting point, not a substitute for evaluation.

What causes pain on the top of my foot?

Pain across the top of the foot often comes from tendons irritated by tight laces or shoe pressure, or from arthritis in the midfoot joints. The cause not to dismiss is a stress fracture — a deep, focal ache over a bone that worsens with activity and lingers afterward — especially in runners or after a jump in mileage or time on your feet.

Why does the outside edge of my foot hurt?

Pain on the little-toe side has a few usual causes: a tailor's bunion (a bump at the base of the fifth toe that rubs on shoes), irritation of the peroneal tendons that run down the outer foot, or a fracture of the fifth metatarsal after a twist or jump. A sudden outer-edge pain after an injury is worth ruling out rather than walking off.

What does burning or tingling in my foot usually mean?

Burning, tingling, or numbness points to a nerve rather than a muscle or joint. Possible sources include a pinched nerve near the inner ankle (tarsal tunnel syndrome), a neuroma between the toes, or neuropathy, which often affects both feet and is common with diabetes. New numbness or loss of sensation should always be evaluated, and promptly if you have diabetes.

Can pain in one spot have more than one cause?

Yes. The heel alone can be plantar fasciitis, Achilles irritation, or a stress fracture depending on exactly where and when it hurts, and more than one problem can be present at once. That's why pinpointing the spot is useful for narrowing possibilities but isn't enough to settle on a single diagnosis or treatment.

Which kind of doctor should I see for foot pain?

A podiatrist (DPM) is a physician who specializes in the foot and ankle and can diagnose and treat the full range of causes in this guide, from tendon and nerve problems to fractures, arthritis, and deformities — medically or, when needed, surgically. For pain that won't settle, starting with a foot and ankle specialist usually gets you to the cause faster.
