When to Seek Treatment for an Ingrown Toenail

Not every ingrown toenail needs a doctor visit — but some do. Here's how to tell the difference before a minor irritation turns into an infection.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

An ingrown toenail happens when the edge of the nail grows into the surrounding skin instead of over it, causing pain, redness, and swelling along the nail border. Many mild cases respond well to a few days of careful home care. But when swelling spreads, drainage appears, or pain becomes severe, waiting can allow a simple irritation to turn into a spreading infection — so knowing which symptoms call for a podiatrist matters.

Ingrown toenails are one of the most common nail complaints we see in Farmington and South Ogden, most often affecting the big toe. Below, our podiatrists break down what causes them, which symptoms are safe to manage at home, and which ones mean it's time to come in.

What causes an ingrown toenail?

Ingrown toenails develop when the edge or corner of a nail curves and presses into the skin fold beside it, causing irritation and sometimes a break in the skin. Common causes include:

  • Cutting nails too short or rounding the corners instead of trimming straight across
  • Shoes that crowd the toes, especially narrow or pointed styles
  • A genetic tendency toward curved or thick nail plates
  • Repeated trauma to the toe, such as stubbing or dropping something on it
  • Sweaty feet or poor foot hygiene, which soften skin and encourage irritation

When is it safe to treat an ingrown toenail at home?

In the early stages, mild pain, slight redness, and tenderness along one side of the nail are typical — and usually manageable without a clinic visit. At this point, simple measures often help:

  • Soaking the foot in warm water for 15–20 minutes, two to three times a day
  • Gently lifting the nail edge away from the skin with clean cotton, if it can be done without forcing it
  • Switching to roomier, properly fitted shoes and avoiding tight socks
  • Keeping the area clean and dry between soaks

If these steps bring steady improvement over a couple of days, continued home care is usually reasonable. If they don't — or if symptoms worsen — it's time to get evaluated.

What signs mean the ingrown toenail is getting worse?

Watch for these signs that a mild ingrown toenail is progressing and needs professional attention:

  • Increasing swelling or warmth around the nail fold
  • Fluid or pus draining from the affected area
  • Redness that continues to spread around the toe rather than staying localized
  • Throbbing pain that doesn't ease with soaking or rest
  • Symptoms that persist beyond 48–72 hours of consistent home care

Drainage, spreading redness, and throbbing pain are classic signs of infection. Left untreated, an infected ingrown toenail can worsen quickly, so these symptoms should prompt a visit rather than more waiting.

Who should seek care sooner rather than later?

Some patients should not wait out even a mild ingrown toenail. If you have diabetes, poor circulation, or a compromised immune system, seek care promptly at the first sign of irritation — infections in these situations can progress rapidly and, in rare and severe cases, threaten the toe itself. The same goes for anyone whose ingrown toenail keeps coming back after treatment, since recurring cases often point to an underlying nail shape or growth pattern that needs a longer-term fix.

Severe pain that interferes with walking, wearing shoes, or sleeping is also a signal that the nail may have penetrated deeply or that an abscess has formed beneath the skin — both are best managed in the office rather than at home.

How do podiatrists treat ingrown toenails?

In the office, treatment is typically quick and well tolerated. For most ingrown toenails, your podiatrist numbs the toe with local anesthesia and trims away only the offending sliver of nail — not the entire nail plate — which relieves pressure and allows the area to heal. For toenails that keep recurring, a procedure that removes a portion of the nail root (matrixectomy) can prevent that section of nail from regrowing, significantly reducing the chance of it coming back in the same spot.

To help prevent future episodes, trim nails straight across rather than rounding the corners, choose shoes with adequate room in the toe box, and keep feet clean and dry. If an ingrown toenail isn't improving with home care, you can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Ingrown toenail FAQs

Can I treat an ingrown toenail myself?

Mild cases with only slight redness and tenderness can often be managed with warm soaks, gentle nail care, and roomier shoes. If symptoms improve within a couple of days, home care is usually enough. If they worsen or don't improve, it's best to see a podiatrist.

How do I know if my ingrown toenail is infected?

Signs of infection include pus or fluid draining from the nail fold, redness that keeps spreading around the toe, increasing warmth or swelling, and throbbing pain. Any of these signs warrant prompt professional care rather than continued home treatment.

How long should I try home treatment before seeing a doctor?

If symptoms are mild, a couple of days of warm soaks and careful nail care is a reasonable trial. If pain, redness, or swelling persist beyond 48–72 hours, or worsen at any point, it's time to schedule an appointment.

Will the podiatrist remove my whole toenail?

Usually not. For most ingrown toenails, only the small offending edge of the nail is trimmed away under local anesthesia — the rest of the nail stays intact. Removal of a portion of the nail root is reserved for toenails that keep recurring in the same spot.

I have diabetes — should I wait to see if it heals on its own?

No. If you have diabetes, poor circulation, or a weakened immune system, seek care at the first sign of an ingrown toenail rather than waiting. Infections can progress quickly in these situations, so prompt evaluation is important.

Why does my ingrown toenail keep coming back?

Recurring ingrown toenails often point to an underlying nail shape or growth pattern rather than a one-time irritation. A procedure that removes a small portion of the nail root can prevent that section from regrowing and reduce future episodes.
