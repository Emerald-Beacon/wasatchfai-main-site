Heel Pain in Winter: Combatting Seasonal Foot Issues in Utah

Cold Utah winters don't just make your toes numb — they can trigger or worsen heel pain. Here's why, and what actually helps.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Cold temperatures reduce circulation to your feet and cause soft tissue to contract and stiffen, which raises tension on the plantar fascia and Achilles tendon and can make winter heel pain worse than usual. Combine that with heavy winter boots that often lack real arch and heel support, and it's no surprise our Farmington and South Ogden offices see more heel pain complaints once the temperature drops.

If your heel pain seems to flare every winter — or shows up for the first time once the snow starts falling — you're not imagining it. Below, our podiatrists explain the seasonal factors behind winter heel pain and the steps that make the biggest difference.

Why does cold weather cause more heel pain?

Cold weather reduces blood flow to the feet, which are already farthest from the heart and among the first areas to feel the effects of chilly temperatures. Reduced circulation leaves the tissues in your heel and arch more vulnerable to stiffness and discomfort. At the same time, cold causes muscles, tendons, and connective tissue to contract and tighten, which increases tension in structures like the plantar fascia and Achilles tendon — the two most common sources of winter heel pain.

What winter foot problems cause heel pain?

Three issues account for most of the increase in heel pain our patients notice during the colder months:

  • Plantar fasciitis — cold tissue contracts and stiffens, increasing tension in the band of tissue that runs along the bottom of the foot and often producing sharp, stabbing pain with the first steps of the day
  • Achilles tendonitis — the tendon connecting your calf muscle to your heel is also affected by cold, and winter activities like skiing, snowshoeing, or shoveling without a proper warm-up can aggravate it
  • Inappropriate footwear — winter boots are built for warmth first, and many lack the arch support and firm heel structure your feet need, leaving the plantar fascia and heel to absorb extra strain

How can I prevent winter heel pain?

Most winter heel pain responds well to a few simple habit changes:

  • Choose winter boots with proper arch and heel support, and add a supportive insole or custom orthotic if your boots run flat
  • Warm up with gentle calf and plantar fascia stretches before heading out into the cold, especially before skiing, sledding, or shoveling
  • Keep your feet warm and dry with insulated, moisture-wicking socks and properly fitted boots
  • Take breaks from prolonged cold exposure when you can, since sustained cold keeps circulation reduced
  • Don't ignore heel pain that lingers — early treatment prevents a minor seasonal flare from becoming a chronic problem

How is winter heel pain treated?

Treatment for cold-related heel pain follows the same conservative, stepwise approach we use for heel pain year-round. Most patients improve with rest, ice, stretching, supportive footwear, and orthotics. If pain from plantar fasciitis or Achilles tendonitis doesn't ease with home care, in-office options such as physical therapy, night splints, or shock wave therapy can help. Surgery is rarely needed and is reserved for cases that don't respond after an extended course of conservative treatment.

When should I see a podiatrist for winter heel pain?

See a podiatrist if your heel pain lasts more than a week or two despite home care, keeps you from normal winter activities, or returns every morning. Seek care sooner if you notice swelling, numbness or tingling, or pain following a fall on ice or snow, since these can point to a different injury. If you have diabetes or circulation problems, any new foot pain in cold weather deserves prompt evaluation. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Winter heel pain FAQs

Why does my heel hurt more in the winter?

Cold temperatures reduce circulation to your feet and cause tissues like the plantar fascia and Achilles tendon to contract and stiffen, increasing tension and making existing heel pain worse. Winter boots that lack proper arch and heel support can add to the problem.

Can winter boots cause heel pain?

Yes. Many winter boots prioritize warmth over structure and lack adequate arch and heel support, which strains the plantar fascia and heel with every step. Choosing boots with better support, or adding a supportive insole or orthotic, can make a significant difference.

Is winter heel pain a sign of plantar fasciitis?

It can be. Cold weather is a common trigger for plantar fasciitis flare-ups because contracted, stiff tissue puts more tension on the plantar fascia. If you notice sharp pain with your first steps in the morning, plantar fasciitis is a likely cause.

Should I stretch before winter outdoor activities?

Yes. Gentle calf and plantar fascia stretches before skiing, snowshoeing, shoveling, or other cold-weather activity help offset the tightening effect of cold temperatures on your tendons and connective tissue, reducing your risk of Achilles tendonitis and heel pain.

When should I see a podiatrist about winter heel pain?

See a podiatrist if heel pain lasts more than a week or two of home care, limits your activities, or returns every morning. Seek care sooner for swelling, numbness, or pain following a fall on ice. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
