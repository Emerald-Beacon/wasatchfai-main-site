How to Improve Your Posture (and What Your Feet Have to Do With It)

Posture starts from the ground up. Here's how the shape and alignment of your feet influence your spine and body position — and what you can do about it.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Your feet are the foundation your entire body is built on, and their alignment is closely tied to the shape of your spine and overall body position. When your feet roll, tilt, or bear weight unevenly, that misalignment travels upward — often showing up as neck, back, hip, or knee discomfort long before anyone thinks to look at their feet.

Posture problems are usually blamed on sitting too much, weak core muscles, or a poor mattress. Those things matter, but foot mechanics are an overlooked piece of the puzzle. Below, our podiatrists explain how foot structure affects posture, the everyday habits that make it worse, and practical steps you can take to stand and move taller.

How are feet and posture connected?

Your feet are the first point of contact between your body and the ground with every step you take. The way they absorb impact and distribute weight sets off a chain reaction up through the ankles, knees, hips, and spine. When your feet are properly aligned, that chain stays balanced. When they are not, your body compensates — often by shifting your center of gravity, changing how your knees track, or rounding your shoulders forward to counterbalance.

Over time, these small compensations become habits, and habits become posture. That is why correcting a foot alignment issue can sometimes resolve back or hip pain that has not responded to other treatment.

What causes poor posture?

Posture is rarely caused by one single thing. Common contributors we see include:

  • Stress and body tension, which causes the shoulders and neck to tighten and round forward
  • Unsupportive footwear that fails to stabilize the arch or heel
  • Pregnancy-related weight shifts, which change your center of gravity
  • Weak stabilizing muscles in the core, hips, and lower legs
  • Poor workplace ergonomics, including chairs and desks that do not support a neutral spine

How does foot structure affect your posture?

The shape of your feet plays a direct role in how your body balances itself. Two structural patterns are especially common:

  • High arches tend to shift weight toward the ball of the foot, which can pull the body into a forward lean and increase strain on the lower back.
  • Flat feet often cause the foot to roll inward (overpronate) with each step, which can rotate the knees and hips inward and contribute to lower back and hip discomfort.

Related conditions such as heel spurs or shin splints can also change the way you walk to avoid pain, which reinforces poor postural habits over time.

Practical ways to improve your posture

You do not need a complete overhaul to start correcting posture — small, consistent changes make a real difference:

  • Sit with your feet flat on the floor and knees bent at roughly a 90-degree angle, rather than crossing your legs or tucking your feet under you.
  • Stand with your ankles, hips, and shoulders stacked in a straight vertical line, and avoid locking your knees.
  • Correct your gait by rolling naturally through the foot — from heel to the ball — rather than slapping down flat-footed or landing hard on your heels.
  • Practice balance-focused activity such as yoga or tai chi, which strengthens the small stabilizing muscles in the feet and ankles.
  • Wear supportive footwear, and consider custom orthotics if you have flat feet, high arches, or a diagnosed alignment issue.

When posture problems point to a foot issue

If you have made ergonomic and lifestyle changes but your posture-related discomfort persists, it may be worth having your foot structure evaluated. A podiatrist can assess your arch type, gait, and weight distribution to determine whether a custom orthotic, footwear change, or targeted treatment could relieve strain further up the chain. This is especially worth pursuing if you also notice heel pain, shin splints, or uneven wear on the soles of your shoes.

Good posture is not just about how you look standing up straight — it affects circulation, breathing, joint wear, and long-term mobility. Because your feet are the foundation for all of it, addressing alignment issues early can prevent bigger problems down the road. Common Questions

Posture and foot health FAQs

Can foot problems really affect my posture?

Yes. Your feet are the base your entire body balances on, so misalignment there — such as flat feet or high arches — can change how weight moves through your knees, hips, and spine, contributing to slouching or an uneven stance.

How do flat feet affect posture?

Flat feet often cause the foot to roll inward with each step, which can rotate the knees and hips inward as well. Over time this shift can contribute to lower back and hip discomfort and make it harder to maintain a neutral standing posture.

Will custom orthotics improve my posture?

Custom orthotics are designed to support your specific arch type and correct abnormal foot movement, which can improve alignment further up the chain. Many patients notice reduced back, hip, or knee strain once their foot mechanics are properly supported.

What is the correct way to stand for good posture?

Aim to keep your ankles, hips, and shoulders stacked in a straight vertical line, with your weight distributed evenly across both feet and your knees unlocked. Avoid leaning your weight onto one hip or rolling your feet inward or outward.

Can shoes make my posture worse?

Yes. Shoes without adequate arch or heel support force your feet to compensate with every step, which can throw off your alignment over time. Supportive, properly fitted footwear is one of the simplest ways to protect your posture.

When should I see a podiatrist about posture-related foot pain?

If lifestyle and ergonomic changes have not resolved your discomfort, or if you also notice heel pain, shin splints, or uneven shoe wear, it is worth having your foot structure and gait evaluated. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
