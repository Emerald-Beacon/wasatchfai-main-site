TTS Pain: Recognizing Tarsal Tunnel Syndrome

Burning, tingling, or numbness along the inside of the ankle and foot can be a sign of a pinched nerve — here's what tarsal tunnel syndrome is and when it's time to see a podiatrist.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Tarsal tunnel syndrome (TTS) occurs when the tibial nerve — which runs through a narrow passage called the tarsal tunnel on the inner side of the ankle — becomes compressed or pinched. That compression can cause burning, tingling, shooting pain, or numbness anywhere from the ankle down into the arch and toes. It's often mistaken for other causes of foot pain, so recognizing the pattern of nerve-type symptoms is the first step toward the right treatment.

The tarsal tunnel is a narrow, protected space formed by bone and a ligament along the inside of the ankle. The tibial nerve, along with blood vessels and tendons, passes through this tunnel on its way into the foot. When something inside or around the tunnel puts sustained pressure on the nerve, it can become irritated or damaged — much like carpal tunnel syndrome, but in the ankle instead of the wrist.

What are the symptoms of tarsal tunnel syndrome?

Because TTS is a nerve condition, its symptoms feel different from typical joint or muscle pain. Common signs include:

  • Burning, tingling, or "pins and needles" sensations along the inner ankle, arch, or toes
  • Shooting or electric-feeling pain
  • Numbness or reduced sensation in the sole of the foot
  • Symptoms that worsen with standing, walking, or exercise
  • Pain that can radiate up into the calf

Symptoms often start subtly and gradually worsen, which is why many people live with TTS for a while before seeking care.

What causes tarsal tunnel syndrome?

Anything that adds pressure inside or around the tarsal tunnel can compress the tibial nerve. Common causes include:

  • Flat feet, which can stretch and place tension on the tibial nerve
  • Benign bone growths or cysts near the tarsal tunnel
  • Swelling from arthritis
  • Varicose veins near the tibial nerve
  • Tumors or lipomas pressing on the nerve
  • Swelling following an ankle injury or sprain
  • Diabetes, which can contribute to nerve changes
  • Overuse from running, standing for long periods, or extensive walking

Because ankle injuries and swelling are a common trigger, TTS sometimes develops after an ankle sprain that didn't fully resolve — another reason it's worth having any injury with lingering symptoms checked.

How is tarsal tunnel syndrome diagnosed?

Diagnosis starts with a physical exam, including the Tinel test, where gently tapping over the tarsal tunnel can reproduce tingling or shooting pain if the nerve is compressed. Depending on the exam findings, your podiatrist may also order additional testing such as electromyography (EMG), nerve conduction velocity testing, MRI, or X-rays to identify what's compressing the nerve and rule out other causes.

How is tarsal tunnel syndrome treated?

Treatment usually starts conservatively and escalates only if needed:

  • Rest and activity modification to reduce pressure on the nerve
  • RICE (rest, ice, compression, elevation) for associated swelling
  • NSAIDs to reduce inflammation and pain
  • Immobilization, such as a brace or boot, in more irritated cases
  • Injection therapy to reduce localized inflammation
  • Orthotic devices to correct foot mechanics that stress the nerve — see our custom orthotics options
  • Physical therapy and targeted exercises to improve mobility and reduce nerve tension

When TTS is caused by a structural issue such as a cyst, bone growth, or tumor pressing on the nerve, addressing that underlying cause is often part of the treatment plan. Most cases improve with conservative, nonsurgical care, especially when treatment starts early.

When should I see a podiatrist?

See a podiatrist if you notice burning, tingling, numbness, or shooting pain along the inside of your ankle or foot, especially if it worsens with activity or doesn't improve with rest. Because tibial nerve compression can worsen over time and, in longer-standing cases, lead to lasting nerve damage, earlier evaluation gives you more treatment options and a better chance at full recovery. Common Questions

Tarsal tunnel syndrome FAQs

What does tarsal tunnel syndrome feel like?

Tarsal tunnel syndrome typically causes burning, tingling, or "pins and needles" sensations along the inner ankle, arch, or toes, sometimes with shooting or electric-feeling pain or numbness in the sole of the foot. Symptoms often worsen with standing, walking, or exercise.

What causes tarsal tunnel syndrome?

TTS happens when something compresses the tibial nerve as it passes through the tarsal tunnel. Common causes include flat feet, swelling from arthritis or an ankle injury, varicose veins, benign growths near the nerve, diabetes, and overuse from running or prolonged standing.

Is tarsal tunnel syndrome the same as carpal tunnel syndrome?

They're similar in concept but affect different nerves and locations. Both involve a nerve being compressed as it passes through a narrow anatomical tunnel — carpal tunnel syndrome affects the median nerve in the wrist, while tarsal tunnel syndrome affects the tibial nerve in the ankle.

How is tarsal tunnel syndrome diagnosed?

Diagnosis usually starts with a physical exam and the Tinel test, which checks whether tapping over the tarsal tunnel reproduces tingling or pain. Your podiatrist may also order EMG, nerve conduction studies, MRI, or X-rays to confirm the diagnosis and identify the underlying cause.

Can tarsal tunnel syndrome go away without surgery?

Yes — most cases improve with conservative treatment such as rest, RICE, NSAIDs, orthotics, bracing, injections, and physical therapy, especially when treatment starts early. Surgery is generally reserved for cases with a structural cause or that don't improve with conservative care.

When should I see a podiatrist for ankle nerve pain?

See a podiatrist if you have burning, tingling, numbness, or shooting pain along the inner ankle or foot, especially if it's worsening or not improving with rest. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
