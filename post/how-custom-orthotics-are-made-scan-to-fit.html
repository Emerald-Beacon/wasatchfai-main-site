How Custom Orthotics Are Made: From Scan to Fit

A custom orthotic isn't just molded to the shape of your foot — it's built from a prescription that changes how you load the ground. Here is each step, from the first exam to the day you walk out wearing them.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated October 13, 2026

Custom orthotics are made in a sequence of steps, not stamped out on an assembly line. A podiatrist first examines your feet and watches how you move, then captures the exact shape of each foot while holding it in a corrected position — using a 3D scan, a foam impression, or a plaster cast. That capture, along with a written prescription, goes to a fabrication lab, where the device is built to your measurements over a few weeks and then fitted to you in person. The scan or mold is only the starting point. What actually makes an orthotic "custom" is the prescription that tells the lab how to change the way your foot meets the floor.

That distinction trips a lot of people up. Pressing your foot into a mold feels like the whole point, but two people with nearly identical footprints can walk out with very different devices depending on what their feet are doing wrong. Understanding the process helps you know what you're paying for — and why a device built this way can do things a shelf insert can't. We compare the two head-to-head in custom orthotics versus store-bought inserts; here, we're walking through how the custom version actually gets built.

It starts with an exam, not a scanner

Before anyone captures the shape of your foot, your podiatrist has to figure out what the finished device needs to do. That begins with a hands-on exam: assessing your arch type, checking how far key joints move, feeling for tender spots, and noting where calluses or wear patterns have formed. Calluses in particular are a map — they show where your foot is taking more pressure than it should.

Next comes gait analysis, which simply means watching you stand and walk. Your provider looks at how your heel strikes, whether your arch collapses inward as you load it, and how your weight rolls toward your toes. Some exams add a pressure plate or mat that records where force concentrates under each foot as you step. All of this builds a picture of the mechanical problem behind your symptoms — an inward-rolling flat foot, a high rigid arch that can't absorb shock, or a tendon such as the posterior tibial tendon that's straining to hold your arch up. The diagnosis drives everything that follows. Capture the shape without it, and you've just copied the problem.

Capturing the shape of your foot

Once your podiatrist knows what needs correcting, the next step is recording the three-dimensional shape of your foot. There are three common ways to do it, and clinics often choose based on the device being made and their lab's preferences:

  • 3D optical or laser scanning. A scanner, or sometimes a tablet-based camera system, records the contours of your foot digitally in seconds. It's clean, fast, and easy to send straight to a lab as a data file.
  • Foam impression box. You press each foot into a block of dense foam that holds the imprint. It's simple and reliable, and the box itself becomes the mold the lab works from.
  • Plaster or fiberglass casting. Your provider wraps the foot in casting material that hardens into a negative mold. This method captures fine detail and lets the clinician position the foot precisely by hand.

Here's the part that matters more than which tool gets used: the position your foot is held in during the capture. A good impression holds the foot in a neutral, corrected alignment — the position a healthy foot would sit in — rather than flattened under your full body weight. If you simply stand on a scanner and let your arch collapse, the lab faithfully reproduces that collapsed shape, which is the opposite of what you want. Capturing the foot in its corrected position is what lets the orthotic hold it there once you're walking on it.

The prescription is the part that makes it custom

With the shape recorded, your podiatrist writes a prescription — a detailed set of instructions for the lab. This is where the diagnosis from your exam turns into specific engineering, and it's the single biggest difference between a true custom orthotic and anything off a shelf. A prescription typically spells out:

  • Shell rigidity: whether the base is rigid for maximum motion control, semi-rigid for a balance of support and give, or soft and accommodative to cushion and offload pressure.
  • Posting, or wedging: angled additions under the heel or forefoot that tip the foot a few degrees to correct how it rolls.
  • Arch height and contour: how aggressively the device fills and supports your arch.
  • Heel cup depth: a deeper cup cradles the heel's fat pad and helps control side-to-side motion.
  • Targeted additions: metatarsal pads, cutouts to take pressure off a sore spot, or extensions under the toes.

Material choice is woven through these decisions, and it's worth understanding on its own — our practical guide to selecting custom orthotics goes deeper into matching materials to your goals. The takeaway is that the prescription, not the mold, is the custom part. Two patients with similar foot shapes but different problems will get devices built to different specifications.

How the lab actually builds the device

Your capture and prescription go to a fabrication lab, and what happens there depends on whether the lab is working from a physical mold or a digital file.

From a plaster cast or foam box, a technician first pours a positive model — a solid replica of your foot. They then modify that model by hand to match the prescription, building up the arch or adding correction where the notes call for it, so the model represents your foot in its corrected position rather than its natural one. The shell material is heated and vacuum-formed or pressed over that modified model, taking its exact shape.

From a 3D scan, the same corrections are made digitally in design software. The shell is then either milled from a solid block by a computer-controlled cutter or 3D printed directly from the file. Printing and milling have made the process faster and more repeatable, but the logic is identical — the device is shaped around a corrected model of your foot, not a raw copy of it.

From there the device is finished: posts and wedges are bonded underneath, the edges are ground down so it fits the contour of a shoe, and a top cover — the surface your foot rests on — is added in a material suited to your activity. This hands-on fabrication is why custom orthotics take a few weeks to arrive rather than coming off a rack. It's also why a well-made shell can last for years with routine care.

The fitting appointment

When your orthotics come back from the lab, you return for a fitting. This visit is more than handing over a finished product. Your podiatrist checks that the device seats correctly against your heel and arch, and you try it on — ideally in the shoes you actually plan to wear, which is why it helps to bring them. A dress shoe, a running shoe, and a work boot all have different internal volume, and an orthotic that fits one may crowd another.

Expect small adjustments. Your provider may grind an edge, add a pad, or fine-tune the posting right there in the office. That's normal and built into the process — the lab builds to your prescription, and the fitting is where it's dialed in against your real feet in your real shoes. If something pinches, slides, or digs in, say so at this appointment. It's far easier to adjust the device now than to push through discomfort at home. Pairing orthotics with supportive shoes matters too, since even a perfect device can't do its job inside a worn-out or unstable shoe.

Breaking them in

You don't go from zero to all-day wear on the first day. Most people are advised to start with a couple of hours and add time gradually over the following days or weeks, giving the muscles and joints of the foot time to adapt to being held in a new position. A bit of awareness or mild pressure early on is expected as your foot adjusts.

Some sensations are not part of normal break-in. Sharp pain, new blisters or raw spots, numbness, or pain that spreads up into the ankle or knee are signals to stop and call the office rather than tough it out — they usually mean the device needs an adjustment, not that your foot needs to get used to it. How long adjustment takes varies from person to person, and a follow-up visit to tweak the fit is common and nothing to worry about. Over the long run, the shell often outlasts the top cover, and a worn device can frequently be refurbished or re-covered instead of fully remade. Your podiatrist will tell you what to expect for your specific device.

Is the whole process worth it?

Not every sore foot needs this. For general cushioning and everyday aches, a quality over-the-counter insert is a sensible first step. The full custom process earns its place when you have a specific, diagnosed mechanical problem that a generic insert can't target — an inward-rolling flat foot, stubborn heel pain that hasn't settled with simpler measures, a tendon straining to support your arch, arthritis that needs pressure taken off a painful joint, or a diabetic foot that needs careful offloading to protect the skin. In those cases, the exam, the prescription, and the custom fabrication are doing specific work that a mass-produced insole simply can't.

If you're weighing whether custom orthotics make sense for you, the honest answer comes from an evaluation, not a guess based on symptoms alone. Common Questions

How custom orthotics are made: FAQs

How are custom orthotics made, step by step?

A podiatrist examines your feet and watches you walk to identify the mechanical problem, then captures the shape of each foot in a corrected position using a 3D scan, foam impression, or plaster cast. A written prescription and that capture go to a fabrication lab, which builds the device to your specifications over a few weeks. You then return for a fitting, where the orthotic is checked in your own shoes and adjusted as needed.

Is a 3D scan better than a foam or plaster mold?

No single method is automatically better. A 3D scan is fast, clean, and easy to send to a lab digitally, while foam boxes and plaster casts capture fine detail and let the clinician position the foot precisely by hand. What matters most is not the tool but that your foot is held in a neutral, corrected position during the capture rather than flattened under your full weight.

What happens at the lab after my foot is captured?

The lab builds a model of your foot — poured from a cast or box, or corrected digitally from a scan — and modifies it to match your prescription so it reflects the corrected position. The shell is then formed over that model, milled from a block, or 3D printed. Posts and wedges are added underneath, the edges are ground to fit a shoe, and a top cover is bonded on. That hands-on work is why the process takes a few weeks.

Are custom orthotics 3D printed?

Some are. Many labs now mill the shell from a block or 3D print it directly from a digital scan, while others still form the shell over a physical model poured from a cast or foam box. All of these approaches shape the device around a corrected model of your foot, so the method is less important than the prescription guiding it.

Should I bring my own shoes to the fitting?

Yes. Bring the shoes you actually plan to wear with the orthotics. Dress shoes, running shoes, and work boots have different internal room, and an orthotic that fits one may crowd another. Trying the device in your real footwear lets your podiatrist confirm the fit and make adjustments on the spot.

Why do new orthotics sometimes need adjustments?

The lab builds to your written prescription, and the fitting appointment is where that device is fine-tuned against your real feet and shoes. Minor grinding, added padding, or a tweak to the posting is a normal part of the process. If you notice sharp pain, blisters, numbness, or pain spreading into the ankle or knee as you break them in, call the office — that usually means an adjustment is needed.
