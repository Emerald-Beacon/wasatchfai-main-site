Flat Feet vs. High Arches: Comparing Functional Limitations

Flat feet and high arches cause opposite problems — but both can be managed with the right support once you know what you're dealing with.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Flat feet (pes planus) and high arches (pes cavus) sit at opposite ends of the same spectrum, and each creates its own functional limitations. Flat feet tend to roll inward too much, straining the arch and surrounding joints, while high arches are too rigid to absorb shock, concentrating pressure on the heel and ball of the foot. Both are manageable — usually without surgery — once they're properly identified.

A healthy foot goes through a specific sequence with every step: it absorbs impact, flattens slightly to adapt to the ground, then firms back up to push off efficiently. Flat feet and high arches disrupt that sequence in different directions, which is why the two conditions cause such different symptoms and require different approaches to treatment.

What are flat feet, and how do they limit movement?

Flat feet occur when the arch collapses toward the ground, either because it never fully developed or because supporting structures — most often the posterior tibial tendon — have weakened over time. With flat feet, your foot rolls inward too much as you walk (a pattern called overpronation), which can allow your ankles, knees, and hips to fall out of proper alignment.

Common causes include posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, prior ankle injuries, prolonged standing, excess body weight, or underlying neuromuscular conditions. Functionally, flat feet are associated with:

  • Fatigue during walking or standing, since the arch isn't doing its share of the shock-absorbing work
  • Weak push-off strength when walking or running
  • Achy, tired feet by the end of the day
  • Balance difficulty on uneven surfaces
  • Increased stress transmitted up into the knees and hips

Because the arch bears extra strain, flat feet are also a common contributor to plantar fasciitis and other heel pain.

What are high arches, and how do they limit movement?

High arches are the opposite problem: the arch stays too rigid and doesn't flatten enough to absorb impact or adapt to the ground. With less of the foot in contact with the ground, weight concentrates heavily under the heel and the ball of the foot. High arches are frequently genetic, but can also result from neurological conditions or nerve trauma.

Functional limitations of high arches include:

  • Poor shock absorption, which transmits more impact through the foot and up the leg
  • Pain concentrated under the heel or ball of the foot
  • Instability and a higher risk of ankle sprains, since a rigid, highly arched foot has less natural give
  • Difficulty finding shoes that fit and support the foot properly

How are flat feet and high arches diagnosed?

Both conditions are usually identified through a physical exam that looks at arch shape while standing and sitting, gait analysis to see how the foot moves through each step, and a check of shoe wear patterns, which often reveal uneven pressure distribution. Imaging is occasionally used to evaluate the bones and tendons more closely, especially if a tendon problem or prior injury is suspected.

How are they treated?

Despite being opposite problems, both conditions typically respond well to the same categories of conservative care, tailored to the specific type of arch:

  • Supportive footwear with the right amount of structure for your arch type — motion-control shoes for flat feet, cushioned shoes with flexible soles for high arches
  • Custom orthotics to redistribute pressure and correct alignment — see our custom orthotics options
  • Calf and foot stretching to maintain flexibility and reduce compensatory strain
  • Strengthening exercises for the muscles that support the arch and improve stability
  • Physical therapy for gait retraining when biomechanics are contributing to pain elsewhere in the body

Surgery is only considered in rare, severe cases — for example, significant posterior tibial tendon damage or a structural deformity that hasn't responded to conservative treatment — and is always a last resort after nonsurgical options have been tried.

When should you see a podiatrist?

Not every flat foot or high arch causes problems, and many people go their whole lives without needing treatment. See a podiatrist if foot pain, ankle instability, or knee and hip discomfort starts interfering with your daily activities, or if you notice your arch changing shape over time, which can signal tendon involvement. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices for a full biomechanical evaluation.

Common Questions

Flat feet & high arches FAQs

Can flat feet or high arches cause knee and hip pain?

Yes. Both conditions change how force travels up through the leg with each step. Flat feet often let the ankles, knees, and hips fall out of alignment as the foot rolls inward too much, while high arches transmit more shock upward due to poor absorption. Either pattern can lead to compensatory pain in the knees, hips, or lower back over time.

Do flat feet and high arches affect balance differently?

Yes. Flat feet tend to cause balance difficulty on uneven surfaces because the collapsed arch provides less structural support. High arches cause instability because the rigid foot has less natural give and a smaller contact area with the ground, which raises the risk of ankle sprains.

Do orthotics actually help flat feet and high arches?

Custom orthotics are one of the most commonly recommended and effective conservative treatments for both conditions. They redistribute pressure, support the arch appropriately for its type, and help correct alignment issues that contribute to pain in the foot, knees, or hips.

What exercises help with flat feet or high arches?

Calf stretches and exercises that strengthen the small stabilizing muscles of the foot and ankle can improve stability and mobility for both conditions. A physical therapist can tailor a program based on your specific arch type and any gait issues identified during an evaluation.

Is flat feet or high arches worse for you?

Neither is inherently worse — they simply create different functional limitations. Flat feet are associated with overpronation, fatigue, and increased knee and hip stress, while high arches are associated with poor shock absorption and a higher risk of ankle instability and sprains. Both are manageable with the right conservative care.

When should I see a podiatrist about my arch type?

See a podiatrist if foot, ankle, knee, or hip symptoms start interfering with your daily activities, or if your arch appears to be changing shape over time. A biomechanical evaluation can confirm your arch type and identify the best combination of footwear, orthotics, and exercises for you.
