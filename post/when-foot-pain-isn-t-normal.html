When Foot Pain Isn't Normal

Sore feet after a long day are common. Pain that lingers, worsens, or changes how you walk is your body telling you something else is going on.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

It's normal to feel sore after a hard workout or a long day on your feet. It is not normal for that pain to stick around, get worse, or start interfering with how you walk and move through your day. Persistent foot pain is your body's way of flagging a problem — from an overworked tendon to a stress fracture — that usually responds well to treatment when it's caught early.

Feet take an enormous amount of abuse. Between the two of them, your feet strike the ground thousands of times a day, absorbing several times your body weight with every step. Some aching is expected. The trick is learning to tell ordinary fatigue apart from pain that means something is actually wrong — and knowing when it's time to have it looked at.

What does the type of pain tell you?

The character of the pain itself is often the first clue. Sharp, stabbing pain in the heel that's worst with your first steps out of bed is a classic sign of plantar fasciitis, one of the most common causes of heel pain we see. Burning, tingling, or a feeling like there's a pebble in your shoe between the toes can point to nerve irritation, such as a neuroma. Dull, aching pain that builds gradually and worsens with activity is more typical of tendon or joint problems, while pain that comes on suddenly and sharply — especially after an injury — deserves prompt attention.

Location matters as much as timing

Where the pain shows up narrows things down further. Forefoot pain, especially with activity, often suggests a metatarsal stress injury from repetitive impact. Pain in the middle of the foot can point to a tendon or ligament injury, including problems with the posterior tibial tendon that supports your arch. Heel pain is frequently plantar fasciitis, though it can also come from a stress fracture or nerve irritation in the area. Pairing the location of the pain with when it happens — first thing in the morning, mid-run, or at the end of a long shift — gives your provider a much clearer picture of what's driving it.

Common causes worth knowing about

A number of everyday conditions are behind most of the foot pain we treat. Plantar fasciitis alone affects a meaningful share of adults at some point, and it tends to improve quickly with early conservative care. Left untreated, it — like Achilles tendinopathy and structural issues such as flat feet — can gradually worsen and become harder to resolve. Other frequent culprits include:

  • Stress fractures from repetitive impact, especially in runners and athletes
  • Gout, which causes sudden, intense joint pain, often at the base of the big toe
  • Rheumatoid arthritis and other inflammatory arthritis, which can cause swelling and stiffness in multiple joints
  • Diabetic neuropathy, which can mask pain and make small injuries easy to miss

When foot pain needs immediate attention

Most foot pain can wait for a scheduled appointment, but a handful of signs mean you should be seen right away. Seek urgent care if you notice:

  • Inability to bear any weight on the foot
  • Visible deformity after an injury
  • Redness or swelling that is spreading rapidly
  • Fever alongside foot pain or swelling
  • A foot that feels unusually cold, looks pale, or has gone numb

These can be signs of a fracture, a severe infection, or a circulation problem — all of which are far easier to treat the sooner they're addressed.

How is foot pain treated?

The right treatment depends entirely on the diagnosis, which is why an accurate exam matters more than guessing based on symptoms alone. Most cases respond well to conservative therapy, including:

  • Activity modification and rest to let irritated tissue calm down
  • Supportive footwear and custom orthotics to correct how weight loads through the foot
  • Physical therapy and targeted stretching
  • Medication to manage pain and inflammation

When conservative care isn't enough, in-office and minimally invasive procedures can often resolve stubborn cases without open surgery, which is reserved for a small number of patients who don't improve with other treatment.

When should you see a specialist?

See a podiatrist if pain persists beyond a week or two of rest and home care, keeps getting worse instead of better, or interferes with your normal daily activities. The earlier a foot problem is diagnosed, the simpler — and often faster — the treatment tends to be. Common Questions

Foot pain FAQs

How do I know if my foot pain is serious?

Foot pain is more likely to be serious if it lasts longer than a week or two, keeps getting worse, wakes you up at night, or changes the way you walk. Pain accompanied by swelling, redness, fever, numbness, or an inability to bear weight should be evaluated promptly.

Why does my heel hurt most in the morning?

Sharp heel pain with your first steps after waking is a classic sign of plantar fasciitis. The tissue tightens overnight and is stretched suddenly when you stand, which is why the pain is often worst right when you get out of bed and eases as you move around.

Can foot pain be a sign of a stress fracture?

Yes. Stress fractures often cause a dull, aching pain in the forefoot or midfoot that builds gradually with activity and improves with rest. They're common in runners and people who suddenly increase their activity level, and they can worsen without treatment.

Is it normal for both feet to hurt at the same time?

Pain in both feet at once can happen with overuse, but it can also point to an inflammatory condition such as rheumatoid arthritis, gout, or diabetic neuropathy. If both feet hurt without an obvious cause like a hard workout, it's worth having it evaluated.

Should I keep exercising through foot pain?

Mild soreness that fades quickly is usually fine to work through, but pain that lingers, sharpens, or changes your gait is a signal to scale back and let the area rest. Continuing to push through that kind of pain often makes the underlying problem worse.

When should I see a podiatrist for foot pain?

See a podiatrist if pain lasts more than a week or two of rest and home care, keeps worsening, or gets in the way of daily activities. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
