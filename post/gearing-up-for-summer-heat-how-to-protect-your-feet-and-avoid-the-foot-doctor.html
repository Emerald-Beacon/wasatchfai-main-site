Gearing Up for Summer Heat: How to Protect Your Feet and Avoid the Foot Doctor

Sandals, sunburn, swelling, and pool-floor fungus — summer brings its own set of foot risks. Here's how to protect your feet all season long.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Summer brings more time outdoors, more sandals, more pool decks, and more sudden jumps in activity — and each of those brings its own set of foot risks. A little preparation goes a long way toward keeping your feet comfortable and healthy all season, instead of landing you in the podiatrist's office.

We see a predictable rise in certain foot complaints every summer. Here's what to watch for and how to protect your feet through the heat, the hikes, and the pool days ahead.

Choose supportive summer footwear

Flat, unsupportive sandals and flip-flops are comfortable but offer very little shock absorption or arch support, and wearing them for hours at a time can contribute to heel and arch pain. If you're going to be on your feet for a while — at an outdoor event, on vacation, or just running errands — look for sandals with real arch support and a secure heel strap, and save the minimal ones for short trips to the mailbox.

Protect your feet from sunburn

The tops of the feet are an easy spot to forget when applying sunscreen, especially in sandals, and sunburned feet can be surprisingly uncomfortable given how much walking they need to do. Apply sunscreen to exposed skin on your feet just as you would your arms or face, and reapply after swimming or sweating.

Watch for blisters from new activity

A sudden jump in summer activity — new hiking boots, more miles, new sports — is a common source of blisters and overuse injuries like Achilles tendonitis. Break in new footwear gradually, wear moisture-wicking socks, and increase activity levels over time rather than all at once. If a blister does form, keep it clean and covered, and avoid popping it if you can help it.

Manage heat-related swelling

Heat causes blood vessels to dilate, which can lead to fluid retention and mild swelling in the feet and ankles, especially with long periods of standing, sitting, or travel. Staying hydrated, moving regularly, and elevating your feet when you can helps. Swelling that's sudden, significant, or affects only one foot should be evaluated rather than assumed to be heat-related.

Avoid athlete's foot and other fungal infections

Pool decks, locker rooms, and shared showers are prime spots for fungal infections like athlete's foot, which thrive in warm, damp environments. Wear sandals in these areas instead of going barefoot, and dry your feet thoroughly afterward — especially between the toes, where moisture lingers.

Take extra care if you have diabetes

Summer activities — walking barefoot on hot pavement, sand, or pool decks — carry added risk if you have diabetes or reduced sensation in your feet, since minor injuries can go unnoticed. Check your feet daily for cuts, blisters, or redness, wear protective footwear even around the house and yard, and see our diabetic foot care guidance for more detail.

When to see a podiatrist

See a podiatrist if you develop a blister or wound that looks infected, foot pain that doesn't improve after a few days of rest, persistent swelling, or any foot injury if you have diabetes or circulation problems. A little attention early in the summer goes a long way toward avoiding bigger problems later. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Summer foot care FAQs

Are sandals bad for your feet?

Flat, unsupportive sandals worn for long periods can contribute to arch and heel pain because they offer little shock absorption or structural support. Occasional wear is fine for most people, but if you're on your feet for hours, choose sandals with good arch support and a secure heel strap.

Why do my feet swell more in summer heat?

Heat causes blood vessels to dilate and can lead to fluid retention in the lower legs and feet, especially with long periods of standing or sitting. Staying hydrated, elevating your feet when possible, and avoiding tight footwear can help. Sudden or one-sided swelling should be evaluated, since it can signal something beyond simple heat swelling.

How do I avoid athlete's foot at the pool or gym?

Athlete's foot thrives in warm, damp environments like pool decks, locker rooms, and shared showers. Wear sandals in these areas instead of going barefoot, dry your feet thoroughly afterward — especially between the toes — and change out of wet swimsuits and socks promptly.

What should I do about a summer blister?

Keep small, intact blisters covered with a clean bandage and avoid the friction that caused it. Don't pop a blister if you can avoid it, since the intact skin protects against infection. See a podiatrist if a blister looks infected, is very large or painful, or if you have diabetes or poor circulation.

Do I need sunscreen on my feet?

Yes — the tops of the feet are a commonly missed spot for sunburn, especially in sandals. Apply sunscreen to any exposed skin on your feet just as you would elsewhere, and reapply after swimming or sweating.
