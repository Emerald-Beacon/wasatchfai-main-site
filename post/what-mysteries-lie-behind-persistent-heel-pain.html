What's Behind Persistent Heel Pain?

Heel pain that lingers week after week rarely has just one cause. Here's how our podiatrists narrow down what's really going on — and what actually works to fix it.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Persistent heel pain is rarely random — it almost always traces back to a specific structure in the heel that's being overloaded, whether that's the plantar fascia, the Achilles tendon, a bursa, or the heel bone itself. Understanding which structure is involved is the key to actually resolving the pain, rather than just managing it flare-up by flare-up.

The heel takes an enormous amount of impact every day — absorbing your body weight with every step, run, and jump. It's built from the calcaneus (heel bone) along with a network of tendons, ligaments, and soft tissue that support that load. When any of those structures is repeatedly overused or strained, inflammation and small tears can develop, and the result is pain that seems to show up out of nowhere and then refuses to go away.

What's actually causing your heel pain?

Several different conditions can produce heel pain, and they don't always feel the same. Common culprits include:

  • Plantar fasciitis — irritation of the thick band of tissue on the bottom of the foot; pain is typically sharpest with the first steps in the morning
  • Achilles tendonitis — inflammation of the tendon at the back of the heel, common among runners and athletes
  • Heel spurs — bony growths that can develop alongside plantar fasciitis, though many people with spurs feel no pain at all
  • Heel bursitis — inflammation of the small fluid-filled sac that cushions the heel bone
  • Stress fractures — tiny cracks in the heel bone from repetitive impact, especially in runners
  • Nerve impingement — a pinched or irritated nerve near the heel that can cause burning or tingling pain

What triggers persistent heel pain?

Because the heel is a weight-bearing structure, anything that adds repetitive stress to it can trigger or worsen pain. Frequent triggers include:

  • Running, hiking, or other high-impact activity, especially with a sudden increase in mileage
  • Prolonged standing or walking on hard surfaces
  • Unsupportive or worn-out footwear
  • Jobs that keep you on your feet for long stretches
  • Structural factors like flat feet or high arches, which change how weight loads through the heel

How is persistent heel pain diagnosed?

Our podiatrists start with a physical exam and a detailed history — when the pain started, what makes it better or worse, and your activity level. We assess tenderness, swelling, and range of motion, and we may evaluate your gait and foot strike pattern to identify biomechanical contributors. X-rays or MRI are sometimes used to rule out a stress fracture, confirm a heel spur, or get a clearer look at tendon and soft-tissue damage.

How is persistent heel pain treated?

Treatment is tailored to the underlying cause, but most heel pain responds well to conservative care, especially when addressed early.

Nonsurgical treatment

  • Physical therapy with targeted stretching and strengthening exercises
  • Custom orthotics to provide support specific to your foot structure
  • Supportive footwear chosen for your activity and foot type
  • Ice and NSAIDs to calm acute inflammation
  • Activity modification to reduce repetitive strain while healing

Advanced interventions

For heel pain that doesn't respond to first-line care, options include corticosteroid injections, PRP (platelet-rich plasma) injections, and extracorporeal shock wave therapy, which uses sound-wave energy to stimulate healing without incisions. Surgery — such as a plantar fascia release or heel spur removal — is considered a last resort, generally reserved for cases that haven't improved after an extended course of conservative treatment, often around six months.

How to prevent heel pain from coming back

Once heel pain resolves, a few habits go a long way toward keeping it that way:

  • Stretch your calves and plantar fascia daily
  • Replace running or walking shoes every 300–500 miles
  • Avoid walking barefoot on hard surfaces
  • Alternate high-impact activity with lower-impact exercise
  • Maintain a healthy weight to reduce ongoing strain on the heel

If your heel pain has lasted more than a week or two, keeps returning, or is limiting your daily activity, it's worth getting a proper diagnosis rather than guessing at the cause. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Persistent heel pain FAQs

What is the most common cause of heel pain?

Plantar fasciitis is the most frequent cause of heel pain, particularly among active individuals. It typically causes sharp pain near the bottom of the heel that is worst with your first steps in the morning or after sitting for a while.

What's the difference between a heel spur and plantar fasciitis?

A heel spur is a bony growth that can develop where the plantar fascia attaches to the heel bone, while plantar fasciitis is inflammation of the fascia itself. Heel spurs often accompany plantar fasciitis but don't always cause pain on their own — imaging helps tell the two apart.

Which specialist should I see for persistent heel pain?

A podiatrist or foot and ankle specialist is the right provider to assess persistent heel pain, identify the underlying cause, and recommend a treatment plan tailored to your symptoms and activity level.

Do home remedies work for heel pain?

Many mild cases improve with rest, stretching, supportive shoes, and over-the-counter pain relievers. However, if pain persists beyond a couple of weeks despite home care, a professional evaluation is recommended to confirm the cause and avoid it becoming a chronic problem.

Are custom orthotics worth it for heel pain?

For many patients, yes. Custom orthotics are built to your specific foot structure and can provide longer-term relief than off-the-shelf inserts by improving alignment and reducing stress on the structures contributing to your heel pain.

When should I see a podiatrist for heel pain?

See a podiatrist if your heel pain lasts more than a week or two, keeps returning, or limits your normal activities. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
