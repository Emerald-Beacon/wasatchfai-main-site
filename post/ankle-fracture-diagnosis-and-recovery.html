Ankle Fracture Diagnosis and Recovery

An ankle fracture can turn simple steps into a painful ordeal. Here's how we diagnose the different types, treat them, and what a realistic recovery timeline looks like.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

An ankle fracture is a break in one or more of the bones that form the ankle joint, and it can turn simple steps into a painful ordeal. Fractures range from a single, simple break that heals with a boot or cast to complex, unstable injuries that require surgical fixation. Prompt evaluation matters — an ankle fracture that is not properly diagnosed and stabilized can shift, heal incorrectly, or lead to long-term joint problems.

Whether you rolled your ankle on a hike, came down wrong after a jump, or took a fall on the ice, our podiatrists in Farmington and South Ogden see ankle fractures regularly. Below, we walk through the types of fractures, how we tell a fracture from a sprain, treatment options, and what recovery typically involves.

What types of ankle fractures are there?

Ankle fractures are generally described along two spectrums:

  • Simple vs. complex: A simple fracture involves a single bone, while a complex fracture involves multiple bones or joint surfaces around the ankle.
  • Stable vs. unstable: A stable fracture stays properly aligned and can heal with immobilization alone. An unstable fracture is at risk of shifting out of position and often requires surgery to hold the bones in place while they heal.

The ligaments around the ankle also play a role — significant ligament injury alongside a fracture can make an otherwise simple break behave like an unstable one.

How is an ankle fracture diagnosed?

Diagnosis generally moves through a few steps:

  1. Physical examination — your podiatrist checks for swelling, bruising, deformity, tenderness over specific bones, and how much weight you can bear on the ankle.
  2. Imaging — X-rays confirm most fractures and show alignment. For more complex injuries, a CT or MRI scan may be used to get a more detailed look at the joint surfaces and any associated ligament damage.
  3. Differentiating from a sprain — because sprains and fractures can feel similar at first, imaging is often the only way to tell the difference with confidence, which is why any ankle injury with significant swelling, bruising, or inability to bear weight should be evaluated.

How are ankle fractures treated?

Treatment depends on whether the fracture is stable or unstable:

Stable fractures

Stable fractures typically heal with immobilization in a walking boot or cast, keeping the bones aligned while new bone forms. Your podiatrist will monitor healing with follow-up X-rays and adjust your activity level as the bone strengthens.

Unstable or displaced fractures

Unstable or displaced fractures often require surgical fixation — plates, screws, or other hardware are used to hold the bones in the correct position while they heal. Surgery is generally recommended when bones are out of alignment, when multiple bones are involved, or when ligament damage makes the joint unstable.

What does recovery from an ankle fracture look like?

Six weeks to several months is typical for healing, and the exact timeline depends on the severity of the fracture and the treatment approach used. Recovery generally moves through stages: initial immobilization to protect the healing bone, followed by individualized physical therapy focused on restoring range of motion, strength, and stability.

Rehabilitation is an important part of recovery, not an afterthought — untreated stiffness or weakness after an ankle fracture can contribute to long-term problems, including arthritis in the joint. Your podiatrist will tailor your therapy plan to your specific fracture and how your ankle is healing.

When should you see a podiatrist for an ankle injury?

See a podiatrist promptly for any ankle injury involving significant swelling, bruising, deformity, or inability to bear weight — these signs can point to a fracture rather than a simple sprain. Getting an accurate diagnosis early allows treatment to start before the injury has a chance to worsen or heal out of position. When you are ready, you can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Ankle fracture FAQs

How can I tell if my ankle is fractured or just sprained?

Sprains and fractures can feel very similar at first, both causing pain, swelling, and bruising. Because they can be hard to tell apart without imaging, any ankle injury with significant swelling, bruising, or inability to bear weight should be evaluated with an X-ray to rule out a fracture.

Do all ankle fractures need surgery?

No. Stable fractures, where the bones remain properly aligned, typically heal with a walking boot or cast. Surgery is generally reserved for unstable or displaced fractures, where hardware is needed to hold the bones in the correct position while they heal.

How long does it take to recover from an ankle fracture?

Six weeks to several months is typical, depending on the severity of the fracture and the treatment approach. Recovery includes an initial period of immobilization followed by physical therapy to restore range of motion, strength, and stability.

Why is physical therapy important after an ankle fracture?

After a period of immobilization, the ankle can lose range of motion and strength. Individualized physical therapy helps restore normal movement and stability, and it reduces the risk of long-term complications such as arthritis in the joint.

What imaging is used to diagnose an ankle fracture?

X-rays confirm most ankle fractures and show how the bones are aligned. For more complex injuries, a CT or MRI scan may be used to get a more detailed look at the joint surfaces and any associated ligament damage.

When should I see a podiatrist after an ankle injury?

See a podiatrist promptly if you have significant swelling, bruising, visible deformity, or cannot bear weight on the ankle after an injury. Early evaluation allows treatment to start before the injury worsens. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
