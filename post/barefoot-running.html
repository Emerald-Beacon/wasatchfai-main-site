Barefoot and Minimalist Running: Benefits, Risks, and How to Transition Safely

Ditching cushioned shoes can strengthen your feet — but rushing the transition is one of the fastest ways to end up in our office. Here's how to weigh the trade-offs.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Barefoot and minimalist running can strengthen small foot muscles and improve balance for some athletes, but it also raises the risk of Achilles tendon strain, stress fractures, and cuts or blisters. Most runners who want the benefits should transition gradually and cautiously, ideally with guidance from a podiatrist, rather than switching all at once.

Barefoot and minimalist running have built a loyal following over the past couple of decades, with proponents arguing that heavily cushioned running shoes weaken the small muscles and structures of the foot over time. It is an appealing idea — but it is also one that carries real injury risk if you approach it the wrong way. Here is what our podiatrists want you to know before you kick off your shoes.

What is barefoot and minimalist running?

True barefoot running means exactly what it sounds like — running with no footwear at all. Minimalist running is the more common middle ground: thin-soled, low-drop shoes that let the foot move and flex more naturally while still offering some protection from the ground. Both approaches are built on the theory that running in thick, structured shoes changes your natural gait and reduces the workload on the small muscles that stabilize your foot and ankle.

What are the potential benefits?

Some runners who make the switch report better balance, improved proprioception (your body's sense of where your foot is in space), and stronger intrinsic foot muscles. Running without a built-up heel can also encourage a shorter stride and a more forefoot- or midfoot-oriented landing, which some research has suggested may improve running efficiency for certain runners. That said, these benefits are not universal, and they come with meaningful trade-offs.

What are the risks?

Barefoot and minimalist running is not without downsides, and it is not something any podiatrist would recommend as a first-line solution for foot pain. Common risks include:

  • Increased strain on the Achilles tendon and calf, which can contribute to Achilles tendonitis
  • A higher risk of stress fractures in the metatarsals during the adjustment period
  • Cuts, punctures, and blisters from direct ground contact
  • A significant adjustment period as your feet, tendons, and gait adapt
  • Added strain for runners who already have flat feet, high arches, or an unstable gait

Because the benefits are modest and the injury risk is real, most foot and ankle specialists do not recommend barefoot running as a treatment for existing foot pain. If you are dealing with pain, a plantar fasciitis flare-up, or another chronic condition, the right first step is usually supportive footwear or custom orthotics, not removing structure from your shoe.

How to transition safely, if you choose to

If you are a healthy runner who wants to try minimalist shoes, the key is patience. Rushing the transition is the single biggest cause of barefoot-running injuries.

  • Start with very short distances — a few minutes at a time — on a soft, even surface like grass or a track
  • Increase your minimalist mileage by no more than about 10% per week
  • Pay close attention to your calves and Achilles tendon, which absorb much more load without a cushioned heel
  • Keep your traditional running shoes in rotation rather than switching over completely
  • Stop and reassess at the first sign of new or worsening foot, ankle, or calf pain

Who should avoid barefoot running?

Barefoot and minimalist running is not a good fit for everyone. Runners with diabetes or any condition that affects circulation or sensation in the feet should avoid going barefoot, since minor cuts or blisters can go unnoticed and become serious. It is also not advisable for people with a history of stress fractures, Achilles tendon problems, flat feet, or other structural foot issues without first talking to a podiatrist. If you are unsure whether your feet and gait can tolerate the transition, we can evaluate your biomechanics and help you decide what is realistic and safe.

Common Questions

Barefoot running FAQs

Does barefoot running actually strengthen your feet?

It can. Running without heavy cushioning forces the small stabilizing muscles in your foot and ankle to work harder, which may improve strength and balance over time. The catch is that this same lack of cushioning is what raises the risk of strain injuries during the adjustment period.

What shoes should I use for barefoot or minimalist running?

Most runners who want to try this approach start with thin-soled, low-drop minimalist shoes rather than going fully barefoot. These shoes still protect the sole of the foot from cuts and debris while allowing a more natural range of motion than a traditional cushioned running shoe.

How long does it take to transition to barefoot running?

A safe transition typically takes several weeks to a few months. Runners should increase minimalist mileage gradually — no more than about 10% per week — while paying close attention to how the calves, Achilles tendon, and feet respond.

Is barefoot running bad for your Achilles tendon?

It can be if you transition too quickly. Without a cushioned heel, the Achilles tendon and calf absorb significantly more load with every step, which is why Achilles strain and tendonitis are among the most common injuries linked to barefoot and minimalist running.

Who should avoid barefoot or minimalist running?

People with diabetes or reduced sensation in the feet, a history of stress fractures or Achilles problems, flat feet, or other structural foot issues should avoid going barefoot without first talking to a podiatrist about whether it is appropriate for them.

Can I use orthotics with minimalist shoes?

Generally, no — minimalist shoes are designed to let the foot move naturally, and adding a rigid orthotic defeats that purpose. If you need arch support or correction for a biomechanical issue, a traditional supportive shoe with custom orthotics is usually the better choice.
