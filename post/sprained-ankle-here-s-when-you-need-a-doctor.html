Sprained Ankle? Here's When You Need a Doctor

Not every rolled ankle needs a trip to the doctor — but some do. Here's how to tell the difference and avoid the chronic pain and instability that come from an untreated sprain.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Most ankle sprains heal on their own with rest, ice, and time. But certain warning signs — severe swelling, an inability to bear weight, visible deformity, or pain that isn't improving after a week — mean it's time to see a doctor. Ignoring a serious sprain doesn't just delay healing; it can lead to chronic instability and repeated sprains down the road.

An ankle sprain happens when the ligaments that stabilize the ankle joint are stretched or torn, most often from rolling or twisting the foot. While mild sprains are common and usually resolve with home care, it's important to know when a sprain is serious enough to need professional evaluation.

What are the levels of ankle sprain severity?

Ankle sprains are generally classified into three grades:

  • Mild (Grade I): Slight stretching and microscopic tearing of the ligament fibers, with mild tenderness and swelling.
  • Moderate (Grade II): Partial tearing of the ligament, with moderate pain, swelling, and some looseness in the joint.
  • Severe (Grade III): Complete tear of the ligament, with significant swelling, bruising, and instability.

Grade I sprains often improve with home care alone, while Grade II and III sprains typically need medical evaluation to heal properly and avoid lateral ankle instability down the road.

What are the warning signs you need a doctor?

See a podiatrist promptly if you notice any of the following after an ankle injury:

  • Severe swelling that develops quickly or continues to worsen
  • Pain that prevents you from bearing weight or walking normally
  • Visible deformity, which can suggest a dislocation or fracture
  • A popping sensation at the time of injury, which can indicate a ligament tear
  • Persistent pain that lasts beyond two to three days
  • Numbness or tingling in the foot, suggesting nerve involvement
  • Circulation problems — a foot that looks pale, blue, or feels cold
  • Chronic instability, with the ankle repeatedly "giving way"
  • No improvement after a full week of rest and home care

Any of these signs mean it's time for an evaluation rather than continuing to wait it out at home.

How is a sprained ankle diagnosed?

Diagnosis typically starts with a physical examination to assess swelling, tenderness, and range of motion. X-rays are often used to rule out a fracture, since some breaks can feel similar to a bad sprain. If ligament damage is suspected but not clear on X-ray, an MRI may be ordered to get a detailed look at the soft tissue.

How is a sprained ankle treated?

Treatment depends on the severity of the sprain. Mild sprains typically respond to R.I.C.E. (rest, ice, compression, elevation), over-the-counter pain relief, and a gradual return to activity. Moderate and severe sprains may require a walking boot or brace to protect the joint, physical therapy to rebuild strength and stability, and in some cases, further imaging to rule out associated injuries. Left untreated, a significant sprain can lead to chronic pain, recurring sprains, and long-term instability in the joint.

If your ankle sprain isn't improving, or if you noticed any of the warning signs above, don't wait it out. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices for a prompt evaluation.

Common Questions

Sprained ankle FAQs

How do I know if my ankle sprain is severe?

Signs of a severe sprain include significant swelling and bruising, an inability to bear weight, visible deformity, and a feeling of instability in the joint. A popping sensation at the time of injury can also point to a more serious ligament tear. Any of these signs warrant a medical evaluation.

Should I go to the emergency room for a sprained ankle?

The emergency room is appropriate if you have visible deformity, cannot bear any weight, notice numbness or tingling, or your foot looks pale, blue, or feels cold — these can point to a fracture or circulation problem. For less urgent but persistent symptoms, a podiatrist visit is usually the right next step.

How long does a sprained ankle take to heal?

Mild sprains often improve within one to two weeks. Moderate sprains can take several weeks, and severe, complete ligament tears may take a few months to fully heal, sometimes with physical therapy. Getting an accurate diagnosis early helps you plan realistic recovery expectations.

Can I walk on a sprained ankle?

It depends on the severity. Mild sprains often allow some weight-bearing as tolerated, while moderate to severe sprains may require a walking boot or brace to protect the joint. If putting weight on your ankle causes significant pain, avoid walking on it and have it evaluated.

What happens if I ignore a severe ankle sprain?

Ignoring a severe sprain can lead to chronic pain, ongoing instability, and repeated sprains, since the ligament may not heal in a way that properly supports the joint. Over time this can develop into lateral ankle instability, which is harder to treat the longer it goes unaddressed.
