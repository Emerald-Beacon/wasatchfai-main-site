Stretching Your Limits: Exercises for Optimal Foot Health

Your feet carry you through tens of thousands of steps a day — a few minutes of targeted stretching and strengthening can go a long way toward keeping them flexible, strong, and pain-free.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Regular exercise is an important, and often overlooked, component of foot care. A handful of simple stretches and strengthening moves — done consistently — can improve flexibility, build strength in the small stabilizing muscles of the foot, and support better balance. These exercises are a great habit for anyone, but they work best as part of a broader foot-health routine, not as a substitute for evaluation when pain is already present.

Below, our podiatrists walk through five foot exercises we frequently recommend, why each one helps, and how to tell when foot pain has moved beyond what stretching alone can fix.

Why does foot exercise matter?

The foot is a complex structure of 26 bones, dozens of joints, and a network of small muscles, tendons, and ligaments that all work together to absorb impact and keep you balanced with every step. Like any other part of the body, those muscles and connective tissues benefit from regular, gentle conditioning. Building flexibility and strength in the feet and ankles can help improve balance, support better walking and running mechanics, and make the foot more resilient to the everyday stress of standing, walking, and exercise.

Five exercises for stronger, healthier feet

1. Toe curls

While seated or standing, curl your toes inward as tightly and comfortably as possible, hold briefly, and then release. This move strengthens the small intrinsic muscles along the bottom of the foot that support your arch. Aim for about 10 repetitions per set.

2. Ankle circles

Lift one foot off the ground and slowly rotate your ankle in a full circle — clockwise, then counterclockwise. Ankle circles improve range of motion and help maintain flexibility in the ankle joint, which is especially useful if you sit for long stretches during the day. Do about 10 rotations in each direction per set.

3. Heel raises

Standing with feet hip-width apart, rise slowly onto the balls of your feet, hold for a moment, and lower back down with control. Heel raises strengthen the calf muscles and Achilles tendon, both of which play a major role in how the foot absorbs force during walking and running. Work up to sets of 10.

4. Towel scrunches

Place a small towel flat on the floor and use only your toes to scrunch it into a ball, then release and reset. This exercise isolates the same intrinsic foot muscles as toe curls and is especially helpful for people rebuilding strength after an injury. Aim for 10 repetitions per set.

5. Arch stretches (ball roll)

Roll a small ball — a tennis ball or dedicated massage ball works well — under your foot for several minutes, applying gentle pressure to the arch as you go. This helps release tension in the plantar fascia and the muscles of the arch, and many patients find it a relaxing way to end the day.

How often should I do these exercises?

Most people benefit from doing this routine a few times a week, and many find it works well as a short warm-up before activity or a cool-down afterward. Consistency matters more than intensity — a few focused minutes done regularly will do more for flexibility and strength than an occasional long session. If you have a specific foot condition such as plantar fasciitis or flat feet, ask your podiatrist which of these exercises are appropriate for you, since certain stretches may need to be modified.

When does foot pain need more than exercise?

These exercises are meant to support general foot health, not treat an existing injury or condition on their own. See a podiatrist if you experience pain that doesn't improve with rest and gentle stretching, sharp or stabbing pain (especially with your first steps in the morning), swelling, numbness or tingling, or any pain that changes the way you walk. People with diabetes or circulation problems should also have new foot symptoms evaluated promptly rather than relying on exercise alone. When you're ready, you can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Foot exercise FAQs

How often should I do foot-strengthening exercises?

Most people benefit from doing a short foot exercise routine a few times a week, whether as a warm-up before activity, a cool-down afterward, or its own standalone habit. Consistency matters more than duration — a few focused minutes on a regular basis is more effective than an occasional long session.

Can these exercises help prevent foot problems?

Building strength and flexibility in the feet and ankles can support better balance and walking mechanics, which may help reduce the risk of certain overuse issues over time. They work best as part of a broader routine that also includes supportive footwear and, when needed, professional evaluation of any existing foot condition.

Are these exercises safe if I already have foot pain?

Gentle stretching can be appropriate for some mild discomfort, but if you have an existing foot condition or ongoing pain, it's best to check with a podiatrist before starting a new exercise routine. Certain stretches may need to be modified depending on your diagnosis, and some conditions need targeted treatment rather than general exercise.

Do I need special equipment for these exercises?

No — most of these exercises require nothing more than a chair, a small towel, and a tennis or massage ball for the arch stretch. They can be done at home in just a few minutes, making them an easy habit to build into a morning or evening routine.

Why do my feet still hurt even though I stretch regularly?

General exercises support overall foot health, but they aren't a treatment for a specific underlying condition. Persistent pain despite regular stretching is a sign that something more specific — such as plantar fasciitis, a stress injury, or a mechanical issue like flat feet — may need a proper evaluation and a targeted treatment plan.

When should I see a podiatrist instead of just stretching?

See a podiatrist if pain doesn't improve with rest and gentle stretching, if you notice sharp or stabbing pain, swelling, numbness, or tingling, or if pain is changing the way you walk. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
