The Season of Ankle Sprains

As trails, courts, and fields fill back up, so does our schedule of ankle sprains. Here's how to recognize one, treat it right, and keep it from happening again.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

An ankle sprain happens when the ankle is forced out of its normal position, stretching or tearing the ligaments that hold the joint together. Sprains are one of the most common injuries we see, especially as people return to running, hiking, and sports after a season indoors — a sudden step onto uneven ground or an awkward landing is often all it takes. Most sprains heal well with prompt, appropriate care, but a sprain that's ignored or treated incorrectly can lead to lingering instability.

Whether you rolled your ankle on a trail, came down wrong after a jump, or twisted it stepping off a curb, knowing what grade of sprain you're dealing with — and what to do in the first few days — makes a real difference in how quickly and completely you recover.

What is an ankle sprain?

An ankle sprain is a ligament injury. The ligaments around the ankle normally keep the joint stable as it moves; when the ankle is forced beyond its normal range — rolling inward is the most common pattern — those ligaments can stretch abnormally or tear. Sprains occur along a spectrum from mild overstretching to a complete tear, which is why two people can describe very different injuries as "just a sprain."

What are the symptoms of an ankle sprain?

Symptoms vary with severity, but common signs include:

  • Tenderness and pain around the ankle, especially on the outside
  • Swelling that develops within minutes to hours
  • Bruising that can spread down into the foot
  • Itching, coldness, or numbness in the foot
  • Stiffness and reduced range of motion

In more severe sprains, people often feel or hear a "pop" at the moment of injury — a sign worth mentioning to your doctor, since it can indicate a more significant tear.

How are ankle sprains graded?

Podiatrists and orthopedists classify sprains into three grades based on how much ligament damage has occurred:

  • Grade 1 (mild): The ligament is overstretched but not torn. Expect mild swelling and soreness, with weight-bearing usually still possible.
  • Grade 2 (moderate): A partial ligament tear, with more noticeable bruising, swelling, and pain that lasts longer.
  • Grade 3 (severe): A complete ligament tear, with significant bruising, swelling, and pain — often making it difficult or impossible to bear weight on the ankle.

Grade 3 sprains, and any sprain that doesn't improve as expected, deserve an in-person evaluation to rule out an associated fracture or chronic lateral ankle instability.

How should I treat an ankle sprain at home?

For mild to moderate sprains, early home care can meaningfully speed recovery:

  • Secure the ankle with a wrap or brace to limit further movement
  • Alternate hot and cold therapy — roughly 30 seconds of heat followed by 2 minutes of cold — to manage pain and swelling
  • Elevate the ankle above heart level when resting to reduce swelling
  • Avoid weight-bearing as needed until pain allows a more normal gait

See a doctor if you can't bear weight on the ankle at all, or if pain and swelling haven't meaningfully improved after 2–3 weeks of home care.

How can I prevent ankle sprains?

A handful of habits go a long way toward keeping your ankles stable through an active season:

  • Stretch and gently roll the ankles before exercise
  • Use an ankle brace or compression sleeve during high-risk activities
  • Wear supportive footwear with good tread for the terrain you're on
  • Build ankle strength with targeted exercises
  • Talk to a podiatrist about the InternalBrace procedure if you experience chronic, repeated sprains

When should I see a podiatrist for an ankle sprain?

See a podiatrist promptly if you cannot bear weight on the ankle, if you heard or felt a pop at the time of injury, or if pain, swelling, or instability persist beyond 2–3 weeks. Repeated sprains on the same ankle are also worth evaluating, since they can signal underlying instability that benefits from a targeted treatment plan. Common Questions

Ankle sprain FAQs

How do I know if my ankle is sprained or broken?

Sprains and fractures can feel similar at first, with pain, swelling, and bruising in both. Signs that point toward a possible fracture include an inability to bear any weight, pain directly over the bone, or visible deformity. If you're unsure, it's safest to have it evaluated and, if needed, imaged.

How long does an ankle sprain take to heal?

Mild sprains often improve within a couple of weeks, while moderate to severe sprains can take several weeks to a few months to fully heal, depending on the grade of the injury and how closely you follow your recovery plan.

Should I wrap or brace a sprained ankle?

Yes, securing the ankle with a wrap or brace in the first days after a sprain helps limit further movement and supports healing. A podiatrist can advise on how long to continue bracing based on the severity of your sprain.

Can I still walk on a sprained ankle?

It depends on the grade. Many mild sprains still allow some weight-bearing, while moderate and severe sprains often make walking painful or impossible. If you can't put weight on the ankle at all, seek medical evaluation.

Why do I keep spraining the same ankle?

Repeated sprains on the same ankle often point to chronic lateral ankle instability, where the ligaments never fully regained their original strength after a previous injury. Targeted strengthening, bracing, or in some cases the InternalBrace procedure can help.

When should I see a doctor for a sprained ankle?

See a podiatrist if you cannot bear weight, felt or heard a pop at the time of injury, or if pain and swelling haven't improved after 2–3 weeks of home care. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
