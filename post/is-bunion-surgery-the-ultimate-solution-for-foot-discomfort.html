Is Bunion Surgery the Ultimate Solution for Foot Discomfort?

Bunions rarely go away on their own — but that doesn't mean surgery is your only option. Here's how to know when conservative care is enough, and when it's time to consider correction.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

A bunion is a bony bump that forms at the base of the big toe when the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint shifts out of alignment, gradually pushing the big toe toward the second toe. Surgery can correct the deformity, but it isn't always the first — or only — answer. Many people find real relief with conservative care, and surgery is generally reserved for cases where pain and dysfunction persist despite it.

If you've noticed a growing bump along the inside of your foot, tightness in certain shoes, or pain that changes how you walk, you're dealing with one of the most common forefoot conditions we see. Below, our podiatrists explain what causes bunions, what nonsurgical treatment can (and can't) do, and how to know if surgery is the right next step for you.

What causes a bunion?

Bunions develop when the joint at the base of the big toe becomes misaligned, causing the big toe to angle inward toward the second toe while the joint itself protrudes outward. Several factors contribute to this shift:

  • Inherited foot shape and structure, including how your bones and ligaments are built
  • Biomechanics — how your foot bears weight and moves through each step
  • Footwear that crowds the toes, such as narrow or high-heeled shoes, which can accelerate the process
  • Inflammatory conditions like arthritis, which can contribute to joint misalignment

Once a bunion starts, it typically progresses gradually over years rather than resolving on its own.

How does a bunion affect daily life?

A bunion is more than a cosmetic concern. As the joint shifts, it can change how weight is distributed across the foot, sometimes contributing to secondary problems like hammertoes, calluses, or pain in the ball of the foot. Many patients also change their gait to avoid pressure on the bump, which can lead to knee, hip, or back discomfort over time.

Beyond the physical symptoms, a painful or progressing bunion can limit the shoes you're able to wear and make standing, walking, or exercising less comfortable — which is often what brings people in for an evaluation in the first place.

What nonsurgical treatments help with bunion pain?

For mild to moderate bunions, conservative care can meaningfully reduce pain and slow progression, even though it won't reverse the underlying bone alignment. Options we commonly recommend include:

  • Wider, supportive footwear that doesn't compress the toe joint
  • Custom orthotics to improve foot mechanics and offload pressure — see our custom orthotics options
  • Padding or toe spacers to reduce friction and rubbing against the bump
  • Stretching and strengthening exercises for the foot and toes
  • Anti-inflammatory medication or steroid injections for flare-ups of joint pain

These measures work well for many patients, particularly when the bunion is caught early and footwear changes are made consistently.

When is bunion surgery the right option?

Surgery is generally considered when conservative treatment no longer controls the pain, when the bunion is progressing quickly, or when it's interfering with normal daily activities despite your best efforts at nonsurgical care. Several surgical approaches exist, and the right one depends on the severity of the deformity:

  • Osteotomy — cutting and realigning the bone to correct the joint angle, used for mild to moderate bunions
  • Lapidus procedure — a fusion at the base of the metatarsal, typically reserved for more severe or unstable deformities; learn more about our Lapiplasty approach
  • Minimally invasive bunion surgery — correction through small incisions, which can mean less soft-tissue disruption for appropriate candidates

Recovery varies by procedure and by patient, generally spanning six weeks to several months. Most people start in a protective boot, transition to supportive shoes, and add physical therapy as healing progresses, with a gradual return to normal activity over the following months. Most patients report significant, lasting improvement in pain and function after surgery, though outcomes depend on the severity of the deformity and how closely post-operative guidance is followed.

So, is surgery the "ultimate" solution?

Surgery is a highly effective option when it's the right fit — but it's a decision, not a default. For many patients, especially those with mild bunions caught early, conservative management provides lasting relief without an operation. The best approach is the one matched to how far the bunion has progressed, how much it's limiting you, and how it responds to the treatments you've already tried. A hands-on evaluation is the only reliable way to know which path makes sense for your foot. When you're ready, you can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Bunion surgery FAQs

Can a bunion go away without surgery?

The bony deformity itself will not reverse without surgery, but pain and irritation can often be well managed with wider shoes, custom orthotics, padding, and other conservative measures — especially when the bunion is caught early.

How do I know if my bunion needs surgery?

Surgery is usually considered when pain continues despite consistent conservative care, when the bunion is progressing quickly, or when it's limiting your daily activities. A podiatrist can evaluate the severity of your bunion and help you weigh your options.

What's the difference between an osteotomy and the Lapidus procedure?

An osteotomy cuts and realigns the toe bone and is typically used for mild to moderate bunions. The Lapidus procedure fuses the joint at the base of the metatarsal and is generally reserved for more severe or unstable deformities.

How long is recovery after bunion surgery?

Recovery typically ranges from six weeks to several months depending on the procedure. Most patients start in a protective boot, progress to supportive footwear, and add physical therapy as healing allows, with a gradual return to full activity.

Will my bunion come back after surgery?

Recurrence is uncommon when the appropriate procedure is chosen for the severity of the deformity and post-operative guidance is followed, though no surgery can guarantee it will never return. Supportive footwear afterward can help protect your results.

Should I see a podiatrist even if my bunion isn't painful yet?

Yes — an early evaluation can help slow progression with footwear and orthotic changes before the bunion becomes more painful or limits your options. It's easier to manage a bunion in its early stages than after it has significantly progressed.
