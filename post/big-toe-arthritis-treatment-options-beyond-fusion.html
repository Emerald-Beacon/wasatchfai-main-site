Big-Toe Arthritis: Treatment Options Beyond Fusion

Stiffness and pain at the base of the big toe don't always lead to a fused joint. There's a whole ladder of treatment to climb first — and plenty of room to stay active along the way.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated October 22, 2026

Big-toe arthritis can usually be managed for years without fusing the joint. Stiffer-soled shoes, a well-made orthotic, activity tweaks, and the occasional injection carry many people a long way. When those stop holding the pain back, a joint-sparing operation that cleans out the arthritic bone — a cheilectomy — often preserves useful motion. Fusion is a durable, reliable option, but it sits near the top of the ladder, not at the bottom.

The medical name for arthritis in the big toe is hallux rigidus, which literally means "stiff big toe." It affects the joint where the big toe meets the foot, the first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint — the hinge you bend every time you push off a step. As the smooth cartilage on the ends of those bones wears down, the joint grows stiff, aches, and often sprouts a bony ridge on top that you can feel through the skin. Because that one joint does so much work, even mild arthritis there can make walking, squatting, and climbing stairs miserable. The good news is that treatment is a staircase, and most people never reach the top step.

What big-toe arthritis actually is

Hallux rigidus is wear-and-tear (osteoarthritis) of that first MTP joint. It can follow an old injury, run in families, or simply arrive with the mileage of a few decades of walking. Early on it may only pinch when you bend the toe up — pushing off a step, kneeling, or wearing a flexible shoe. Over time the joint stiffens, the bony bump on top enlarges, and shoes start rubbing it. If you're still sorting out whether your big-toe pain is arthritis or something else, our guide on telling hallux rigidus apart from a bunion walks through the differences, and our overview of how to recognize foot and ankle arthritis covers the broader warning signs. This article picks up where diagnosis leaves off: what you can actually do about it.

One thing worth settling early — a suddenly hot, red, swollen big toe that flares over hours is far more likely to be gout or an infection than osteoarthritis, and it needs prompt evaluation rather than a wait-and-see approach. Hallux rigidus builds slowly. A toe that turns angry overnight is telling you something different.

First-line relief without surgery

Most people start here, and many stay here. The whole idea behind conservative care is simple: stop forcing the sore joint to bend. When you take the bending out of the equation, the pain often follows.

  • Stiffer-soled and rocker-bottom shoes. A shoe with a rigid sole stops the big-toe joint from flexing as you roll through a step, and a curved "rocker" bottom lets your foot roll forward without the toe doing the work. For a lot of patients this single change is the biggest lever of all.
  • A Morton's extension orthotic. This is a custom orthotic built with a firm extension under the big toe that limits how far the joint bends. It's one of the most targeted non-surgical tools for hallux rigidus specifically, because it addresses the exact motion that hurts.
  • Activity adjustments. Swapping deep squats, hill sprints, or long barefoot stretches for lower-impact movement — cycling, swimming, an elliptical — keeps you active while giving the joint a break.
  • Anti-inflammatory measures. Ice after activity and, when your physician approves them, over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medicines can quiet a flare. These manage symptoms; they don't rebuild cartilage.

Conservative care doesn't reverse arthritis — nothing does — but it can keep a mild or moderate joint comfortable for a long stretch. Give any one change a few weeks before you judge it.

Do injections help?

Injections are the next rung when shoe and orthotic changes aren't enough on their own. A corticosteroid injection into the joint can calm inflammation and buy a window of real relief, which is useful during a bad flare or while you decide about surgery. What an injection won't do is cure the arthritis or regrow the worn cartilage, and because repeated steroid injections into a small joint carry their own trade-offs, they're used thoughtfully rather than on a set schedule.

Other injectable options exist and come in and out of favor, but the evidence behind them for the big-toe joint is mixed, and a responsible conversation about any of them starts with an exam and an honest discussion of what it can and can't deliver for your particular joint. Think of injections as a tool to manage symptoms and inform the next decision — not a destination.

Cheilectomy: cleaning out the joint while keeping it

This is the option most people don't realize exists, and it's the heart of "beyond fusion." A cheilectomy is a joint-sparing operation in which the surgeon removes the bony ridge on top of the joint and trims away rough, overgrown bone that's blocking motion and jamming the toe. The joint stays — it isn't fused and it isn't replaced. The aim is to free up movement, ease the pain of pushing off, and let the shoe stop rubbing that bump.

Cheilectomy tends to suit earlier and mid-stage arthritis, where enough healthy cartilage remains to make preserving the joint worthwhile. Because it keeps your own joint, it leaves the door open to other procedures later if the arthritis keeps advancing. Recovery varies from person to person and is something your surgeon will map out based on your joint and your goals, but the trade is straightforward: a smaller operation that preserves motion, with the understanding that it doesn't stop arthritis from progressing over the years.

Other joint-preserving procedures

Between a simple cheilectomy and a fusion sits a middle tier of operations that try to keep the joint moving in different ways. The right one depends on how much cartilage is left, the shape of your bones, your activity level, and your surgeon's judgment:

  • Osteotomy. A precise cut that reshapes or repositions bone around the joint to shift load off the worn area and improve how the toe bends. It's sometimes paired with a cheilectomy.
  • Interpositional arthroplasty. The surgeon places soft tissue into the joint as a spacer after removing damaged surfaces, cushioning the bones so they no longer grind directly on each other while preserving some movement.
  • Joint implants. For some patients, an implant — including newer synthetic cartilage options — can resurface the joint and maintain motion. Candidacy is specific, and whether an implant makes sense for you is a decision made at an in-person evaluation, not from an article.

None of these is right for everyone, and each carries its own considerations. What they share is a goal: keep the big toe bending rather than locking it still.

Where fusion fits — and why it isn't a failure

Fusion (arthrodesis) permanently joins the two bones of the joint so they no longer move against each other. Because the painful motion is gone, the pain goes with it, and fusion has a long track record as a durable, dependable answer for advanced hallux rigidus — the stage where the cartilage is largely gone and joint-sparing procedures no longer have much to work with.

The trade is motion: the big toe won't bend at that joint afterward. Most people adapt well, walk comfortably, and return to the activities that matter to them, often more than they expected after years of pain. Fusion lands near the top of the ladder not because it's a last resort in the grim sense, but because it gives up a movement you can't get back — so it makes the most sense once the joint has little useful motion left to preserve. If you've read that fusion is the only fix for an arthritic big toe, that's the misunderstanding this whole article exists to clear up. It's one option among several, chosen for the right joint at the right stage.

How the right option gets chosen

Treatment for hallux rigidus is matched to the stage of the arthritis and to you. An exam shows how far the toe still bends and where it hurts; an X-ray shows how much cartilage and spur formation are present. From there, the pattern usually runs from least to most invasive: footwear and orthotic changes first, injections to manage flares, a joint-sparing operation like cheilectomy when conservative care runs out, other joint-preserving procedures for the right anatomy, and fusion reserved for advanced disease.

Your own priorities steer the plan as much as the X-ray does. A runner who needs toe motion, someone who's on their feet all day for work, and a person whose main goal is pain-free walking may each land on a different rung. There's no single right answer and no fixed timeline — recovery and results vary by patient and are confirmed by your surgeon. When to see a foot and ankle specialist

It's worth getting the big toe evaluated when pain starts changing what you do — avoiding certain shoes, cutting activities short, or feeling the joint stiffen and the bump grow. Catching hallux rigidus earlier keeps more treatment options open, including the joint-sparing ones that depend on having cartilage left to save.

Some signs call for prompt attention rather than a scheduled visit: a big toe that becomes suddenly hot, red, and swollen over hours; an inability to bear weight; a wound or sore over the joint that won't heal; or spreading redness or fever. Those point to gout, infection, or injury, not slow-building arthritis. Common Questions

Big-toe arthritis FAQs

What can I try for big-toe arthritis before considering fusion?

Treatment usually climbs a ladder from least to most invasive: stiffer-soled or rocker-bottom shoes and a Morton's extension orthotic first, then anti-inflammatory measures or an injection to settle flares, then a joint-sparing operation such as a cheilectomy if those run out. Fusion sits near the top of that ladder and is matched to advanced arthritis, not used as a first step.

What is a cheilectomy?

A cheilectomy is a joint-sparing surgery that removes the bony ridge on top of the big-toe joint and trims overgrown bone that's blocking motion. The joint is kept rather than fused or replaced. It's generally suited to earlier and mid-stage arthritis, where enough cartilage remains to make preserving the joint worthwhile.

Will a cortisone shot cure hallux rigidus?

No. A corticosteroid injection can calm inflammation and provide a window of relief, which is helpful during a flare or while weighing surgery, but it doesn't cure the arthritis or regrow worn cartilage. Because repeated injections into a small joint carry trade-offs, they're used thoughtfully rather than on a set schedule.

Do orthotics really help big-toe arthritis?

They can, especially a custom orthotic built with a Morton's extension — a firm section under the big toe that limits how far the painful joint bends. Paired with a stiff-soled or rocker-bottom shoe, this takes the bending out of each step, which is often where the pain comes from.

Is joint replacement an option for the big toe?

For some patients, an implant — including newer synthetic cartilage options — can resurface the joint and maintain motion. Candidacy depends on your anatomy, the stage of arthritis, and your activity goals, so whether it makes sense for you is a decision made at an in-person evaluation rather than from general information.

How do I know when it's time for surgery?

Surgery usually enters the conversation when footwear changes, orthotics, and injections no longer control the pain or when the joint is stiffening to the point that it limits daily activity. An exam and X-ray show how much cartilage is left, which guides whether a joint-sparing procedure or fusion fits. You can request an appointment to talk it through.
