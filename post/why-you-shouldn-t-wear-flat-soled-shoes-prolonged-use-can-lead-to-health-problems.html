Why You Shouldn't Wear Flat-Soled Shoes

Thin, unsupportive soles feel harmless at first — but worn day after day, they can quietly set off a chain of foot, joint, and skin problems.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Flat-soled shoes — thin-soled flats, worn-out sneakers, and other footwear with little to no arch support — can lead to real foot and body problems when worn regularly. Without cushioning or structure to support the arch, your feet absorb far more impact than they are built to handle on their own. Over time, that added strain can contribute to plantar fasciitis, misalignment up the kinetic chain, blistering, and ingrown toenails.

A single day in flats or worn sneakers usually isn't a problem. It's the repeated, daily wear — especially on hard surfaces or during long periods of standing and walking — that tends to catch up with patients. Below, our podiatrists explain what makes flat-soled shoes risky and how to choose footwear that protects your feet instead of straining them.

What makes a shoe "flat-soled" and risky?

The shoes we're talking about typically share two features: a thin, unsupportive sole with no built-in arch support, and a narrow toe box that crowds the toes together. Ballet flats, worn-through sneakers, thin sandals, and some minimalist shoes all fall into this category. Neither feature is dangerous on its own in small doses, but combined and worn daily, they remove the structure your foot relies on to move and absorb shock properly.

Can flat-soled shoes cause plantar fasciitis?

Yes — unsupportive footwear is one of the more common contributing factors we see. The plantar fascia is the band of tissue that runs along the bottom of your foot and helps absorb shock with every step. Without a supportive sole to share that load, the fascia takes on more strain than it's designed for, which can lead to the small tears and inflammation behind plantar fasciitis — a sharp, stabbing heel pain that's often worst with your first steps in the morning.

How can flat shoes lead to skeletal misalignment?

Your feet are the foundation for the rest of your skeleton. When a shoe offers no arch support or stability, the foot has to compensate to keep you balanced, and that compensation doesn't stay in the foot — it travels upward. Over time, an unstable base can contribute to shifts in how the knees, pelvis, hips, and lower back align and move, which can show up as pain in joints far from your feet.

Why do flat-soled shoes cause blisters?

Thin soles provide minimal shock absorption, which increases friction between your foot and the shoe with every step. That friction is a common cause of blisters on the heel and along the sides of the foot, especially during long days on your feet or in shoes that don't fit well to begin with.

Can tight, flat shoes cause ingrown toenails?

They can contribute to them. A narrow, cramped toe box — common in many flat-soled styles — presses the toes together and can force a toenail to grow into the surrounding skin instead of straight out. Ingrown toenails can become painful and, if the skin breaks down, prone to infection.

What should you look for in a healthier shoe instead?

You don't have to give up flats entirely — the goal is choosing footwear that supports your foot's natural structure. When shopping, look for:

  • Built-in arch support, or enough room to add a supportive insert or custom orthotic
  • A thicker, cushioned sole that absorbs shock rather than transmitting it straight to your joints
  • A wider, rounder toe box that lets your toes sit naturally
  • A secure, comfortable fit that doesn't require you to grip or curl your toes to keep the shoe on

Athletic sneakers with good arch support and low, stable wedge heels are generally better everyday choices than thin flats or worn-out shoes. If you're on your feet for long stretches at work, rotating in a more supportive shoe on those days can make a meaningful difference.

When should you see a podiatrist about shoe-related foot pain?

See a podiatrist if foot pain lasts more than a week or two despite changing your footwear, if you notice a toenail becoming red, swollen, or painful along the edge, or if you're developing recurring blisters or calluses in the same spot. Catching these issues early — and pairing better footwear with the right treatment — usually resolves them well before they become chronic. Common Questions

Flat-soled shoes FAQs

Are flat shoes bad for your feet?

Flat, thin-soled shoes aren't automatically harmful, but wearing them regularly without arch support can strain the plantar fascia, increase friction that leads to blisters, and crowd toes in a narrow toe box. Occasional wear is usually fine — the risk comes from daily, prolonged use.

Can shoes with no arch support cause heel pain?

Yes. Without support, the plantar fascia absorbs more shock than it's designed for with each step, which is a common contributing factor in plantar fasciitis — a leading cause of heel pain.

Can shoes really affect my knees, hips, or back?

They can. Your feet are the foundation for the rest of your skeleton, and an unstable base can lead to compensatory changes in how the knees, pelvis, hips, and spine align and move over time.

Why do flat shoes give me blisters?

Thin soles absorb very little shock, which increases friction between your foot and the shoe with every step. That extra friction is a common cause of blisters on the heel and sides of the foot.

Can narrow shoes cause ingrown toenails?

A narrow, cramped toe box can press the toes together and force a nail to grow into the surrounding skin instead of straight out, which is a common contributor to ingrown toenails.

What kind of shoes should I wear instead?

Look for shoes with built-in arch support or room for a custom orthotic, a thicker cushioned sole, and a wider toe box. Supportive athletic sneakers and low, stable wedge heels are generally better everyday choices than thin flats or worn-out shoes.
