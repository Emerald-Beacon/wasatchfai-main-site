Managing Foot Pain After Surgery: Your Recovery Guide

What's normal, what's not, and how to move through the weeks after foot or ankle surgery with less pain and a smoother recovery.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Some pain and swelling after foot or ankle surgery is normal, and it typically follows a predictable pattern — a dull ache that gradually improves day by day. Sharp, worsening pain, or pain paired with fever or spreading redness, is not part of that normal pattern and should prompt a call to your surgeon. Understanding what to expect at each stage of recovery makes it much easier to manage discomfort, protect your surgical site, and get back on your feet on schedule.

Every surgery and every patient heals a little differently, but the fundamentals of a smooth recovery are consistent. Below, our physicians walk through what to expect in the first 72 hours, how to manage pain effectively, when physical therapy begins, and the warning signs that mean you should call our office.

What's normal after foot or ankle surgery?

Swelling is expected after any foot or ankle procedure — the tissue has been disturbed, and fluid buildup is part of normal healing. Along with swelling, most patients feel a dull, aching pain that is manageable with medication and gradually improves over the following days and weeks. What's not normal is sharp, stabbing pain that gets worse instead of better, or pain accompanied by fever, spreading redness, or a foul odor from the incision — these warrant a call to your surgical team.

What should you do in the first 72 hours?

The first three days after surgery set the tone for the rest of your recovery. Three habits matter most during this window:

  • Elevation: Keep the foot elevated above heart level as much as possible. This is one of the most effective ways to control swelling and pain.
  • Ice therapy: Apply ice in cycles of about 20 minutes on, then off, to reduce swelling and numb discomfort — following your surgeon's specific instructions for your procedure.
  • Careful mobility: Use crutches, a knee scooter, or another assistive device exactly as instructed, and avoid putting weight on the surgical foot until you're cleared to do so.

How should you manage pain during recovery?

Take prescribed pain medication as directed by your surgeon, especially in the first several days when discomfort is typically highest. Many patients also find it helpful to combine medication with supportive, non-pharmaceutical strategies, such as consistent elevation and icing, gentle relaxation techniques, and an anti-inflammatory-friendly diet that includes foods like omega-3-rich fish and turmeric. These measures don't replace prescribed medication, but they can help you rely on less of it as healing progresses.

When does physical therapy start, and what does it involve?

Once your surgeon clears you to begin, physical therapy plays a central role in regaining strength and normal movement. Early sessions typically focus on teaching safe, proper movement patterns and gradually restoring range of motion. As healing progresses, therapy shifts toward strength building, and may include supportive treatments like ultrasound or electrical stimulation to support tissue healing. Following your therapy plan closely is one of the biggest factors in how well — and how quickly — you regain full function.

What's a realistic recovery timeline?

Recovery timelines vary by procedure, but general patterns hold true for many foot and ankle surgeries: most patients see meaningful improvement within about 6–8 weeks, while complete healing of the surgical site and surrounding tissue often takes 3–6 months. More complex procedures, such as major reconstructions or fusions, can require a longer timeline. Your surgeon will give you a timeline specific to your procedure and progress.

What footwear should you wear as you heal?

As you transition out of a surgical boot or cast, ease back into footwear gradually. Cushioned insoles, a wide toe box, and adjustable closures (rather than tight laces) help accommodate lingering swelling and protect the healing area. Ask your surgeon when it's appropriate to return to your regular shoes, and consider custom orthotics if you need additional support during the transition.

When should you call your surgeon?

Contact our office promptly if you notice a fever above 100.4°F, spreading redness or warmth around the incision, increasing rather than decreasing pain, or drainage that looks infected. Questions about driving or returning to work are common too — both depend on your specific procedure and job demands, so ask your surgical team for guidance tailored to your situation. If you have questions during your recovery, you can always request an appointment or reach out to either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Post-surgery recovery FAQs

How much pain is normal after foot or ankle surgery?

A dull, manageable ache that gradually improves day by day is normal. Sharp, worsening pain — or pain paired with fever or spreading redness — is not typical and should prompt a call to your surgeon.

How long does it take to fully recover from foot surgery?

Most patients see meaningful improvement within 6–8 weeks, while complete healing often takes 3–6 months. More complex procedures can take longer. Your surgeon will give you a timeline specific to your procedure.

What are the warning signs of an infection after surgery?

Watch for a fever above 100.4°F, spreading redness or warmth around the incision, increasing pain instead of gradual improvement, or foul-smelling drainage. Contact your surgical team promptly if you notice any of these.

When can I start physical therapy after foot or ankle surgery?

Physical therapy typically begins once your surgeon clears you, often starting with safe movement patterns and range of motion before progressing to strength building. Your surgeon will time this to your specific procedure and healing progress.

When can I drive or return to work after surgery?

This depends on your specific procedure, which foot was operated on, and your job demands. Desk jobs generally allow an earlier return than jobs requiring standing or walking. Ask your surgical team for guidance tailored to your situation.

What shoes should I wear while recovering from foot surgery?

As you transition out of a surgical boot, choose shoes with cushioned insoles, a wide toe box, and adjustable closures to accommodate swelling. Ask your surgeon when it's appropriate to return to your regular footwear.
