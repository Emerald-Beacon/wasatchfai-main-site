Why Does Heel Pain Get Worse After Rest?

If your heel hurts most with the first steps out of bed — or after sitting at your desk all afternoon — the tissue in your foot is telling you something specific. Here's the mechanics behind it.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Heel pain gets worse after rest because the tissues that support your foot — most often the plantar fascia or Achilles tendon — tighten, cool, and shorten while you are still. When you suddenly put weight on them again, that stiff tissue is forced to stretch quickly, producing a sharp jolt of "first-step pain" that typically eases as you keep moving. This pattern is one of the most reliable clues podiatrists use to diagnose the underlying cause.

Patients often describe it the same way: the first steps out of bed are the worst, or standing up after a long meeting sends a stab of pain through the heel that fades within a few minutes of walking. That "worse after rest, better with movement" pattern is not random — it reflects exactly what is happening inside the tissues of your foot.

What actually happens in your foot during rest?

While you sleep or sit for an extended period — generally 30 to 60 minutes or longer — the plantar fascia and surrounding soft tissue lose the gentle tension that normal daily movement provides. Blood flow to the area slows, the tissue cools slightly, and micro-tears from the day's activity begin to heal in a shortened position rather than their normal length. The result is a tighter, stiffer band of tissue by the time you stand back up.

The moment you bear weight again, your body relies on what is called the windlass mechanism: as your toes bend upward with each step, the plantar fascia tightens like a cable to support the arch. On a fascia that has already tightened overnight, that extra pull is what produces the sudden, stabbing pain.

Which conditions cause pain that flares after rest?

This "start-up pain" pattern shows up across several common foot and ankle conditions, though the exact cause changes where the pain is felt and how long it lingers.

  • Plantar fasciitis — by far the most common cause, producing sharp pain in the bottom of the heel with the first steps of the day
  • Achilles tendinopathy — stiffness and pain at the back of the heel or lower calf that eases with gentle movement
  • Stress fractures — pain that may also worsen with activity rather than fully resolving, and can be tender to direct pressure
  • Nerve entrapment (such as tarsal tunnel syndrome) — burning or tingling that can also feel worse after periods of stillness

Because plantar fasciitis and Achilles tendon problems share so many risk factors, it is common to have some irritation in both areas at once. Our heel pain and Achilles tendonitis pages go into more detail on each.

Why does the pain improve once you start walking?

As you move, circulation to the area increases, the tissue warms slightly, and it stretches back out to a more normal length. Most people notice meaningful relief within five to ten minutes of walking. That temporary improvement can be misleading, though — it does not mean the underlying irritation is gone, only that the tissue has stretched enough to reduce tension for the moment. The stiffness returns the next time you rest.

How can you reduce first-step heel pain?

Because the pain is driven by tissue stiffening during rest, the most effective strategies focus on preventing that stiffness from building up in the first place, or easing into movement more gradually.

  • Stretch before standing. Gentle calf and plantar fascia stretches, or simple ankle pumps, done right before you get out of bed can reduce the intensity of that first step
  • Consider a night splint. Worn overnight, these keep the fascia in a lengthened position so it does not tighten as much; studies show benefit over a consistent 4–8 week period
  • Choose supportive footwear. Shoes with good arch support and cushioning reduce strain on the fascia throughout the day
  • Take breaks from sitting. If your job keeps you seated for long stretches, standing and moving periodically prevents the tissue from fully tightening
  • Warm up before exercise. A short warm-up allows tissue to loosen gradually rather than being stretched abruptly

For persistent cases, our physicians may also recommend custom orthotics to better support the arch throughout the day.

When should you see a podiatrist?

Occasional mild stiffness after rest is common and often responds well to the steps above. It is time to have your heel evaluated if the pain has lasted more than six to eight weeks despite home care, if it is severe enough to change how you walk, or if you cannot bear weight on the foot at all. Swelling, redness, warmth, or fever alongside heel pain should be evaluated promptly, as these can point to infection or another condition that needs prompt attention rather than home stretching. Common Questions

Heel pain after rest FAQs

Why does my heel hurt worse in the morning?

Overnight, the plantar fascia and surrounding tissue tighten and shorten while you sleep. The first steps out of bed suddenly stretch that tightened tissue, producing a sharp jolt of pain that is the hallmark symptom of plantar fasciitis.

Why does heel pain go away after I start walking?

Movement increases blood flow and gradually stretches the stiffened tissue back to a more normal length, usually within five to ten minutes. This does not mean the underlying problem is resolved — the stiffness typically returns after your next rest period.

Is heel pain that flares after sitting always plantar fasciitis?

Not always. While plantar fasciitis is the most common cause, Achilles tendinopathy, stress fractures, and nerve entrapment can all produce pain that is worse after rest. A podiatrist can pinpoint the exact cause with an exam.

Do night splints actually help with heel pain?

Yes, for many people. Night splints keep the plantar fascia gently stretched while you sleep so it does not tighten as much overnight. Consistent use over 4 to 8 weeks has been shown to reduce first-step pain for many patients.

How long should I try home care before seeing a doctor?

If stretching, supportive shoes, and activity changes have not improved your heel pain after six to eight weeks, it is a good time to be evaluated. See a podiatrist sooner if you cannot bear weight or notice swelling, redness, or fever.

Can heel pain after rest be a stress fracture instead of plantar fasciitis?

It's possible. Stress fractures can also feel worse after periods of stillness, but unlike plantar fasciitis, the pain often continues or worsens with activity rather than easing. A podiatrist can use an exam and imaging to tell the two apart.
