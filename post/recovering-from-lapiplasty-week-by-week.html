Recovering From Lapiplasty, Week by Week

Lapiplasty recovery unfolds in stages — a quiet first couple of weeks, then steady, protected progress back to walking, shoes, and the things you love. Here's what each phase usually looks like.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated October 20, 2026

Lapiplasty recovery moves through a predictable sequence: a protected early phase focused on elevation and rest, a stretch of walking in a surgical boot, a gradual return to supportive shoes, and several more months while the corrected joint fully heals. Most people are up and moving in a boot sooner than they expect, but the deeper bone healing takes longer and sets the pace for everything else. The exact timing is set by your surgeon, who adjusts it to your bone quality, your health, and how the correction looks on follow-up X-rays.

Knowing the shape of that recovery in advance makes it far less stressful. When you understand why the first two weeks are slow on purpose, or why your surgeon wants you in a boot longer than a friend who had a different operation, the restrictions start to make sense instead of feeling arbitrary. This guide walks through recovery stage by stage so you can plan your weeks off work, your help at home, and your return to activity around what your foot is actually doing.

What makes Lapiplasty recovery different

A bunion isn't just a bump — it's a bone that has drifted out of alignment in three dimensions. Lapiplasty addresses the problem at its root by rotating the entire first metatarsal back into position and fusing the unstable joint at the base of it, holding everything with titanium plates. That fusion is the key to understanding recovery. You aren't only healing a skin incision; you're waiting for two bones to knit together into one solid unit, and that biology can't be rushed.

Because the hardware locks the correction firmly in place, many Lapiplasty patients are allowed to put protected weight through the heel and outer foot in a boot earlier than older screw-only techniques traditionally permitted — some of which kept patients entirely off the foot for weeks. That earlier weight-bearing is one of the procedure's selling points, and we explain how it stacks up against an osteotomy in our breakdown of how Lapiplasty differs from traditional bunion surgery. Early weight-bearing is not the same as early freedom, though. The boot, the limits on how much you walk, and the slow reintroduction of normal shoes all exist to protect that healing fusion. You can read more about the procedure itself on our Lapiplasty page.

Weeks 0–2: protect and elevate

The first two weeks are the quietest and, for many people, the most important. Your foot will be bandaged and splinted or placed in a boot, and the single most useful thing you can do is keep it elevated above the level of your heart as much as possible. Swelling is the enemy of comfort and healing in this stage, and gravity is working against you every minute your foot hangs down. Think of this period as deliberate stillness, not wasted time.

Expect to rely on a knee scooter, crutches, or a walker, depending on what your surgeon prescribes. Pain is usually most noticeable in the first several days and then eases; your care team will give you a plan to manage it. Keep the surgical dressing clean and dry, and don't peek under it or get it wet unless you've been told it's okay. You'll typically come back around two weeks out so sutures can be checked or removed and your progress assessed. Arrange for help at home before surgery — meals, driving, and anything that lives on a high shelf are much harder on one foot.

Weeks 2–6: finding your feet in the boot

This is the stretch where recovery starts to feel like it's moving. Once your surgeon confirms the early healing looks right, you'll usually progress to putting more weight through the foot inside the boot, often with the help of your scooter or crutches at first and then without. The boot is doing real work here: it keeps the forefoot from flexing and bearing load before the fusion is ready, so wearing it faithfully — including the hours you'd rather not — directly protects your result.

A few things tend to define these weeks:

  • Swelling that comes and goes. It's normal for the foot to puff up by evening, especially on days you're more active. Keep elevating when you can.
  • Gentle motion. Your surgeon may have you begin moving the toes or ankle to keep things from getting stiff. Follow their specific instructions rather than inventing your own routine.
  • Return to desk work. Many people with seated jobs head back during this window, as long as they can elevate the foot at their desk. Jobs that keep you on your feet take longer.

Follow-up X-rays guide every decision in this phase. If the images show the fusion maturing as expected, you advance; if not, your surgeon may hold you back a bit, and that caution is a good thing.

Weeks 6–12: back into shoes and rebuilding strength

Somewhere in this range — only when your X-rays support it — most patients transition out of the boot and into a supportive, roomy shoe. That first step back into a real shoe is a milestone, but the foot is not finished healing, and it will tell you so. Expect lingering swelling, some stiffness in the big-toe joint, and a foot that tires more easily than its partner. All of that is ordinary.

Physical therapy often becomes a bigger part of the picture now. The goal is to restore motion in the big toe, rebuild the strength you lost during the weeks of limited walking, and retrain a normal, confident gait so you're not favoring the foot out of habit. A supportive shoe — and in some cases a custom orthotic — helps distribute load evenly while things settle. Resist the urge to jump straight back into long walks, hikes, or the gym; building up gradually is how you avoid a painful setback. For the broader principles of healing after any foot operation, our guide to managing foot pain after surgery covers pain control, therapy, and realistic pacing in more depth.

Months 3–6: the fusion keeps maturing

By three months, most people are walking in normal shoes and returning to the ordinary rhythm of their days. But bone remodeling continues well beyond the point where you feel recovered, and higher-impact activity — running, court sports, long mountain hikes, anything that pounds the forefoot — is usually reintroduced later and only with your surgeon's clearance. Northern Utah's trails and ski season aren't going anywhere; giving the fusion time now protects the correction for years.

Residual swelling can come and go for months, often more noticeable after a busy day or at altitude. Scar tissue softens gradually. The big-toe joint keeps regaining motion with use and therapy. This long tail of healing is completely normal and doesn't mean anything is wrong — it's simply how bone and soft tissue recover. Your follow-up visits exist to confirm the fusion is solid before you push into the things you've been waiting to do. The recovery resources on our site can help you set expectations for this longer phase.

What can slow your recovery down

A few factors have an outsized effect on how a fusion heals. Smoking and nicotine in any form reduce blood flow to the bone and are strongly linked to slower, less reliable healing — stopping before and after surgery genuinely matters. Poorly controlled blood sugar in people with diabetes can delay healing and raise infection risk, which is why your team may want your numbers in a good range first. And the most common self-inflicted setback is simply doing too much too soon: leaving the boot off early, skipping elevation, or sneaking in a long walk before the bone is ready. The restrictions feel conservative because the cost of breaking them — a delayed fusion or a loss of correction — is high.

When to call your surgeon

Most of recovery is uneventful, but a handful of symptoms mean you should reach out right away rather than wait for your next appointment. Call the clinic or seek urgent care promptly if you notice:

  • Spreading redness, warmth, or drainage from the incision, or a fever
  • Calf pain, swelling, or tenderness, which can signal a blood clot
  • Pain that suddenly worsens or is no longer controlled by your plan
  • Numbness, a cold or pale foot, or new tingling that doesn't ease with elevation
  • A pop or sudden change in the shape of the foot after a stumble or fall

None of these should be met with a wait-and-see approach. Catching a problem early is almost always simpler to address than catching it late. Common Questions

Lapiplasty recovery FAQs

How soon can I put weight on my foot after Lapiplasty?

Many patients are allowed to begin protected weight-bearing in a surgical boot earlier than with older screw-only techniques, often within the first couple of weeks once the surgeon confirms early healing looks right. The exact timing is individual — your surgeon sets it based on your bone quality and how your follow-up X-rays look, so follow their instructions rather than a general timeline.

How long will I wear the walking boot?

The boot typically stays on for several weeks and comes off only when X-rays show the fusion is healing as expected. It keeps the forefoot from bearing load before the bone is ready, so wearing it as directed — including the hours you'd rather skip — directly protects your correction. Your surgeon decides when you transition into a supportive shoe.

When can I drive again after Lapiplasty?

Driving depends on which foot had surgery, whether you're still in a boot, and whether you're taking medication that affects alertness. Most people wait until they're out of the boot, off those medications, and can move the foot safely in an emergency. Ask your surgeon to clear you before you get behind the wheel, especially if it was your right foot.

Will I need physical therapy?

Often, yes. Once you're out of the boot, therapy helps restore motion in the big-toe joint, rebuild the strength lost during weeks of limited walking, and retrain a normal gait so you stop favoring the foot. Whether you need formal therapy or a home program depends on your progress and your surgeon's judgment.

Is Lapiplasty recovery painful?

Discomfort is usually most noticeable in the first several days and then steadily eases. Your care team will give you a plan to manage it, and keeping the foot elevated in the early weeks makes a real difference. Pain that suddenly worsens, or that your plan no longer controls, is a reason to call the clinic rather than push through.

When can I get back to running or hiking?

Higher-impact activity is generally reintroduced in the later months of recovery and only with your surgeon's clearance, because the fused bone keeps remodeling well after you feel recovered. Returning too early risks a setback. Build back gradually from walking, and let your follow-up X-rays — not the calendar or how you feel — guide the timing.
