What Are Impact Ankle Fractures? Examples & Treatment

High-energy ankle breaks from falls or collisions are a different animal than a simple sprain — here's how to recognize them and what treatment typically involves.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

An impact ankle fracture is a break caused by significant force — a fall from height, a vehicle collision, or a hard landing during sports — rather than a routine twist or roll. Because the force involved is so much greater, these fractures are often more severe and unstable than a typical ankle sprain or stress fracture, and surgery is frequently required to restore normal alignment.

If you've been in a fall or collision and your ankle is swollen, deformed, or too painful to bear weight on, it's worth understanding what kind of fracture you might be dealing with — and why these injuries are treated differently than a garden-variety sprain. Below, our podiatrists break down the most common types of impact ankle fractures and how each is typically managed.

What makes an impact fracture different from a sprain?

A sprain involves stretched or torn ligaments, while a fracture is a break in the bone itself. Impact fractures result from high-energy trauma — think a fall from a ladder, a car accident, or a heavy landing after a jump — so they often involve more than one bone, more soft-tissue damage, and more instability in the joint. That combination is why impact fractures more often require surgical stabilization than lower-energy injuries.

What are the common types of impact ankle fractures?

Medial malleolus fracture

The medial malleolus is the bony bump on the inside of your ankle. A fracture here typically causes severe pain, swelling, and bruising, and often makes it impossible to bear weight. Because this area helps stabilize the ankle joint, treatment usually involves surgery with internal fixation — screws, plates, or rods — to hold the bone in proper position while it heals.

Avulsion fracture

An avulsion fracture happens when a small fragment of bone is pulled away at the point where a ligament or tendon attaches. It can result from a single high-impact event or from repetitive stress over time. Treatment depends on how displaced the fragment is: stable avulsion fractures are often managed with a walking boot for four to six weeks, while more displaced fragments may need surgical repair.

Pilon fracture

A pilon fracture involves the lower end of the shinbone (tibia) where it meets the ankle joint, and it typically results from extreme-force impacts such as a fall from height or a car accident. Symptoms include severe swelling, bruising, and visible deformity. Stable, well-aligned pilon fractures may be treated with immobilization, but unstable fractures usually require surgery — sometimes staged over more than one procedure — followed by a structured course of physical therapy.

Trimalleolar fracture

This is one of the more serious ankle fracture patterns, involving breaks at three points around the ankle along with frequent ligament damage. Trimalleolar fractures are considered inherently unstable and almost always require surgical repair to restore the joint's alignment and function.

How are impact ankle fractures diagnosed?

Diagnosis starts with a physical exam and X-rays to identify which bones are involved and how displaced they are. For more complex fracture patterns — particularly pilon and trimalleolar fractures — a CT scan may be used to map out the injury in greater detail before planning surgery.

How are impact ankle fractures treated?

Because these fractures result from significant trauma, surgery is common. The specific approach depends on which bones are broken, how displaced they are, and the condition of the surrounding soft tissue:

  • Internal fixation using screws, plates, or rods to hold fractured bone in place while it heals
  • Immobilization in a cast or boot for fractures that are stable and well-aligned
  • Staged surgery for severe fractures with significant soft-tissue swelling, allowing the tissue to settle before definitive repair
  • Physical therapy after healing to restore strength, range of motion, and stability

Recovery timelines vary widely depending on the fracture pattern and whether surgery was needed, but most patients need a period of non-weight-bearing or limited weight-bearing followed by a gradual return to activity.

When should you seek care?

Go to urgent care or an emergency room immediately for a visibly deformed ankle, an inability to bear any weight, or an open wound near the injury. For swelling, bruising, and pain that doesn't resolve after a fall or collision — even without obvious deformity — it's still worth having it evaluated promptly, since some fractures aren't obvious without imaging. Our team offers same-day appointments for urgent injuries; you can also request an appointment to have a suspected fracture assessed.

Common Questions

Impact ankle fracture FAQs

How do I know if my ankle is fractured versus sprained?

You often can't tell for certain without an X-ray, since both can cause pain, swelling, and bruising. Signs that point more toward a fracture include inability to bear any weight, visible deformity, and pain directly over the bone rather than around the joint. When in doubt, have it evaluated.

Do all impact ankle fractures need surgery?

Not all — stable, well-aligned fractures such as some avulsion fractures can sometimes be treated with a walking boot. However, because impact fractures involve significant force, many do require surgery to restore proper alignment and stability, especially trimalleolar and unstable pilon fractures.

What is the difference between a pilon fracture and a trimalleolar fracture?

A pilon fracture involves the lower end of the shinbone where it meets the ankle joint, usually from a high-force impact like a fall. A trimalleolar fracture involves breaks at three points around the ankle joint itself, often with ligament damage. Both are typically unstable and often require surgery.

How long does recovery from an impact ankle fracture take?

Recovery varies significantly based on the fracture type and whether surgery was needed. Many patients spend several weeks non-weight-bearing or in a boot, followed by a period of physical therapy to rebuild strength and mobility. Your physician can give you a timeline specific to your injury.

What should I do immediately after a high-impact ankle injury?

Avoid putting weight on the ankle, elevate it, and apply ice to limit swelling. If there is visible deformity, an open wound, or you cannot bear any weight, seek emergency care right away. Otherwise, have the ankle evaluated promptly to rule out a fracture.

Can I walk on a fractured ankle?

You generally should not try to walk on an ankle you suspect is fractured, as this can worsen the injury or displace the bone further. Use crutches or avoid weight-bearing until you've been evaluated and given clear guidance from a physician.
