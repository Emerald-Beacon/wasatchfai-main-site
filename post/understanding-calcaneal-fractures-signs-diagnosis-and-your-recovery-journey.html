Understanding Calcaneal Fractures: Signs, Diagnosis & Your Recovery Journey

The calcaneus is the largest bone in your foot and absorbs shock with every step. Here's what a fracture looks like, how it's diagnosed, and what recovery involves.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

A calcaneal fracture is a break in the calcaneus, or heel bone — the largest bone in your foot and a key player in absorbing shock with every step you take. Because these fractures usually come from a high-impact event, they cause sudden, severe heel pain and often need prompt evaluation. Treatment ranges from rest and immobilization to surgery, depending on how the bone has broken.

Whether it followed a fall, a car accident, or a hard landing during sports, a suspected heel fracture is not something to wait out. Our podiatrists in Farmington and South Ogden diagnose and treat calcaneal fractures using imaging, personalized treatment plans, and — when needed — modern surgical techniques. Here's what to know.

What is a calcaneal fracture?

The calcaneus sits at the back of the foot, forming the structure of the heel and supporting your body weight with every step. Because it bears so much load, a break here can significantly affect your ability to stand and walk. Calcaneal fractures range from simple cracks to complex, multi-part breaks that extend into the nearby joints.

What causes a calcaneal fracture?

Most calcaneal fractures result from high-impact events, including:

  • Falls from a height, landing directly on the heel
  • Car accidents, where the heel absorbs sudden force
  • High-impact sports involving jumping, especially with inadequate warm-up
  • Direct trauma to the heel from a heavy object or forceful impact

Athletes in jumping sports face increased risk, particularly when landing mechanics or warm-up routines aren't adequate.

What are the warning signs of a calcaneal fracture?

The hallmark symptom is sudden, severe pain in the heel following an injury. Other common signs include:

  • Sudden, severe pain in the heel, especially with weight-bearing
  • Swelling around the heel and ankle
  • Bruising that spreads across the sole or side of the foot
  • Difficulty or inability to bear weight on the affected foot
  • Visible deformity in more severe fractures

If you have heel pain following a fall, accident, or hard landing and cannot put weight on the foot, treat it as a possible fracture and seek prompt evaluation.

How is a calcaneal fracture diagnosed?

X-rays are typically the first step and can confirm most fractures. For more complex breaks — particularly those involving the joint surfaces — a CT scan may be needed to map out the fracture pattern in detail and guide treatment planning.

How is a calcaneal fracture treated?

Not every calcaneal fracture requires surgery. Treatment depends on the pattern and severity of the break.

Nonsurgical treatment

Simple, stable fractures that have not shifted out of place may be treated with rest and immobilization — typically a cast or boot along with strict non-weight-bearing precautions — while the bone heals.

Surgical treatment

Complex or displaced fractures, especially those involving the joint surfaces, often need surgery to restore the normal shape of the heel bone and joint alignment. Modern surgical techniques aim to reconstruct the bone as precisely as possible to support long-term function and reduce the risk of complications.

Recovery timeline

Bone healing usually takes about 6 to 12 weeks, but full recovery — including regaining strength, motion, and confidence in the foot — can take several months and typically involves a course of physical therapy.

Why prompt treatment matters

Delaying treatment for a calcaneal fracture can lead to chronic pain, joint instability, and the eventual development of arthritis in the foot and ankle. Because these injuries are often complex, personalized treatment plans and, when appropriate, surgical intervention are important for the best possible long-term outcome.

If you suspect a heel fracture after a fall, accident, or high-impact injury, don't wait — request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices, or seek emergency care if the injury is severe.

Common Questions

Calcaneal fracture FAQs

What does a calcaneal fracture feel like?

A calcaneal fracture typically causes sudden, severe pain in the heel right after an injury, along with swelling, bruising, and difficulty or inability to bear weight on the affected foot. More severe fractures can also cause visible deformity.

What usually causes a broken heel bone?

Most calcaneal fractures come from high-impact events such as falls from a height, car accidents, or hard landings during jumping sports. Direct trauma to the heel can also cause a break.

How is a calcaneal fracture diagnosed?

X-rays are usually the first step and confirm most fractures. For more complex breaks, especially those involving the joint surfaces, a CT scan may be used to get a detailed view of the fracture pattern.

Do all calcaneal fractures need surgery?

No. Simple, stable fractures that haven't shifted out of place may heal with rest and immobilization alone. Complex or displaced fractures, particularly those involving the joint, often need surgery to restore proper alignment.

How long does it take to recover from a calcaneal fracture?

Bone healing generally takes about 6 to 12 weeks, but full recovery — including regaining strength, motion, and confidence in the foot — can take several months and usually involves physical therapy.

What happens if a calcaneal fracture isn't treated?

Delaying treatment can lead to chronic pain, joint instability, and an increased risk of developing arthritis in the foot and ankle. Prompt evaluation and a personalized treatment plan give you the best chance at a full recovery.
