Proven Strategies for Diabetic Foot Care in Older Adults

Aging and diabetes both raise the stakes for your feet. Here's the daily routine, nutrition guidance, and support system that keeps older adults with diabetes safe and mobile.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Diabetic foot care in older adults takes more than daily hygiene. It requires regular self-checks, supportive footwear, steady nutrition and blood sugar control, and prompt medical attention when something looks or feels off. Aging changes skin, circulation, and nerve sensitivity on their own — combine that with diabetes, and small foot problems can turn serious faster than they would in a younger, non-diabetic patient.

The good news is that most diabetic foot complications in older adults are preventable with a consistent routine. Below, our podiatrists walk through the daily habits, nutritional support, and warning signs that make the biggest difference — along with when it's time to bring in a professional.

Why foot care matters more as you age with diabetes

Diabetes can reduce blood flow to the feet and dull nerve sensation over time, a combination that means an older adult may not feel a blister, cut, or pressure sore until it has already become infected. Add the normal effects of aging — thinner skin, slower healing, and reduced balance — and even a minor foot injury can escalate quickly. That's why a proactive routine matters so much more than reacting after a problem appears.

Daily habits that make the biggest difference

Consistency is what protects the foot, not any single dramatic step. A simple daily routine goes a long way:

  • Inspect both feet every day, including the soles and between the toes — a handheld mirror helps with hard-to-see spots
  • Wash feet gently with lukewarm water and mild soap
  • Dry thoroughly, especially between the toes, to prevent fungal growth
  • Moisturize the tops and bottoms of the feet, but avoid lotion between the toes
  • Wear cushioned, seam-free diabetic socks and supportive, well-fitted shoes
  • Avoid walking barefoot, even indoors, to reduce the risk of unnoticed injury
  • Trim toenails straight across and file sharp edges to avoid ingrown nails

Nutrition that supports healing and circulation

What you eat directly affects how well your body heals and how efficiently blood reaches your feet. A diet built around lean proteins, whole grains, fruits, and vegetables supports both blood sugar stability and tissue repair. Foods rich in calcium, vitamin D, and omega-3 fatty acids can further support circulation and bone health, which matters for balance and fall prevention as well. Keeping blood sugar in a steady, well-managed range is one of the most protective things an older adult with diabetes can do for their feet.

Managing pain, swelling, and blood sugar together

Pain and swelling in the feet and lower legs are common in older adults with diabetes and are often connected to blood sugar control. Elevating the feet when resting, staying active within your ability, wearing compression as recommended by your physician, and following your prescribed blood sugar management plan all help keep swelling and discomfort in check. Persistent or worsening swelling should be evaluated rather than managed at home alone.

Why regular professional foot care is essential

Routine podiatrist visits are essential for older adults with diabetes, even when nothing seems wrong. A podiatrist can spot changes in skin, circulation, and nerve function long before they become noticeable to the patient. Most older adults with diabetes benefit from at least one comprehensive foot exam per year, and more frequent visits are appropriate for anyone with existing circulation or nerve issues, prior foot ulcers, or difficulty seeing or reaching their own feet. Professional nail and callus care also reduces the risk of an at-home injury.

Building a support system

Family members, caregivers, and home health aides can play a real role in diabetic foot care, especially for older adults with limited vision, flexibility, or mobility. A second set of eyes on a daily foot check can catch a problem that would otherwise go unnoticed. If you're a caregiver, ask your loved one's podiatrist what specific warning signs to watch for and how often checks should happen.

Warning signs that need prompt attention

Some changes should never wait for a scheduled appointment. Contact a podiatrist promptly if you notice:

  • A sore, cut, or blister that isn't healing
  • Redness, warmth, or swelling around any area of the foot
  • New numbness, tingling, or loss of sensation
  • Sudden color changes in the skin, such as pale, red, or bluish tones
  • Drainage, odor, or a wound that appears to be getting larger

These signs can indicate infection, poor circulation, or nerve damage that needs medical attention before it progresses. If diabetic foot complications have already affected your diabetic foot care needs, or if you have concerns about joint changes like Charcot arthropathy, our physicians can build a plan tailored to you.

Common Questions

Diabetic foot care FAQs

How often should an older adult with diabetes see a podiatrist?

Most older adults with diabetes should have at least one comprehensive foot exam per year. More frequent visits are appropriate for anyone with existing circulation or nerve problems, a history of foot ulcers, or difficulty performing their own daily foot checks.

What should a daily diabetic foot check include?

Check both feet — including the soles and between the toes — for cuts, blisters, redness, swelling, or color changes. A handheld mirror makes it easier to see the bottom of the foot. Wash, dry thoroughly, and moisturize (avoiding between the toes) as part of the same routine.

Can diet really affect foot health in diabetes?

Yes. A diet built around lean proteins, whole grains, fruits, and vegetables supports steady blood sugar and better healing. Nutrients like calcium, vitamin D, and omega-3 fatty acids further support circulation, and stable blood sugar is one of the most protective factors for foot health.

Why is barefoot walking discouraged for people with diabetes?

Reduced nerve sensation from diabetes can mask a cut, splinter, or pressure injury from a hard surface. Wearing supportive, well-fitted shoes and socks — even indoors — reduces the chance of an unnoticed injury that could progress before it's caught.

What foot changes are urgent for someone with diabetes?

A non-healing sore, new redness or warmth, sudden swelling, drainage or odor, or a sudden color change all warrant prompt evaluation. These can signal infection, poor circulation, or nerve damage that needs attention before it becomes more serious.

Can caregivers help with diabetic foot care?

Yes — caregivers and family members can be a valuable second set of eyes, especially for older adults with limited vision, flexibility, or mobility. A daily foot check by a caregiver can catch changes early, before they become urgent problems.
