Diabetic Foot Care: Prevention Tips and Strategies

A handful of daily habits — done consistently — prevent the vast majority of diabetic foot complications before they start.

By the physicians at Wasatch Foot & Ankle Institute · Updated July 13, 2026

Preventing diabetic foot problems comes down to four habits: daily foot inspections, proper footwear, stable blood sugar, and routine podiatric screening. None of these require special equipment or a major lifestyle overhaul — they just need to happen consistently. Below is a practical, everyday plan you can start using immediately, plus the warning signs that mean it's time to call your podiatrist.

Diabetes can cause nerve damage and reduced circulation in the feet, which is why prevention matters more here than almost anywhere else in diabetes care — a problem you can't feel is a problem you're less likely to catch on your own. That's the reasoning behind every strategy below.

Start with a daily foot check

Make it part of your routine, like brushing your teeth: every day, look over both feet — top, bottom, heels, and between the toes — for redness, swelling, cuts, blisters, or anything that looks different from the day before. If you have trouble seeing the bottom of your feet, use a handheld mirror or ask a family member to help. Catching a small problem on day one, rather than day ten, is often the difference between a bandage and a trip to the clinic.

Build a simple washing and moisturizing routine

Wash your feet daily with mild soap and lukewarm (not hot) water, and dry them thoroughly — especially between the toes, where trapped moisture invites fungal infection. Follow with a moisturizer on dry areas like the heels to prevent cracking, but skip the spaces between the toes, since extra moisture there can do more harm than good. Trim toenails straight across to help prevent ingrown nails, and if you have thickened nails, calluses, or corns, let your podiatrist handle them rather than trying it yourself with home tools.

Choose footwear that protects, not just fits

Well-fitting shoes are one of the simplest and most effective prevention tools available. Look for a wide, deep toe box that doesn't crowd or rub the toes, and always wear clean, dry, seamless socks to reduce friction. Shake out your shoes before putting them on to check for pebbles or debris you might not otherwise feel. Never walk barefoot — indoors or out — since an unnoticed step on something sharp is one of the most common ways a diabetic foot injury starts. If you have any structural changes in your feet, ask about custom orthotics, which redistribute pressure away from vulnerable areas.

Keep blood sugar in your target range

Stable blood glucose supports both nerve function and your body's ability to fight infection and heal wounds. Working with your primary care team to keep your numbers close to your target range is one of the most protective things you can do for your feet, even though it isn't foot care in the traditional sense. Elevated blood sugar over time is a major driver of the nerve and circulation changes discussed throughout this guide.

Don't skip your scheduled screenings

Most people with diabetes should have a professional foot exam at least once a year, and more often if you already have neuropathy, poor circulation, or a history of foot ulcers. These visits catch things a home inspection can miss, including reduced pulses, subtle sensation loss, or pressure points that are building toward a callus. Comprehensive diabetic foot care from a podiatrist is what ties all of these prevention strategies together into an actual plan tailored to your risk level.

Know which signs mean "call today," not "mention at my next visit"

Most day-to-day foot care is about steady habits, but a few signs should move you to same-week or same-day care: a wound that hasn't started improving in one to two weeks, drainage or a foul odor from a wound, redness that is spreading, a fever, or any area of the foot that turns dark or black. Numbness, tingling, or a foot that looks or feels different from the other one should also be evaluated rather than watched. When in doubt, it is always better to call and be reassured than to wait.

Common Questions

Diabetic foot care prevention FAQs

How often should I check my feet if I have diabetes?

You should inspect your feet every day, looking over the top, bottom, heels, and between the toes for redness, swelling, cuts, or blisters. Use a mirror or ask for help if you can't easily see the bottom of your feet.

Is it safe to remove my own calluses or corns?

No. Home removal of calluses or corns can accidentally create a wound, which is especially risky if you have reduced sensation or circulation from diabetes. Let your podiatrist manage calluses, corns, and thickened nails safely.

What kind of shoes are best for diabetic foot care?

Look for shoes with a wide, deep toe box that don't rub or crowd the toes, paired with clean, dry, seamless socks. Shake out shoes before wearing them to check for debris, and never walk barefoot, since an unfelt injury is one of the most common causes of diabetic foot ulcers.

How does blood sugar control relate to foot health?

Elevated blood sugar over time damages the nerves and blood vessels that keep feet healthy, while stable blood sugar supports better healing and infection resistance. Keeping glucose in your target range is one of the most protective long-term steps you can take for your feet.

How often should I have a professional diabetic foot exam?

Most people with diabetes should have a foot exam at least once a year. If you already have neuropathy, poor circulation, or a history of foot ulcers, your podiatrist may recommend checkups every few months instead.

What foot symptoms need same-week medical attention?

Contact your podiatrist promptly for a wound that hasn't improved in one to two weeks, drainage or a foul odor, spreading redness, fever, darkened or blackened skin, or any new numbness or shape change in the foot. These signs should not wait until your next scheduled visit.
