Posterior Tibial Tendon Dysfunction

Pain along the inside of your ankle and a flattening arch are the earliest signs of posterior tibial tendon dysfunction — the sooner we catch it, the more treatment options you have.

What is posterior tibial tendon dysfunction?

Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD) occurs when the posterior tibial tendon — one of the major supporting structures of the foot — becomes damaged or inflamed and loses its ability to hold up the arch. As the tendon weakens, the arch of the foot gradually flattens. PTTD is the most common cause of flatfoot that develops in adulthood, which is why it is often called adult acquired flatfoot.

The posterior tibial tendon runs from the calf, behind the inside of the ankle, and attaches along the bottom of the foot, where it helps support the arch and stabilize the foot with every step. When the tendon is overused or injured, it can stretch, become inflamed, or partially tear, and without this support the foot's structure begins to change over time.

What are the symptoms of posterior tibial tendon dysfunction?

Early PTTD typically causes pain and swelling along the inside of the foot and ankle, tracking the path of the tendon. As the condition progresses, the changes in foot shape become more noticeable. Common signs include:

  • Pain, redness, warmth, and swelling on the inside of the foot and ankle
  • An arch that appears to be flattening or has already flattened
  • The front of the foot and toes turning outward
  • The ankle rolling inward
  • In later stages, pain shifting to the outside of the foot below the ankle as the bones begin to impinge
  • Difficulty standing on the toes of the affected foot

What causes posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, and who is at risk?

PTTD is most commonly caused by chronic overuse of the posterior tibial tendon. Repetitive activities such as running, walking, hiking, and stair climbing put ongoing strain on the tendon, and over time this strain can lead to inflammation, degeneration, and tearing. The condition tends to develop gradually rather than from a single injury.

Because PTTD changes how the foot bears weight, it can also contribute to the arthritis and joint stress seen in conditions like flat feet and arthritis of the foot and ankle if it is not addressed.

How do we diagnose posterior tibial tendon dysfunction?

We diagnose PTTD with a physical examination that evaluates the shape of your foot, the strength of the posterior tibial tendon, and how you stand and walk. Your podiatrist will look for the classic signs of a flattening arch, outward-turning forefoot, and inward-rolling ankle, and will check for tenderness along the tendon's path.

Imaging such as X-rays, ultrasound, or MRI may be used to evaluate the extent of tendon damage and to check for related changes in the bones and joints. Identifying the stage of PTTD is important, because treatment options differ depending on how advanced the condition is.

How is posterior tibial tendon dysfunction treated?

Treatment for PTTD depends on how far the condition has progressed. Early-stage PTTD generally responds well to conservative care, while more advanced cases may require surgery to restore foot function.

Nonsurgical treatment

  • Orthotic devices or ankle bracing to support the arch and reduce strain on the tendon
  • Immobilization in a short-leg cast or walking boot to rest the tendon
  • Physical therapy, including ultrasound therapy and rehabilitative exercises
  • NSAIDs such as ibuprofen to reduce pain and inflammation
  • Shoe modifications with built-in arch-support inserts

Surgery

Surgery becomes necessary when PTTD has progressed substantially or has failed to improve with non-surgical treatment. The specific procedure depends on the stage of the condition and may involve repairing or transferring a tendon, realigning bones, or fusing joints to restore stability. Our surgeons will discuss the most appropriate option for your stage of PTTD and your activity goals.

What is recovery from posterior tibial tendon dysfunction like?

Recovery from nonsurgical PTTD treatment often involves a gradual return to activity as pain and swelling improve, guided by ongoing use of orthotics or bracing and continued physical therapy to protect the tendon long term. Because PTTD tends to progress if untreated, sticking with your supportive footwear and activity modifications helps prevent the arch from flattening further.

If surgery is required, recovery is longer and typically includes a period of immobilization followed by supervised physical therapy to rebuild strength and restore normal walking mechanics. Our team will give you a personalized recovery timeline based on the specific procedure performed.

When should you see a podiatrist for posterior tibial tendon dysfunction?

See a podiatrist as soon as you notice pain or swelling along the inside of your ankle, or if you feel your arch beginning to flatten. PTTD is a progressive condition, and catching it early gives you access to more conservative treatment options and a better chance of avoiding surgery.

You should also seek evaluation if you notice your foot or toes turning outward, your ankle rolling inward, or new pain developing on the outside of your foot, as these can indicate the condition is advancing. You can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction FAQs

What is adult acquired flatfoot?

Adult acquired flatfoot is another name for posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (PTTD), the most common cause of flatfoot that develops during adulthood. It happens when the posterior tibial tendon becomes damaged and can no longer adequately support the arch, causing the foot to gradually flatten.

What does posterior tibial tendon dysfunction feel like?

Early PTTD usually causes pain, warmth, and swelling along the inside of the ankle and foot, tracing the path of the tendon. As the condition progresses, the arch may flatten, the forefoot can turn outward, and pain can shift to the outside of the foot below the ankle.

Can posterior tibial tendon dysfunction be treated without surgery?

Yes, especially when caught early. Orthotics, ankle bracing, immobilization, physical therapy, and anti-inflammatory medication can relieve symptoms and support the arch in earlier stages. More advanced cases that have not responded to conservative care may need surgery.

Will my flat foot get worse if I don't treat it?

PTTD tends to be progressive, meaning it can worsen over time without treatment. Left unaddressed, it can lead to further arch collapse, changes in foot alignment, and arthritis in the foot and ankle. Early treatment gives you the best chance of avoiding these complications.

What orthotics or braces help with PTTD?

Custom orthotic devices and ankle bracing designed to support the arch and control excess motion are common first-line treatments. The right device depends on the stage of your PTTD, and your podiatrist can recommend the best option for your foot.

When is surgery needed for posterior tibial tendon dysfunction?

Surgery is generally reserved for PTTD that has progressed substantially or has not improved with nonsurgical treatment. Procedures vary based on the stage of the condition and may involve tendon repair, tendon transfer, bone realignment, or joint fusion.

When should I see a podiatrist for PTTD symptoms?

See a podiatrist as soon as you notice pain or swelling along the inside of your ankle or a flattening arch. Because PTTD is progressive, early evaluation preserves more treatment options. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
