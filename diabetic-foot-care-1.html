Diabetic Foot Care

Diabetes can be dangerous to your feet — even a small cut can produce serious consequences. Daily attention and regular professional checkups keep small problems from becoming big ones.

Why does diabetes affect foot health?

Diabetes can be dangerous to your feet — even a small cut can produce serious consequences. Two changes in the body explain why. First, diabetes can cause nerve damage, or peripheral neuropathy, which reduces feeling in the feet so that cuts, blisters, or pressure points can go unnoticed. Second, diabetes can reduce blood flow to the feet, which slows healing and lowers the body's ability to fight infection.

Together, reduced sensation and reduced circulation mean that a minor injury — a blister from a new shoe, a small cut while trimming a nail — can progress to a wound, ulcer, or infection before it's ever felt. That is why proactive, daily foot care and regular professional checkups are such an important part of managing diabetes.

What are the warning signs to watch for?

Because reduced sensation can mask early problems, visual inspection matters as much as how your feet feel. Signs that need prompt attention include:

  • Cuts, blisters, or sores that aren't healing as expected
  • Redness, warmth, or swelling anywhere on the foot
  • Changes in skin color or temperature
  • Numbness, tingling, or burning sensations
  • Ingrown toenails, corns, or calluses that are painful or discolored
  • Any drainage, odor, or sign of infection

If you notice any of these changes, don't wait to see if it resolves on its own — contact our office promptly. Reduced sensation from neuropathy can also contribute to more serious complications such as Charcot arthropathy, which makes early attention especially important.

What daily foot care habits protect against diabetic complications?

Consistent daily habits are the foundation of diabetic foot care. We recommend the following routine:

  • Inspect your feet daily, using a mirror if needed to check the soles and between the toes for cuts, blisters, redness, swelling, or nail changes
  • Wash with lukewarm water, never hot, since reduced sensation can make it hard to judge water temperature safely
  • Dry gently, especially between the toes, to prevent moisture buildup
  • Moisturize daily, but avoid the area between the toes to reduce the risk of fungal infection
  • Trim nails carefully, cutting straight across and never too short
  • Leave corns and calluses to a professional rather than trimming them yourself
  • Wear clean, dry socks daily and avoid socks with tight elastic bands
  • Never walk barefoot, even indoors
  • Check the inside of your shoes before putting them on to make sure nothing is caught inside
  • Keep blood sugar well controlled and avoid smoking, both of which support healthy circulation and healing

How do we support diabetic foot health at our office?

Alongside the habits above, regular professional foot exams are essential for anyone with diabetes. During a diabetic foot exam, we check circulation and sensation, inspect the skin and nails closely, and look for early signs of pressure points, calluses, or skin breakdown before they progress. We also address nail and callus care that isn't safe to manage at home.

When appropriate, we recommend protective footwear or custom orthotics to redistribute pressure away from vulnerable areas, and we coordinate closely with your primary care or endocrinology team on the medical management of your diabetes.

How often should someone with diabetes have their feet checked?

Most people with diabetes benefit from a professional foot exam at least once a year, though your podiatrist may recommend more frequent visits if you have neuropathy, circulation problems, a history of foot ulcers, or other risk factors. Between visits, your own daily inspection remains your first line of defense.

What role does footwear play in diabetic foot care?

The right shoes are one of the simplest and most effective ways to protect an at-risk foot. Well-fitted shoes with a roomy toe box, supportive soles, and no seams or rough spots that could rub against the skin help prevent the friction and pressure points that lead to blisters and ulcers. Breaking in new shoes gradually, rather than wearing them for a full day right away, gives you a chance to catch any hot spots before they become a problem. For patients with significant neuropathy, deformity, or a history of ulcers, specialized diabetic or protective footwear built around a custom orthotic can further reduce pressure on vulnerable areas of the foot.

When should you see a podiatrist for a diabetes-related foot concern?

Contact our office promptly if you notice any wound, redness, swelling, or change in sensation that doesn't resolve quickly, and seek same-day care for any sign of infection, a wound that isn't healing, or sudden changes in the shape or color of the foot. Regular scheduled visits, even when nothing seems wrong, are just as important as urgent care when a problem appears. Common Questions

Diabetic foot care FAQs

Why is diabetic foot care so important?

Diabetes can reduce sensation and blood flow to the feet, so minor injuries like cuts or blisters can go unnoticed and heal more slowly. Left unaddressed, small problems can progress to wounds, ulcers, or infections — daily care and regular checkups catch issues early.

How often should I check my own feet?

Inspect your feet every day, using a mirror if needed to see the soles and between your toes. Look for cuts, blisters, redness, swelling, or changes in your nails, and contact our office if anything doesn't look right.

How often should I see a podiatrist if I have diabetes?

Most people with diabetes benefit from a professional foot exam at least once a year, with more frequent visits recommended if you have neuropathy, circulation problems, or a history of foot ulcers.

Can I trim my own corns and calluses?

It's best to leave corns and calluses to a professional. Trimming them yourself carries a risk of injury, and with reduced sensation from diabetes, an accidental cut may not be felt right away.

Why shouldn't I walk barefoot if I have diabetes?

Walking barefoot, even indoors, increases the risk of stepping on something that causes a cut or puncture wound. Reduced sensation from neuropathy means you might not feel the injury right away, giving it time to worsen before it's noticed.

What should I do if I notice a wound or sore on my foot?

Contact our office promptly for any wound that isn't healing as expected, or any redness, swelling, warmth, or drainage. Do not wait to see if it improves on its own — early treatment is the best way to prevent a serious complication.

How do I schedule a diabetic foot exam?

You can request an appointment online or call our Farmington or South Ogden office directly to schedule a diabetic foot exam or address a specific concern.
