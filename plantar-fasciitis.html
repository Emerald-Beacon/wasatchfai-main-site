Plantar Fasciitis

Stabbing heel pain that's worst with your first steps in the morning — we find the source and build a plan that finally ends it.

What is plantar fasciitis?

Plantar fasciitis is inflammation of the plantar fascia — the thick band of tissue running along the bottom of your foot from your heel bone to your toes. It is the most common cause of heel pain, producing a sharp, stabbing ache that is usually worst with your first steps in the morning. It happens when this tough band is repeatedly overstretched or develops small tears.

The plantar fascia is one of the hardest-working structures in your body, supporting your arch and absorbing shock with every step. When the tissue is strained beyond what it can tolerate, it becomes irritated and inflamed. Left unaddressed, that ongoing tension at the heel can also contribute to the formation of a heel spur, a small bony growth where the fascia attaches. The good news is that the large majority of cases improve with conservative, nonsurgical care — especially when treatment starts early. If your heel pain overlaps with other symptoms, our general heel pain guide can help you compare possible causes.

What are the symptoms of plantar fasciitis?

The hallmark symptom is a stabbing heel pain that is sharpest with your first steps after waking or after long periods of rest, then often eases as you move. Pain typically centers on the bottom or inner edge of the heel. Common signs include:

  • Stabbing pain on the heel, near the heel, or on the bottom of the foot
  • "First step pain" — a sharp jolt when you stand up after sleeping or sitting
  • Pain that flares up after exercise rather than during it
  • Discomfort after prolonged standing, walking, or sitting
  • Heel tenderness that improves with gentle movement but returns after rest

What causes plantar fasciitis, and who is at risk?

Plantar fasciitis is caused by repeated strain on the plantar fascia that leads to irritation and small tears. Runners and athletic people are at higher risk because of repeated impact, but the condition is also very common in people who are overweight or who spend long hours on their feet. Factors that raise your risk include:

  • Tight Achilles tendons or calf muscles that increase pull on the heel
  • Flat feet or high arches that change how weight loads the fascia
  • Frequently wearing high-heeled or worn-out, unsupportive shoes
  • An unusual walking pattern or foot positioning
  • Being overweight or obese, which adds load to the arch
  • Being age 40 or older
  • Exercise or activity that places excessive strain on the fascia
  • Jobs that require extended standing or walking on hard surfaces

Plantar fasciitis is more common among women than among men. Because tight calf muscles and poor arch support are frequent contributors, Achilles tendon problems and flat feet often go hand in hand with plantar fasciitis.

How do we diagnose plantar fasciitis?

We diagnose plantar fasciitis with a hands-on physical examination and a review of your symptoms and medical history — imaging is not usually required. During the exam, your podiatrist locates the exact point of tenderness, checks your arch and range of motion, and assesses how you walk to understand the strain on your foot.

In some cases we order imaging such as an X-ray or MRI. These studies do not confirm plantar fasciitis directly, but they help pinpoint the source of pain and rule out other causes of heel pain, such as a stress fracture, nerve entrapment, or arthritis. Getting the diagnosis right matters, because heel pain has several possible causes and each is treated differently.

How is plantar fasciitis treated?

The great majority of plantar fasciitis cases resolve with nonsurgical treatment, so we start with the most conservative options and advance only if your pain persists. A typical plan combines at-home measures with in-office therapies tailored to how long you have had symptoms and how active you are.

Nonsurgical treatment

  • Rest, ice, and activity changes to calm the initial inflammation
  • Stretching and physical therapy for the plantar fascia and calf
  • NSAIDs and other medications to reduce pain and swelling
  • Custom orthotics and supportive footwear to offload the arch — see our custom orthotics options
  • Night splints that keep the fascia gently stretched while you sleep
  • Corticosteroid injections for stubborn, focused pain

In-office & minimally invasive options

For heel pain that has not responded to first-line care, we offer advanced in-office treatments that can help you avoid open surgery:

  • Extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) — sound-wave energy that stimulates healing in the fascia without incisions
  • The Tenex procedure — a minimally invasive, ultrasound-guided technique that removes damaged tissue through a tiny opening, typically under local anesthesia

Surgery

Surgery is rarely needed and is reserved for the small number of cases that do not improve after an extended course of conservative care. When appropriate, the procedure involves partially releasing the plantar fascia from the heel bone to relieve tension. Our physicians will always exhaust nonsurgical options first and discuss the risks and benefits with you before recommending surgery.

What is recovery from plantar fasciitis like?

Most people recover from plantar fasciitis without surgery, though it takes patience — meaningful improvement often unfolds over several weeks to a few months of consistent treatment. Sticking with your stretching routine, wearing supportive shoes or orthotics, and easing back into activity gradually are the biggest factors in a lasting recovery.

Because the plantar fascia is under load every time you stand, doing too much too soon is the most common reason symptoms linger or return. If you have advanced in-office treatment such as shock wave therapy or the Tenex procedure, we will give you specific, personalized aftercare guidance so you know exactly what to expect at each stage.

When should you see a podiatrist for heel pain?

See a podiatrist if heel pain lasts more than a week or two of home care, keeps you from your normal activities, or returns each morning. Early treatment is more effective and can prevent a short-term problem from becoming a chronic one.

You should seek care sooner if you have severe pain, swelling, numbness or tingling, signs of infection, or heel pain following an injury — these may point to a different or more serious condition. People with diabetes or poor circulation should have any new foot pain evaluated promptly. When you are ready, you can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Plantar fasciitis FAQs

What does plantar fasciitis feel like?

Plantar fasciitis usually feels like a sharp, stabbing pain in the bottom of the heel that is worst with your first steps in the morning or after sitting for a while. The pain often eases as you move around, then returns after long periods of standing or at the end of the day.

How long does plantar fasciitis take to heal?

Most cases improve over several weeks to a few months with consistent conservative treatment. Recovery depends on how long you have had symptoms and how closely you follow your stretching, footwear, and activity plan. Starting treatment early generally leads to faster relief.

Will plantar fasciitis go away on its own?

Mild cases sometimes settle with rest and supportive shoes, but heel pain that lasts more than a week or two often needs treatment to fully resolve. Ignoring it can allow the problem to become chronic and harder to treat, so it is best to be evaluated if the pain persists.

What is the fastest way to relieve plantar fasciitis pain?

Early relief usually comes from resting the foot, applying ice, stretching the calf and plantar fascia, and switching to supportive footwear or orthotics. For pain that does not settle, in-office options such as a corticosteroid injection or shock wave therapy can speed things along. Your podiatrist can recommend the right combination for your situation.

Is plantar fasciitis the same as a heel spur?

No — they are related but not the same. Plantar fasciitis is inflammation of the fascia, while a heel spur is a small bony growth that can form where the fascia attaches to the heel. Many people with heel spurs have no pain, and treating the plantar fasciitis usually resolves the symptoms.

Do I need surgery for plantar fasciitis?

Surgery is rarely necessary. The large majority of patients improve with nonsurgical care, and minimally invasive in-office options like shock wave therapy or the Tenex procedure help many people avoid an operation. Surgery is only considered when symptoms persist after an extended course of conservative treatment.

When should I see a podiatrist for heel pain?

See a podiatrist if your heel pain lasts more than a week or two, limits your activities, or keeps returning each morning. Seek care sooner for severe pain, swelling, numbness, or pain after an injury. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
