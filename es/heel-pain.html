Dolor de talón

El dolor de talón casi nunca se resuelve solo con esperar. Encontramos la causa exacta y la tratamos.

El dolor de talón tiene varias causas distintas, y cada una se trata de forma diferente. La más común es la fascitis plantar, pero el espolón calcáneo, la tendinitis de Aquiles, el síndrome del túnel tarsiano y las fracturas por estrés producen molestias parecidas. Identificar cuál de ellas es la suya determina por completo el tratamiento.

Señales de que debe consultar

El dolor leve después de un día largo suele ser normal. Conviene que lo evalúe un especialista cuando:

  • El dolor dura más de dos semanas a pesar del reposo
  • Los primeros pasos de la mañana son muy dolorosos
  • Cambia su forma de caminar o de apoyar el pie
  • Hay hinchazón, enrojecimiento o fiebre
  • No puede apoyar el peso sobre el talón

Cómo se diagnostica

La evaluación empieza por su historia clínica y un examen físico: dónde duele exactamente, en qué momento del día y con qué actividades. Usamos radiografías y ecografía en el consultorio cuando hacen falta para distinguir entre un problema de la fascia, del tendón, del hueso o del nervio.

Tratamientos disponibles

La gran mayoría de los pacientes mejora sin cirugía. Según la causa, el plan puede incluir estiramientos dirigidos, cambios de calzado, plantillas ortopédicas hechas a medida, terapia física, inmovilización temporal, infiltraciones o terapia de ondas de choque. La cirugía se reserva para los casos que no responden al tratamiento conservador, y su médico le explicará con detalle qué esperar.

Si su dolor se concentra en la planta del pie y es peor por la mañana, lea también sobre la fascitis plantar.

¿Cuánto tarda la recuperación?

Depende de la causa y de cada paciente. Un caso reciente de fascitis plantar suele responder en semanas; un problema de años puede necesitar varios meses de trabajo constante. Su médico le dará un plazo realista para su caso concreto, no una promesa general.

What is heel pain?

Heel pain is discomfort centered on the underside, back, or side of the heel bone, and it is one of the most common reasons people visit a podiatrist. It can range from a dull ache after a long day on your feet to a sharp, stabbing pain with every step. Because the heel absorbs the shock of nearly every stride you take, even a small amount of irritation there can make walking, standing, and exercising genuinely difficult.

Heel pain is not a single condition — it's a symptom that can come from several different sources in and around the heel. The most common underlying cause is plantar fasciitis, inflammation of the band of tissue that runs along the bottom of the foot, but heel spurs, arthritis, direct injury, and tendon irritation can all produce similar symptoms. Getting an accurate diagnosis is the key to effective treatment, since each of these causes responds to a different approach.

What are the symptoms of heel pain?

Symptoms vary depending on the underlying cause, but most people describe an ache, throb, or sharp pain localized to the heel that worsens with activity or after rest. Common signs include:

  • Pain on the bottom, side, or back of the heel
  • Stiffness or sharp pain with your first steps in the morning
  • Pain that builds throughout the day with standing or walking
  • Swelling, warmth, or tenderness around the heel
  • Pain that flares after exercise or a change in activity level

If your pain is sharpest with your first steps after waking, that pattern is especially characteristic of plantar fasciitis — see our plantar fasciitis guide for a closer look at that specific condition.

What causes heel pain, and who is at risk?

Heel pain typically comes from one of a handful of common sources, each with its own cause and treatment path.

  • Heel spurs — a calcium deposit that builds up on the underside of the heel bone. Heel spurs can vary in size and are generally considered quite painful, often developing alongside long-term strain on the plantar fascia.
  • Arthritis of the foot — inflammation of the joints in and around the foot, which can cause heel pain either directly or indirectly by changing the way you walk.
  • Heel injuries — direct trauma from improper foot placement during walking or running, which can range from bruising to a stress fracture of the heel bone.
  • Tendonitis — inflammation or irritation of the tendons around the heel, including the Achilles tendon, which can cause pain that is felt locally or referred elsewhere in the foot.
  • Plantar fasciitis — the most frequent cause of heel pain, caused by irritation of the plantar fascia; learn more on our plantar fasciitis page.

Runners and other athletes, people who are overweight, and anyone who spends long hours standing or walking on hard surfaces face a higher risk of developing heel pain. Because Achilles tendon problems and flat feet both change the way load travels through the heel, they are common contributing factors as well.

How do we diagnose heel pain?

We diagnose heel pain with a hands-on physical exam, a review of your symptoms, and a discussion of your activity level and footwear. Your podiatrist will locate the exact point of tenderness, check your range of motion, and evaluate your gait to understand where the extra strain is coming from.

In some cases we order imaging such as an X-ray to look for a heel spur, stress fracture, or arthritic changes in the joint. Because several different conditions can cause similar symptoms, a clear diagnosis is essential to choosing the treatment that will actually resolve your pain.

How is heel pain treated?

Treatment depends on the underlying cause, but most heel pain responds well to conservative care started early. We generally begin with the least invasive options and escalate only if pain persists.

Conservative treatment

  • Icing and pain management — reducing swelling with ice and an over-the-counter anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen
  • Physical therapy — targeted stretching and gait modification to reduce unnecessary stress on the heel while it heals
  • Custom shoes or inserts — orthopedic support that reduces heel stress and helps prevent future problems; see our custom orthotics options
  • Heel injections — a steroid injection to manage pain and swelling when other conservative methods haven't been enough

In-office & advanced options

For heel pain that doesn't improve with first-line care, we offer advanced in-office treatments designed to help you avoid open surgery. If you've been dealing with heel pain that just won't heal, our in-office heel pain procedure page walks through what an advanced, minimally invasive option looks like and whether it may be right for you.

What is recovery from heel pain like?

Most heel pain improves steadily with consistent conservative care, though the timeline depends on the underlying cause and how long you've had symptoms. Sticking with your stretching routine, wearing supportive footwear, and easing back into activity gradually all help prevent the pain from returning.

Doing too much too soon is the most common reason heel pain lingers or comes back. If you move on to an in-office or advanced treatment, we'll give you specific aftercare guidance so you know exactly what to expect at each stage of recovery.

When should you see a podiatrist for heel pain?

The sooner a podiatrist can evaluate and treat your heel pain, the less chance it has of getting worse. See a podiatrist if your heel pain lasts more than a week or two despite home care, limits your normal activities, or keeps returning. Seek care sooner if you have significant swelling, numbness, signs of infection, or heel pain following a specific injury.

People with diabetes or circulation problems should have any new foot or heel pain evaluated promptly. When you're ready, you can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Preguntas sobre dolor de talón

¿Por qué me duele más el talón al levantarme?

El dolor agudo en los primeros pasos de la mañana es el signo clásico de la fascitis plantar. El tejido se contrae durante la noche y se estira de golpe al ponerse de pie, por eso duele más al levantarse y mejora al caminar un rato.

¿El dolor de talón se quita solo?

A veces mejora con reposo y buen calzado, pero cuando lleva más de dos semanas o vuelve una y otra vez suele indicar un problema estructural que no se resuelve esperando. Cuanto antes se trate, más sencillo suele ser el tratamiento.

¿Necesito cirugía para el espolón calcáneo?

Casi nunca. El espolón en sí rara vez es la fuente del dolor; lo habitual es que el problema sea el tejido inflamado a su alrededor, que responde al tratamiento conservador. La cirugía se considera solo cuando ese tratamiento no funciona.
