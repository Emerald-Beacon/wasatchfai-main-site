Talar Dome Lesions

Deep ankle pain, clicking, or a sense of instability after a sprain that never quite healed right — we find the cartilage damage behind it and build a plan to fix it.

What is a talar dome lesion?

A talar dome lesion is an injury to the cartilage and underlying bone of the talus, the bone that forms the top of the ankle joint and connects your foot to your leg. You may also hear it called an osteochondral defect (OCD) or an osteochondral lesion of the talus (OLT) — different names for the same underlying problem, where a patch of cartilage and the bone beneath it is damaged.

The dome of the talus is covered in smooth cartilage that lets the ankle glide through its normal range of motion. When that cartilage is injured and does not heal properly, the affected area can soften, crack, or in some cases separate into a loose fragment inside the joint. Because cartilage has a limited blood supply, these injuries are notoriously slow to heal on their own, which is why persistent deep ankle pain after a sprain deserves a closer look. If your pain is centered more on the outer ankle ligaments than deep in the joint, our lateral ankle instability page may be a better match for your symptoms.

What are the symptoms of a talar dome lesion?

Talar dome lesions often cause a deep, aching pain within the ankle joint itself rather than pain along the outer ligaments. Symptoms typically worsen with weight-bearing activity and can include:

  • Deep, aching ankle pain that intensifies with standing, walking, or sports
  • A "clicking" or "catching" sensation when moving the ankle
  • Episodes of the ankle locking or feeling unstable
  • Swelling that comes and goes, often tied to activity level
  • Persistent stiffness or a reduced range of motion

Because these symptoms can overlap with a simple sprain, a talar dome lesion is often missed at first and only diagnosed when pain lingers well beyond the expected healing window for a sprain.

What causes a talar dome lesion?

Most talar dome lesions are caused by an ankle injury, most commonly an ankle sprain. During a significant sprain, the talus can be compressed or twisted against the adjacent bones with enough force to damage the cartilage surface. In the weeks that follow, the injured cartilage fails to heal properly and begins to deteriorate or break apart rather than knitting back together.

Not every talar dome lesion is tied to a single memorable injury — repetitive stress on the ankle joint, prior ankle fractures, and chronic instability from earlier untreated sprains can all contribute over time. This is one of the reasons we encourage patients to have ankle injuries properly evaluated rather than simply "walking off" a sprain.

How do we diagnose a talar dome lesion?

Diagnosis starts with a physical examination and a detailed review of your injury history and current symptoms. Your podiatrist will assess your range of motion, check for tenderness deep within the joint, and evaluate ankle stability. In some cases, an injection of local anesthetic into the joint is used to help confirm that the pain is coming from within the ankle itself rather than the surrounding ligaments.

Imaging plays a key role in confirming the diagnosis. X-rays can sometimes reveal a lesion, but many talar dome injuries are cartilage-only and do not show up well on a standard X-ray. An MRI or other advanced imaging is often needed to see the full extent of the cartilage and bone involvement and to guide treatment planning.

How is a talar dome lesion treated?

Treatment depends on the size, location, and severity of the lesion, as well as how long you have had symptoms. We generally begin with nonsurgical care and move to surgery only when conservative measures do not provide lasting relief.

Nonsurgical treatment

  • Immobilization in a cast or walking boot to protect the joint and allow the cartilage to settle
  • NSAIDs to help control pain and inflammation
  • Physical therapy to restore range of motion and rebuild strength around the joint
  • Ankle bracing for added stability during activity and recovery

Surgery

When conservative treatment fails to relieve symptoms, surgery may be recommended. Surgical treatment typically involves removing the loose bone and cartilage fragments from the joint and, depending on the case, stimulating new cartilage growth in the affected area. Your podiatrist will walk you through the specific approach that fits your lesion and activity goals.

What is recovery from a talar dome lesion like?

Recovery timelines vary widely depending on whether treatment is nonsurgical or surgical and how significant the cartilage damage is. Nonsurgical care generally involves a period of protected weight-bearing followed by a gradual return to activity guided by physical therapy. Surgical recovery takes longer, since the joint needs time to protect newly stimulated or repaired cartilage before returning to full activity.

Because cartilage heals slowly and unevenly, patience and following your podiatrist's activity guidelines closely make a real difference in how well the joint recovers.

When should you see a podiatrist for ankle pain?

See a podiatrist if deep ankle pain, clicking, catching, or a sense of instability persists after a sprain — especially once the initial swelling and bruising have resolved but the joint still does not feel right. These are signs the injury may involve more than the ligaments alone.

Seek prompt evaluation if you experience locking of the ankle joint, an inability to bear weight, or pain that is getting worse rather than better. When you are ready, you can request an appointment at either of our Northern Utah offices.

Common Questions

Talar dome lesion FAQs

What does a talar dome lesion feel like?

Most people describe a deep, aching pain inside the ankle joint that worsens with standing, walking, or sports. Many also notice clicking, catching, or occasional locking of the joint, along with swelling that comes and goes with activity.

How do you get a talar dome lesion?

Talar dome lesions are usually caused by an ankle sprain or other ankle injury that damages the cartilage covering the talus. Instead of healing normally, the injured cartilage deteriorates or breaks apart, sometimes leaving a loose fragment within the joint.

Can a talar dome lesion heal without surgery?

Many smaller lesions improve with nonsurgical treatment such as immobilization, physical therapy, and anti-inflammatory medication. Surgery is generally reserved for lesions that do not respond to a genuine course of conservative care.

Why do talar dome lesions get missed at first?

Because the symptoms — deep ankle pain, swelling, and stiffness — overlap closely with a routine ankle sprain, a talar dome lesion is often assumed to be a sprain until pain lingers beyond the normal healing window. An MRI is frequently needed to confirm the diagnosis.

What imaging is used to diagnose a talar dome lesion?

X-rays are often the first step but can miss cartilage-only injuries. An MRI is typically needed to fully evaluate the extent of the cartilage and underlying bone damage and to help plan treatment.

What happens during surgery for a talar dome lesion?

Surgery typically involves removing loose bone and cartilage fragments from the joint. Depending on the size and location of the lesion, additional techniques may be used to stimulate healthy cartilage growth in the damaged area.

When should I see a podiatrist for ankle pain?

See a podiatrist if deep ankle pain, clicking, catching, or instability continues after a sprain, especially once swelling and bruising have resolved. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
