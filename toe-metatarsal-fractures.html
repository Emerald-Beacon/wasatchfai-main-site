Toe & Metatarsal Fractures

A stubbed toe or sudden foot pain after an impact can mean more than a bruise — we diagnose the break and get you healing the right way.

What is a toe or metatarsal fracture?

A fracture is simply a broken bone, and the foot is a common site for one because of how much force it absorbs every day. Your foot contains 26 bones in total — 19 of them are toe bones, called phalanges, and the rest include the metatarsals, the five long bones that make up the midfoot and connect your toes to the rest of your foot.

Fractures generally fall into two categories. A traumatic, or acute, fracture happens instantly from a direct impact — stubbing your toe hard, dropping something heavy on your foot, or a twisting injury during sports. A stress fracture, on the other hand, develops gradually from repetitive strain, such as running or standing for long hours, where small cracks form in the bone faster than it can repair itself.

What are the symptoms of a traumatic fracture?

Traumatic fractures usually happen at a clear, identifiable moment, and often come with recognizable warning signs:

  • An audible crack or snap at the moment of injury
  • Sharp, "pinpoint" pain right at the site of impact, which sometimes eases somewhat after the first few hours
  • A toe that looks visibly abnormal, crooked, or out of place
  • Bruising and swelling that typically appear within 24 hours

Importantly, being able to walk or move the toe does not rule out a fracture. Many people assume that if they can still put weight on the foot, nothing is broken — but this is a common misconception, and a proper evaluation is the only way to know for sure.

What are the symptoms of a stress fracture?

Stress fractures develop more gradually and can be easy to dismiss at first because there is no single injury moment. Watch for:

  • Pain that appears during normal activity, such as walking or standing
  • Pain that eases with rest but returns once activity resumes
  • "Pinpoint" tenderness when the area is touched directly
  • Swelling in the area without the bruising typically seen in traumatic fractures

Because these symptoms can be mistaken for a minor strain, stress fractures are sometimes not diagnosed until they have progressed. If your pain is centered specifically in the fifth metatarsal near the outside of the foot, our fifth metatarsal fracture page covers that injury in more detail.

What happens if a fracture isn't properly treated?

Fractures that are not diagnosed and treated correctly can lead to lasting problems. Possible complications include bony deformities that limit the normal movement of the foot, the development of arthritis in the affected joint, chronic pain, and nonunion — where the bone fails to heal back together properly. These complications are one of the main reasons it is worth having any suspected fracture evaluated rather than assuming it will heal on its own.

How are toe fractures treated?

Most toe fractures are treated conservatively. Common approaches include:

  • Rest to allow the bone to heal without repeated stress
  • Splinting to hold the toe in proper alignment
  • Rigid or stiff-soled footwear to limit motion at the fracture site while you continue to walk
  • Buddy taping to a neighboring toe, when appropriate for the specific fracture pattern
  • Surgery with fixation devices for fractures that are significantly displaced or unstable

How are metatarsal fractures treated?

Metatarsal fractures are treated based on the specific bone involved, the fracture pattern, and how displaced it is. Options include:

  • Rest and activity avoidance to protect the healing bone
  • Immobilization or casting to keep the foot stable during healing
  • Surgery for breaks that are displaced or otherwise unstable
  • Physical therapy and rehabilitation to restore strength and motion once the bone has healed

What is recovery from a toe or metatarsal fracture like?

Recovery time varies depending on which bone is broken, the fracture pattern, and whether treatment is nonsurgical or surgical. Toe fractures often heal over several weeks with protective footwear, while metatarsal fractures — particularly those requiring surgery — can take longer and involve a more gradual return to weight-bearing activity. Following your podiatrist's guidance on activity restrictions is the most important factor in avoiding complications like nonunion.

When should you see a podiatrist for a foot injury?

See a podiatrist any time you suspect a broken toe or metatarsal, even if you can still walk on the foot — mobility alone does not rule out a fracture. Seek prompt care for a visibly deformed toe, significant swelling or bruising, or pain that does not improve within a few days. Common Questions

Toe & metatarsal fracture FAQs

Can I walk on a broken toe or foot bone?

Often, yes — but the ability to walk does not rule out a fracture. Many toe and some metatarsal fractures still allow limited walking, which is why an evaluation is the only reliable way to know whether a bone is broken.

How can I tell if my toe is broken versus just bruised?

A break often involves pinpoint pain right at the injury site, visible deformity, and swelling or bruising that develops within 24 hours. A traumatic fracture may also produce an audible crack at the moment of injury. Because bruises and fractures can look similar, an exam or X-ray is the best way to be sure.

What is the difference between a traumatic fracture and a stress fracture?

A traumatic fracture happens instantly from a direct impact, such as stubbing your toe or dropping something on your foot. A stress fracture develops gradually from repetitive strain, like running or prolonged standing, and often causes pain that builds with activity and eases with rest.

What happens if a fracture is not treated properly?

Untreated or improperly treated fractures can lead to bony deformities, arthritis, chronic pain, and nonunion, where the bone fails to heal back together. This is why any suspected fracture should be evaluated rather than left to heal on its own.

Do all toe and metatarsal fractures need surgery?

No. Many toe fractures heal with splinting, rest, and stiff-soled footwear, and many metatarsal fractures heal with immobilization or casting. Surgery is generally reserved for fractures that are significantly displaced or unstable.

How long does it take for a toe or metatarsal fracture to heal?

Healing time varies by bone and severity. Toe fractures often heal over several weeks with protective footwear, while metatarsal fractures — especially those requiring surgery — can take longer and involve a more gradual return to activity.

When should I see a podiatrist for a foot injury?

See a podiatrist any time you suspect a broken toe or foot bone, especially with visible deformity, significant swelling or bruising, or pain that does not improve within a few days. You can request an appointment at our Farmington or South Ogden office.
